# Who moves an enterprise deal, and how to tell

An account record is full of titles, and titles mislead. The person who decides a purchase is rarely the one with the grandest job name; it is whoever keeps the evaluation alive, plus whoever holds the budget line above the deal's size. So before I rank anyone I go through the record one person at a time and set down what each has done; the table, the gaps and the plan are drawn from that reading, where the seller can check every call. Nothing enters the map that the record does not hold: no name for a person known only by role, no reporting line, no meeting, count or date.

## Evidence that counts

Strongest: they arranged a meeting with someone else, built something internal for us (a business case, a briefing, a budget request), shared data we did not ask for, took our material to their own leadership. Middle: they ask detailed questions, send follow-ups, answer quickly, confirm requirements. Weakest: they attended, were copied, said something positive once. One warm conversation followed by silence is the weakest signal, even from a chief officer.

Six readings a newcomer misses:

- **A stated spending line decides the buyer.** If a contact names the largest amount they can sign and the deal is larger, the economic buyer is whoever they say signs above it, met or not, and the champion is the route to that person. A limit above the deal size makes the contact buyer and champion in one; nobody higher is needed.
- **With no stated line, the buyer is unconfirmed and finance is the first place to look.** Do not crown the grandest title. When the champion says a finance head's team must be comfortable with the budget, that finance head is the likely economic buyer: call the role "Economic buyer, unconfirmed", make the missing line to them the critical gap, and let the plan's first ask settle who signs, the finance head or someone above. "Leadership will want a final briefing" is a last look by people still to be named.
- **An executive who endorsed and delegated is the sponsor, at arm's length.** One who said the project fits and told a colleague to keep driving it is the executive sponsor on their side, working through that colleague; her silence afterwards is what delegation looks like. Our own line to her is thin, scored on what came back, and it runs through him. An executive who was warm once, handed it to nobody and went quiet is a lapsed sponsor: her silence is a warning, not delegation, and she is won back through whoever on our side or theirs set up that meeting.
- **A finished review stays finished.** If a reviewer wrote that their requirement is met, that gate is closed even if an older stage label still calls it pending. On a small purchase a narrow check its owner called fine is finished too.
- **Momentum is evidence.** A promise with no act after it, a business case that has sat with an approver for weeks, "things are moving slowly on her end": say the deal is stalling, and at whose desk.
- **On a large deal the loose facts matter.** A rival also in the evaluation or a stated deadline is what delay costs. Material we sent for someone to pass upward, with no word that it arrived, is something to check with whoever asked. Colleagues who were forwarded our material are names to ask for. One of our own executives who arranged or sat in a meeting with a senior person there is the warm route back to that person.

## Relationship strength, 0 to 5

Score the relationship with us as it stands now, with a direction (rising, steady, stalling, quiet, done). **5**: drives the evaluation, answers fast, and has introduced us to colleagues or built internal material for us. **4**: the same energy, but a promise to take it upward has produced nothing yet: stalling. **3**: solid inside their own lane, such as a reviewer who answers and follows up. **2**: thin: one good meeting, a procedural exchange, a narrow check passed, or warmth gone quiet (one answer to several emails). **1**: copied, present but silent, our messages unanswered. **0**: never contacted. Quote the counts the record gives (sent against replies); invent none, and never work out how many days or months ago something was.

## What is missing depends on the deal

Gaps are what this deal still has to pass, judged from its size, stage and authority lines; they are not a roster check. A small team purchase inside its buyer's own limit, with no procurement or legal step mentioned, has no real gaps: say coverage fits the deal, and do not go looking for finance, legal, security or executive contacts. On a large deal name what the record leaves open (who signs, finance, procurement, security, legal) as questions for the champion, never as invented contacts. A role mentioned without a name and unnamed people at a meeting are gaps and get no row; a person the record names gets a row even at 0. If every line to the people who decide runs through one person, the deal is single-threaded on that person: say so with that word, as the first gap.

## Order of moves

The first move is whatever unblocks the deal soonest. Look in this order: what we owe (an unanswered email of theirs, a document a reviewer asked for); then access to whoever signs, through the person who holds the relationship, with something to carry (a one-page answer, a short session they host, a date this month); then names and roles for the unnamed; then the gates still to come. Only when the record says a message of theirs has had no reply from us is the silence ours, and answering it is then move one; a last-activity date does not say who wrote last. When the last word is ours (a form already returned) nothing is owed and the wait is theirs. Never write directly to a buyer or executive who is silent or has never met us; a reviewer who stopped answering is approached through the active colleague closest to them, not with another email. A procurement, security or technical contact who has told us what they need is answered by us directly. A small purchase gets two or three moves around its next booked event.

## Sponsor

Say who the executive sponsor is on their side, on what signal, and how direct our line is. An endorsing executive who delegated is the sponsor at arm's length, reached through the colleague she chose. Where the person who must approve has never met us, say there is no sponsor yet, who it has to be and which move would earn it; never promote the champion to fill the space. If one of our own executives has already met someone senior there, name him as the counterpart to bring in. On a small deal the buyer is the sponsor and nobody above is needed.

## Requests this skill does not answer

A request for a reporting-line chart, a ranking or a forecast is not a map request, and names with titles are not a record when the notes are only mentioned (mentioned is not pasted). Build no table, role, score or plan. The reply keeps the same title start, "# Stakeholder Map — <Account> | not built: <what was asked> needs data this record does not hold", and is a note of 200 to 280 words in four short parts with bold lead-ins: what was asked and what the seller holds, naming the contacts; why nothing is drawn (a title shows neither who reports to whom nor who decides, a guess on a leadership slide is read as fact, and the real chart has to come from the customer: tell the seller to ask a contact there for it); what their material does support, a stakeholder map, with what it would show at this deal's size and stage; and a short list of exactly what to paste.

## Writing the map

First read references/field-notes.md, on its own, once. Then send the map as your whole reply; its first characters are always "# Stakeholder Map — ", then the account, deal value and stage. Nothing comes before that title: no remark about the skill or the notes, no rule line. Write to the seller as a colleague ("you", "your champion", "I would").

- `## Reading the record`: two or three sentences sizing the deal (amount, stage, what a deal of this shape has to pass, any rival or deadline), then one short paragraph per named person: what the notes and counts show them doing, with dates and their own words, then the role and strength that earns; say outright where title and behaviour part ways, and give each email or silence to the person the record gives it to. Then "Mentioned, never met:", "Waiting on us:" (only what the record shows as owed: a message of theirs it says we have not answered, a document they asked for that it does not show us sending; a remark on a call is not an unanswered message, and if nothing is owed write "nothing") and, where it applies, the sentence that the deal is single-threaded.
- `## Stakeholder table`: Name | Title | Role in this deal | Strength and direction | Evidence, one row per named person, restating the reading.
- `## Coverage gaps`: a bullet each, the single thread first.
- `## Engagement plan`: numbered moves, each opening with a bold sentence that names the action and the person, then the signal behind it, what it unlocks and what it waits on.
- `## Executive sponsor`: one paragraph.
- A last paragraph, "**Why the map reads this way.**": three or four sentences on the calls that differ from the titles or the stage label, each with its signal.

A large deal runs 700 to 1,000 words; a small purchase stays under 400.
