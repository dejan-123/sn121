# A contact weighs what they do for this deal, not what their title says

Two habits ruin a stakeholder map: reading a title as influence, and reading a roster as coverage. A finance chief who has never replied is not a relationship, and a director who runs every meeting may be carrying the deal. So anchor the deal first, in the input's own words: its size and stage, who can approve it, what is still outstanding, and who holds our access. Then give every contact a tier and a strength with the signal behind each, find the gaps the deal's shape creates, put the move that unblocks the deal first, and name the sponsor the record supports.

## Tiers

The tier is the contact's weight over this deal, read from what they have done and what the input says they control. A title raises nobody without behaviour or a stated authority behind it, and a modest title lowers nobody who is visibly moving the deal.

**Decision-maker** is earned by one of two routes, named in the Evidence cell:
- **A stated authority that covers this deal:** a budget limit above the deal's size, or a signature the input says is theirs.
- **Someone's word that the deal needs their approval:** a contact saying spend at this level goes to that person, or that a committee they chair must pass it. The tier stands even when they have never met us; the strength then shows how far away they are.
Seniority alone takes neither route: a chief officer the input never ties to this decision is not the decision-maker, whatever the title.

**Champion:** acts for the deal without being asked: opens meetings, shares internal data, forwards our material, makes introductions, briefs leadership. Words of support with nothing done after them are sentiment, not championing.

**Gate:** owns a step the deal must pass, such as security, procurement, legal or compliance. The gate is open until its owner says in writing that it is met; a cleared gate needs upkeep, not a campaign.

**Specifier:** shapes requirements or tests fit without holding the step or the money: a technical lead, the head of the team that will use it.

**Peripheral:** present, with no stake or action on record. A contact who came once, said little and has not answered is peripheral whatever the title, unless they also own a gate.

A tier is earned one contact at a time: each takes the heaviest one their evidence supports, and a quiet gate owner stays a gate. When nobody we have met holds the authority, say so, name who came closest and why they stayed down, and carry the approver as a gap.

## Anchor detail

Size and stage set the next step the deal must clear; a stated authority limit is set against the deal's size in its bullet. Gaps come from this shape, never from a checklist of standard personas. A deal larger than the champion can sign leaves its approver as a gap while that approver is unmet or cold. A person the input mentions but does not give us, such as a director the champion says must see the numbers, is a gap whose route is whoever mentioned them; they take no table row and no invented name. When every route into the account runs through one person, the deal is single-threaded: name that person in the first gap. A small or early deal whose buyer holds the authority and whose gates are clear may have no real gap; say so, and add no procurement, legal or executive roles the input does not call for.

## Whose signal, and its strength

Every row takes one strength, scored 1 to 5 with its word: **5 Strong**, answers readily and acts unprompted; **4 Working**, answers and engages when asked; **3 Thin**, brief or occasional replies to mostly our outreach; **2 Cold**, outreach unanswered, or silence after an early contact; **1 None**, never engaged. Many messages sent and few returned is thin or cold, however senior the contact or warm the one call. Add the direction, warming, steady or cooling, by setting the latest activity against the earlier record: a strong start followed by silence is cooling.

A signal belongs to whoever produced it. What a contact says or does to us directly is theirs; support reported by someone else, an executive described as on board or a reviewer said to be satisfied, is the reporter's evidence until the person says it themselves. Count both directions, how often we wrote and how often they answered, with recency and who started each exchange; one unprompted action (data shared, a forward, an introduction) outweighs any number of polite replies, and a contact who only answers when chased is working at best.

## Reading across the contacts

Read the contacts together before fixing any tier, and say what each does to the others: an introduction credits the person who made it, a relayed endorsement waits on its source, and a gate owner's written word outranks anyone's summary of it. Where signals pull apart (warm words with no action; quick replies with nothing done), say which you weight and why, and mark a call resting on thin evidence as provisional. One person keeps one row even when two sources describe them, and two people met together keep two rows. A competitor in the evaluation is context for urgency, not a contact to map. Quote counts and dates exactly as the input gives them (two replies to nine emails, the last a month ago). Never invent a name, a meeting, a reply or a figure.

## Owners and order

Every action names our owner, the contact or gap it serves, the signal behind it, the ask, its timing against the deal's next step, and what it depends on. Order them by what unblocks the deal, not by seniority or ease: the first move goes to the gap or gate on the critical path, and a gate that could stall the step runs in parallel. A dormant or unmet approver is reached through whoever has access to them, with a specific ask and a reason to meet, never by cold outreach from our side. On a single-threaded deal the first action goes through the access holder, never around them: ask them to bring the next person in, with a reason that serves their own case. A champion whose own limit sits below the deal's size is asked to sponsor the approver's meeting, not to approve. "Send a follow-up" is not an action, and no action carries an odds-of-signing figure.

The sponsor paragraph recommends, by name, the customer executive whose authority covers the deal and whose record shows support, paired with an executive on our side. When nobody qualifies, because the one with authority is cold, unmet or only talked about, it says so plainly, names who comes closest and what must happen first. An executive who endorsed once and then handed the work to someone else is a candidate, not yet a sponsor, and the paragraph names the meeting that would turn them into one. A champion is the sponsor only when their own authority covers the deal; otherwise they are the route to the sponsor, and the paragraph says so.

A request the input cannot carry (reporting lines with no reporting data, odds of signing, notes or activity it mentions but does not include) is not built from titles: a short reply says what is missing, what the map will show once it arrives, and exactly what to paste.

## The reply

Before writing, call read_reference on `references/worked-cases.md`: invented accounts mapped in full, with and without real gaps, and the reason for each call. Take the form, never the facts.

The reply's first characters are `## `, the account's name, a colon, then "stakeholder map", the deal size and the stage; nothing comes before that heading. Then **Deal read**, up to five bullets: size and stage with the next step; the approvals needed and who can give them; open steps; where our access sits. Then the table `Contact | Title | Tier | Strength | Evidence`, one row per known contact in the input's order: the Tier cell gives its reason in a few words, the Strength cell the score, word and direction, the Evidence cell the counts, dates and actions behind both. Then **Count**, each tier with its contacts, every contact in exactly one tier. Then **Coverage gaps**, each with why this deal needs it and the route to close it, single-threading first when present; **Engagement plan**, numbered, the most unblocking move first; **Executive sponsor**, two to four sentences; and **Signal that would change the map**, one sentence naming the missing record that would most move a tier. Keep a full map between about 3,500 and 6,500 characters; a request the input cannot carry gets about 150 to 250 words.

After the reference is read, the reply starts straight at its heading, with no notes, plan or mention of this skill before it. Begin the reply with `## ` and the account's name, with nothing before it.
