# Account evidence brief

A seller needs to know who can approve, who advocates, and whom we can reach. Explain each from the record's evidence before presenting the map.

Read `references/account-briefs.md` first: its contrasting examples supply form, never facts. Reply with the deliverable alone.

## Establish what is known

Names, titles, amounts, dates, quotations and counts come only from the user's record. Examples supply no events. Distinguish proposed actions from things that happened.

For each contact, cite their acts, latest exchange, stated authority and open requests. Explain the role and relationship those support in one or two sentences. A title alone proves neither.

Copy the record's labeled figures exactly — "6 sent, 1 reply" stays 6 sent and 1 reply, in that direction. With no counts, use recorded acts and acknowledge the limitation. Use stated elapsed times or dates; calculate elapsed time only from a reference date supplied in the input. Never invent a deadline, quarter, or date the record does not give.

Attribute reported information: the director's account of an owner's review is not our conversation with the owner. Distinguish never contacted, copied only, and formerly engaged but now dormant.

## Explain the people before assigning labels

Describe roles supported by the record:

- **Buyer:** the person or body with the final approval for this purchase. Compare a stated spending ceiling with the amount. A senior title alone is not authority: "her team must be comfortable with the budget" makes her a gate to pass, not a proven signer. If no final authority is established, say the buyer is not yet identified. Accepting a cost allocation or clearing a technical condition is not necessarily purchase authority.
- **Champion:** advances the purchase through observable work — convening, sharing internals, building the case, securing access. Enthusiasm without delivery is untested support.
- **Evaluator:** checks suitability; describe their remit and the check's state.
- **Process owner:** administers a required step (purchasing, contract review); responsiveness is not advocacy.
- **Executive supporter:** has expressed or demonstrated backing — say whether it is current, historical or reported through another person.

A person can hold several roles. Delegation is not rejection: an executive who delegates yet keeps securing resources can still sponsor; one who delegated and stopped engaging is a candidate to re-engage through the delegate.

Requesting a proposal, demo or business case is not endorsement. An executive never introduced stays unreached even after receiving material through the champion. Invent no name, approval limit, meeting or attitude.

## Assess the relationship separately

Use a 1-5 strength score, a direction and supporting evidence: **5** sustained advocacy and reliable exchange; **4** engaged but reach untested; **3** dependable working exchange in a limited remit; **2** brief, transactional, or earlier warmth gone weak; **1** almost no reciprocal engagement.

Never contacted means **unscored** — write "unscored", not a number, for someone with no exchange. An earlier endorsement does not raise a score: a senior contact whose recent record is one acknowledgment to many messages and weeks of silence sits at 1 or 2, whatever they once said. A completed narrow check is complete and steady, not dormant — a reviewer with nothing left to do has not gone cold. Direction: improving, steady, stalling, cooling, dormant — or not started when no relationship exists. Improvement needs new evidence of progress, not just activity.

An unfulfilled promise means "untested" or "stalling" — name it. A slow approver does not weaken the responsive champion. High reply counts do not mean same-day replies.

A later direct confirmation can close an earlier pending review. A warm meeting followed by one-sided contact supports historic interest but weak access. For unresolved conflicts, give a provisional call and one question that would settle it. Do not hedge established facts.

## Separate completed work from open dependencies

Review each request as an exchange: requested, supplied, accepted, or still awaiting an answer. These states are not interchangeable.

Their unanswered message is our debt; ours unanswered is their weak access. A document already returned is not a document we still owe — it is awaiting their response. "Reviewing" or "under review" is an open state, not "unreviewed" and not done. Receipt of material does not close a review unless acceptance is recorded.

An explicit "satisfied for now" closes the stated narrow requirement unless a new condition follows. Do not reopen it, demand another confirmation or treat the reviewer's silence as disengagement. A new question can coexist with a completed review: answer that question without relabeling the whole review pending.

## Find gaps appropriate to this purchase

When one champion is the only route to people whose agreement is needed, call the account single-threaded and explain the exposure. Ask that champion to arrange access, including names of unidentified attendees. A small purchase its engaged buyer can authorize alone needs no enterprise roster, sponsor or extra introductions.

For a large purchase, uncertain approval or contract paths are questions, not established board meetings or legal requirements. Preserve "likely" where the input does. Missing titles do not automatically create tasks — a record that names no legal or finance contact creates no legal step — deal size alone never implies one.

People identified only by role and unnamed groups belong in an access-gap sentence, not invented table rows. Include every named contact, including those never engaged. The fact that someone is named in CRM does not make their relationship established.

## Turn the findings into an ordered plan

List only actions that move the deal — a satisfied check earns no step, not even a courtesy confirmation. Lead with the outstanding reply or prerequisite blocking progress. Include useful material with that reply and a concrete next conversation. Independent tracks — paperwork and access — run in parallel, not in series.

Each step names a route, prompting fact and result. Respect dependencies and the contact's stated concern; invent no agenda.

Use an existing relationship to reach an unreached or dormant executive: the champion, the delegate, or our executive who arranged the earlier meeting. The first ask goes to that intermediary — ask them to arrange the conversation, never for the executive's contact details to reach them directly. Do not send a direct pre-meeting note to an executive who has not agreed to meet. For an unresponsive reviewer, use an engaged colleague involved in the same work.

For a modest purchase, use the scheduled event and buyer's questions; do not demand an early signature. Mention a competitor only if supplied, with a concrete implication rather than generic urgency.

## Keep sponsorship and signing authority distinct

State whether there is an active customer sponsor, a candidate needing renewed engagement, or no established sponsor. A sponsor needs influence and demonstrated backing; signing authority alone is not advocacy. A supportive executive can help obtain a decision without personally holding the spending limit.

Say "no separate executive sponsor is needed" only when the champion is the authorized buyer. Otherwise do not relabel the working champion as the missing executive — say plainly whether the seat is filled by an active sponsor, held by a candidate needing renewed engagement, or empty. Identify the executive candidate only when evidence supports one: the person the record shows must approve, or one who has acted for the deal. A never-met approver is a person to reach and qualify, not an active supporter.

Our executive involvement needs a customer counterpart, purpose and route. Reuse a recorded executive connection. Otherwise explain what must happen before escalation.

## Deliver a readable account brief

Use ordinary sales language: 500-800 words for complex records, 200-350 for small purchases, less for thin evidence. Avoid stock openings.

1. `# <Account> — <main finding>`; omit unknown deal details.
2. `## Account read`: size and stage where given, then a short evidence-backed assessment of each named contact. End with unreached roles and outstanding obligations. Explain the few surprising calls.
3. `## Relationship map`: Contact | Deal role | Strength and direction | Basis. The table must agree with the assessment. Short cells; named people only.
4. `## Coverage and next steps`: material gaps, then ordered actions with routes and dependencies. Say when coverage fits.
5. `## Sponsorship`: current backing, candidate and route, and whether our executive involvement is justified.

Before sending, check: no invented person, figure, request or approval layer; returned work is not owed; a closed check is not a task; the sponsor paragraph agrees with the evidence and table.

## When the requested document cannot be supported

For a reporting-line diagram, forecast or other different deliverable, do not substitute a map unasked. Give a short redirect — under 170 words, headed `# <Account> — <requested document>`: the missing input, its authoritative source, and the alternative (influence, relationships, gaps, engagement order). Do not rank names, nominate a hierarchy source, or infer reporting lines. A sparse stakeholder-map request still gets a limited map with explicit unknowns.
