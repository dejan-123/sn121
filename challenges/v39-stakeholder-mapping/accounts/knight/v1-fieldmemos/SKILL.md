# Stakeholder mapping from behaviour

A job title says which chair a person sits in. It does not say whether that person moves your deal. The record does: who started the conversation, who spent their own capital on you, who answered, and who went quiet. This skill reads the record and writes the memo a sharp colleague would hand the seller — the honest version, not the org chart version.

## How to read the record

Behaviour beats title. Real pull shows in acts that cost something: initiating contact, running the agenda, sharing internal material unasked, forwarding your work upward, making introductions, writing the internal business case. Attending, nodding, and "looks promising" are politeness, not pull.

Authority is a threshold, not a rank. When the record states a person's spending limit, hold it against the deal value. A deal above someone's line has a signer they cannot be — and the memo names whoever signs and whether you have any line to them at all.

Hand-offs point forward, not backward. An executive who told a delegate to run it has moved the working relationship to that delegate. A leader who endorsed and then handed the effort down is a sponsor candidate, not a sponsor — her delegate is the route back to her, and the map shows the candidate plainly: she endorsed, she delegated, she stepped back.

Interest is not endorsement. A signer who asked for a business case asked for a document — the request carries no backing with it. An executive you have never met stays never met even when one arrives through the champion: a signature or a request is history, not a relationship. Judge only what their own acts on record show.

Champions prove themselves or stall. A supporter whose warmth produced an introduction or a decision is tested. One whose promise produced nothing is stalling — call it that, however good the meeting felt.

Closed means closed. A security, legal, or compliance reviewer who confirmed completion in writing is a finished gate, whatever an earlier stage label says. A narrow check on a small deal that the checker called satisfied earns at most one light courtesy — never a demand for sign-off.

Whose message sits last. Check who wrote most recently before blaming silence. Their unanswered message is ours to answer — that debt opens the plan. Ours means the quiet is mutual.

## Strength and direction

Rate every named contact 0–5 from their acts, counts, and dates — not from warmth felt in a meeting:

- 5: initiates, answers same day, has already spent capital for us (introduction made, internal document written).
- 4: engaged and responsive today but untested — support has not yet produced an introduction or a decision; a supporter whose promise led nowhere sits here, stalling.
- 3: a working relationship inside their own scope.
- 2: thin — one good meeting, a form, a brief acknowledgment, a satisfied narrow check, or earlier warmth gone quiet.
- 1: cold — copied, attended without engaging, leaves our mail unanswered.
- 0: never engaged.

Strength is the relationship today, and the evidence settles it: six sent, one short answer back is weak whatever the meeting felt like; eleven sent, nine back is strong. Trajectory is rising, stable, cooling, or dormant from their last act's date — never our last send.

Never invent numbers. A fabricated "8/10 replied" is worse than no number: when the record gives no counts for someone, cite what they did and when, exactly as the record states them. The input states no today, so write the date itself — never "N days ago" or "in the N days since."

## When signals pull apart

Records are rarely uniform. Settle conflicts in a fixed order:

- Recency outweighs volume: twenty warm replies last quarter lose to six quiet weeks now.
- Initiation outweighs reply-count: a contact who answers everything but starts nothing is a friend, not a driver.
- An owned act outweighs reported warmth: "she told the board she wants it" is a claim until the record shows the act.
- A written close outweighs seniority: a review closed in the reviewer's own words is closed at any title.
- Authority outweighs the org chart: a stated ceiling on a bigger deal is the gap wherever the chart puts her.

When two calls still both fit, say which the record cannot settle and the one question that would — an owned ambiguity beats a confident wrong call.

## Gaps belong to the deal's shape

Ask what this size and stage needs to close with the people already involved — not what a textbook roster lists. A small purchase inside the buyer's own stated authority, with no procurement or legal step raised, needs no economic-buyer hunt, no legal contact, no second thread, and no executive above the buyer: say so in a sentence and move on. A large deal above the champion's ceiling needs the person who signs. People named by role but never introduced — "my head of engineering," an unnamed CEO — are gaps to fill through whoever mentioned them; they get no name and no table row. When one person carries every live line on a deal that needs more than that person, call it single-threaded, plainly, and put it first among the gaps. A small deal inside one buyer's authority is naturally single-threaded — that is normal, not a gap.

## Ordering the moves

1. Anything we owe the buyer that they made a precondition — and any message of theirs still unanswered.
2. The blocker a contact or the decision-maker named in her own words.
3. The introduction to whoever signs, brokered by whoever has access.
4. Widening the thread and whatever a nearly closed gate still needs.

A reply we owe should carry something the deal needs — the material that answers the decision-maker's open questions, an offer the champion can host — never a bare request for an update. Do not spend the first move confirming authority the input never questioned; go to the named blocker. A silent executive or dormant signer is reached through the champion with a reason tied to the executive's own question — going around the champion can cost the champion. A silent gatekeeper is reached through the engaged colleague who works beside them, not by a third unanswered email. Every move names the contact, the signal that earns it, and what it waits on.

## The executive sponsor

The sponsor is the person whose authority covers the deal and who has done something for it — an endorsement, an act, an open door. On a small deal inside the buyer's own authority, the buyer is the sponsor: say so plainly and close — do not hunt above them, and do not call the arrangement a candidate.

A candidate is the executive the deal still needs: someone with signature authority who has acted for us or whom a trusted contact can reach — the delegated executive reached through her delegate, the never-met signer reached through the champion. The champion doing the work is never the candidate and never the sponsor problem; when no executive has acted, say the sponsor seat sits empty and the champion carries the deal until the route lands. The route to any candidate runs through someone who already trusts us — never cold.

When the record supports it, say whether one of our own executives should be the one to walk in.

## When the request is not a stakeholder map

Some asks want a different artefact: an org chart of reporting lines, a draft email, a forecast. Others name people but carry no activity to read. Do not build a map or guess a hierarchy from titles. Write a short redirect — under 170 words, first line bold "**Redirect — …**" with nothing before it — saying what was asked, why this input cannot produce it, where that artefact comes from, and what to paste for a stakeholder map. When the request only overshoots the record, answer what the record supports: categories, not names — never invent the reporting lines or the people.

## Writing the memo

Open references/field-memos.md once, on its own, before writing — it holds worked examples of the form. Read the account silently; the reply is the memo alone, and its first characters are "**Stakeholder Map —" (or "**Redirect —"). Nothing comes before it.

Write the way a senior colleague would brief the seller, second person — "your champion," "you have no sponsor today" — not as a form. The first line is the bold title "**Stakeholder Map — <Account> | <deal value>, <stage>**". After the title, no bold section labels. In order:

- An opening paragraph: who actually moves the deal, what blocks it, the fact that changes the obvious reading, and what waiting costs. Its last sentence leads to the table.
- The table: Contact | Title | Influence tier | Strength (0–5) | Trajectory | Evidence — a row for each person the input names; roles known only by title get no row. Evidence cites counts, dates, or their words.
- One sentence noting which roles were mentioned without names.
- A paragraph on what the coverage lacks for a deal this size and stage — plain sentences, the single-threaded call first when it applies.
- The plan: one line on why the order matters, then numbered steps as
 each naming the contact, the signal behind it, and what it waits on.
- A closing paragraph on the executive sponsor — who, why, and the route if there is none yet.

Stop after the sponsor paragraph — no trailing notes, counts, or sign-off lines.
