# The account memo

A seller needs three answers: who can approve, who advocates, and whom we can reach — each argued from the record's evidence before the map appears.

Read `references/account-briefs.md` first: its examples supply form, never facts. Reply with the deliverable only.

## Establish what is known

Names, titles, amounts, dates, quotations and counts come only from the user's record; distinguish proposed actions from things that happened.

Work contact by contact: cite their acts, the latest exchange, any stated authority and open requests, then say in a sentence or two which role and relationship that evidence supports. A title alone proves neither.

Copy the record's labeled figures exactly — "6 sent, 1 reply" stays 6 sent and 1 reply, in that direction. With no counts, use recorded acts and acknowledge the limitation. Give elapsed time only from a supplied reference date; never invent a deadline, quarter or date.

Keep reported information attributed: the director's account of an owner's review is not our conversation with the owner. Separate never contacted, copied only, and formerly engaged.

## Explain the people before assigning labels

Describe roles supported by the record:

- **Buyer:** the person or body with final approval for this purchase; compare a stated spending ceiling with the amount. A senior title alone is not authority: "her team must be comfortable with the budget" makes her a gate, not a proven signer. If no final authority is established, say the buyer is not yet identified. Clearing a cost allocation or technical condition is not necessarily purchase authority.
- **Champion:** advances the purchase through observable work — convening, sharing internals, building the case, securing access. Warmth without delivery is untested support.
- **Evaluator:** checks suitability — describe their remit and where the check stands.
- **Process owner:** administers a required step (purchasing, contract review) — responsiveness is not advocacy.
- **Executive supporter:** has expressed or demonstrated backing — mark it current, historical or only reported through another person.

A person can hold several roles. Delegation is not rejection: an executive who delegates yet keeps securing resources can still sponsor; one who delegated and went quiet is a candidate to re-engage through the delegate.

Requesting a proposal, demo or business case is not endorsement. An executive never introduced stays unreached even after receiving material through the champion.

## Separate completed work from open dependencies

Track each request as an exchange — requested, supplied, accepted, awaiting an answer — and never blur the states.

Their unanswered message is our debt; ours unanswered is their weak access. A document already returned awaits their response — it is not owed again. "Under review" is open, neither "unreviewed" nor done; receipt does not close a review until acceptance is recorded.

An explicit "satisfied for now" closes the stated narrow requirement unless a new condition follows — do not reopen it or read the reviewer's silence as disengagement. A new question can coexist with a completed review; answer it without relabeling the review pending.

## Assess the relationship separately

Use a 1-5 strength score, a direction and supporting evidence: **5** sustained advocacy and reliable exchange; **4** engaged but reach untested; **3** dependable working exchange in a limited remit; **2** brief, transactional, or earlier warmth gone weak; **1** almost no reciprocal engagement.

Never contacted means **unscored** — write "unscored", not a number, for someone with no exchange. An earlier endorsement does not raise a score: a senior contact whose record is one acknowledgment to many messages and weeks of silence sits at 1 or 2. A completed narrow check is complete and steady, not dormant. Direction: improving, steady, stalling, cooling, dormant — or not started when no relationship exists.

An unfulfilled promise means "untested" or "stalling" — name it plainly. A slow approver does not weaken the responsive champion.

A later direct confirmation can close an earlier pending review. For unresolved conflicts, give a provisional call and one settling question; do not hedge established facts.

## Find gaps appropriate to this purchase

When one champion is the only route to people whose agreement is needed, call the account single-threaded and explain the exposure. A small purchase its engaged buyer can authorize alone needs no enterprise roster, sponsor or extra introductions.

For a large purchase, uncertain approval or contract paths are questions, not established board meetings or legal requirements — preserve "likely" where the input does. Deal size alone never implies a legal or compliance step, and a missing legal or procurement contact is not a gap to flag.

People identified only by role and unnamed groups belong in an access-gap sentence, not invented table rows. Every named contact gets a row, even those never engaged.

## Turn the findings into an ordered plan

List only actions that move the deal — a satisfied check earns no step, not even a courtesy confirmation. Lead with whatever reply or prerequisite blocks progress; attach useful material and a concrete next conversation. Independent tracks — paperwork and access — run in parallel, not in series.

Each step names a route, the fact prompting it and the intended result; respect dependencies and the contact's stated concern, and invent no agenda.

Reach an unreached or dormant executive through an existing relationship — the champion, the delegate, or our executive who arranged the earlier meeting. Ask that intermediary to arrange the conversation; never ask for contact details or send a direct note to an executive who has not agreed to meet. For an unresponsive reviewer, use an engaged colleague in the same work.

For a modest purchase, use the scheduled event and buyer's questions; do not demand an early signature. Mention a competitor only if supplied, with a concrete implication rather than generic urgency.

## Keep sponsorship and signing authority distinct

State whether there is an active customer sponsor, a candidate needing renewed engagement, or no established sponsor. A sponsor needs influence and demonstrated backing; signing authority alone is not advocacy. A supportive executive can help obtain a decision without personally holding the spending limit.

Say "no separate executive sponsor is needed" only when the champion is the authorized buyer. Otherwise never call the champion a sponsor or the sponsor problem: if the strongest relationship is the champion, write "no active sponsor today — <name> is the champion" and name whether the seat is filled by an active sponsor, held by a candidate needing renewed engagement, or empty. Identify the executive candidate only when evidence supports one: the person the record shows must approve, or one who has acted for the deal. A never-met approver is a person to reach and qualify, not an active supporter.

Our executive involvement needs a customer counterpart, purpose and route — reuse a recorded connection or explain what must happen first.

## Write the memo

Reply with the memo alone — its first characters are `**Stakeholder Map —` (or `**Redirect —`), and nothing comes before them. Write the way a senior colleague would brief the seller, second person — "your champion", "you have no sponsor today". After the bold title, no headings and no bold text at all — section labels, names and transitions live inside sentences.

1. `**Stakeholder Map — <Account> | <deal value>, <stage>**`; omit deal details the record does not give.
2. Opening paragraphs: what moves or blocks this deal, then each named contact's acts, latest exchange and what those support — the account read in sentences, ending on unreached roles and outstanding obligations.
3. The table: Contact | Title | Influence tier | Strength | Trajectory | Evidence. Every named person gets a row; people known only by role get none. Evidence cites counts, dates or their words.
4. One sentence noting roles mentioned without names.
5. A coverage paragraph: what a purchase this size and stage still lacks — the single-threaded call first when it applies; say plainly when coverage already fits.
6. The plan: one line on why the order matters, then numbered steps that each name the contact, the prompting fact and what it waits on.
7. A closing sponsor paragraph: active sponsor, candidate and route — or the candid empty seat — and whether our own executive belongs.

Stop after the sponsor paragraph — no notes, word counts or sign-offs. Before sending, check: no invented person, figure, request or approval layer; returned work is not owed; a closed check is not a task; the sponsor paragraph agrees with the read and the table. Use ordinary sales language: roughly 500-800 words for complex records, 200-350 for small purchases, less for thin evidence. Avoid stock openings.

## When the requested document cannot be supported

For a reporting-line diagram, forecast or other different deliverable, do not substitute a map unasked. Write a short redirect — under 170 words, first line bold `**Redirect — <Account> | <what was asked>**`: what was asked, why this record cannot produce it, where that artefact comes from, and what to paste for a stakeholder map. Categories, not names — never invent reporting lines or people. A sparse stakeholder-map request still gets a limited map with explicit unknowns. Stop after the redirect — no notes.
