# Brief before the watch

A manager stands a weekly watch with each report: thirty
minutes, one chart. This skill writes the brief the manager
reads before stepping in — what the week's log actually shows,
which courses only they can set, which matters belong to a
different officer entirely. The manager's draft list is a
proposed heading; the log decides where the meeting actually
steers.

Call `read_reference` on `references/log-drills.md` before
composing. Three logs are worked there end to end, including
one correctly returned unread as current. Learn the drills;
plot fresh headings each time.

## Logbook or live chart?

A brief needs the current watch's entries: this week's
messages, action states, figures, live topics. A logbook from
a watch already stood — minutes, notes, transcripts — records
what was ordered then, not what happened since. Hand it back:
identify it as a past watch's log, restate each recorded order
with its owner and date, say current status is unknown, and
request this week's entries and fresh topics. An old due date
isn't proof of a live gap; never mark it overdue on the new
chart. Personal entries in an old log stay private. New entries
arriving beside an old log are usable — chart from the new,
keep the old as context. Business bound for a skip-level or a
formal rating wants its own officer; name the right one and
stop.

## Plot each heading against the chart

Inventory the manager's proposed items, the report's own, and
open orders both directions. Behind every item, find the entry
that grounds it — a message, a ticket, a figure, the report's
own words. A characterization without an entry is weather
guesswork: keep the item, ask the question, make no claim.
Merge items sharing one cause but keep both visible; add items
only when an entry demands them.

Set the course to the hand on the helm. Work stalled behind
another crew's input or the manager's own unfinished order is
the manager's course to set — not a mark against the report —
and it steers early because the fleet waits. One officer
reviewing every chart for several crews is a crewing decision:
filed "quick" or "low" while four crews hold position, it
takes the lead and the reordering is said plainly. A report
who already drafted a rotation gets asked what it takes to run
it. Read the full signal log on manner: someone who sounded a
justified alarm on the open deck while keeping a courteous
private line deserves the unblock *and* one precise question
about whether the open deck was the right place — either half
alone misreads the watch.

Match the moment's true size:

- A named save, shipped result, or specific thanks opens the
  brief. A wish for "something positive" with no entry opens
  nothing.
- Work under way gets the question that moves it; work done
  and already credited doesn't get logged twice.
- One rough moment, already corrected, gets a single
  what-next-time question — not a standing order about
  character.
- A repeated pattern or openly developing skill gets a
  question anchored to the entries; when the report named the
  cause, design the early-warning drill together.
- A bearing the report stated gets unhurried time even on a
  quiet week — ask their direction, assign none, and promise
  no rank or rate from one conversation.

Pay reviews, family and health matters, formal grievances, and
fleet-wide policy belong to other officers — one line naming
the proper room and owner, nothing scripted. Career bearings
stay on this chart; pay rulings do not. A thin log makes a
short brief: an honest "where do we stand?" is good seamanship;
an invented position is not.

## Compute before you chart

Work every figure the log offers — both readings and the
difference: `7 of 9 sites (was 5)`, `58 to 71 = +13 points`,
`three holds, one shared cause`. Date gaps compute only against
a supplied meeting date. A missing figure stays missing —
flagged, never guessed.

## Write the brief

Name the report and role, then a short read of the watch —
what moved, what holds, the one decision not to leave unset —
figures shown worked. Then the items in order: plain everyday
labels, the grounding entry, and a line the manager can
deliver — three sentences at most. Earned credit opens;
manager-held unblocks lead; the report's own bearing keeps
its time. Close with the manager's own orders and one
elsewhere-note only when needed. No ship's jargon for its own
sake, no audit table, no padding.

Final pass: every proposed item is charted, merged with a
named sibling, or referred with a reason; nothing private is
scripted; nothing is invented.
