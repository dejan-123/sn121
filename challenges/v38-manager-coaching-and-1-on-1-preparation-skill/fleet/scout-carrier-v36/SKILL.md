# Prepare the conversation, not a verdict

Read the week before trusting the draft. A label is a proposed use of meeting time, not proof that the evidence supports it. Show the manager what each proposed item became and why, then write a page they can use in the room. Nothing in the example file is evidence about a real report: copy its decision-making and its shape, not its people, numbers, sentences or dates. Start with `## Read of the week` for a forthcoming one-on-one; a record of a meeting already held takes the separate note described below.

## Check what arrived

A draft for an upcoming meeting is preparation even when it is the only input. A sentence saying "no signals, no log, no figures" describes a thin draft, NOT a past meeting: always produce the full page, not a refusal or a note asking for more input. Say what was not supplied, keep the live questions it can support and ask for the records that would settle them. The past-meeting redirect is allowed only when the supplied material actually records a meeting already held (minutes, transcript, attendees and agreed actions). Those are not this week's evidence, even when the request asks for next week's prep. Answer with the short follow-up note, not a fabricated future brief. A skip-level, a review rating or a meeting about someone else is likewise not this report's weekly one-on-one.

For a genuine prep packet, walk the draft in its own order. Each numbered entry in `## Read of the week` quotes the proposed topic, names the dated row, message, figure or report-owned request that tests it, and says in ordinary language whether the item stays, changes, merges, leaves the meeting or lacks support. Two or three sentences are enough. After the entries, `Not in the draft:` catches open manager-owned rows and any report request or outside-party process the manager missed. `Numbers:` shows calculations with their operands and the log's closed/open state.

## Make the meeting calls

- **Recognition** earns the first slot when a named person credits a particular contribution or a measured result establishes it. Give the manager the actual contribution and its effect, not generic applause. Without a source for praise, do not invent an opener; say why the draft's praise cannot yet stand.
- **Unblock** is work waiting on an approval, partner, another team or an unmanageable queue, even if its log row says IN PROGRESS. It belongs to the manager to move: name whom they will contact, what they will decide and what they will bring back. Support a remedy the report already suggested rather than presenting it as the manager's idea. A "status" title does not turn a dependency into a report performance question. Messages sent while chasing that dependency are not automatically evidence of a tone problem.
- **Quick feedback** is one dated occurrence of the report's behaviour. Give the incident fairly, credit any repair they already made and ask one light question about the unresolved consequence. A first mistake remains one occurrence even if someone senior noticed it. Do not write a character judgment.
- **Coaching** needs the same behaviour on at least two separate dated occasions or a development skill the report explicitly chose. Ask two concrete questions, without a verdict or a second quick-feedback item on the same behaviour. If the report already identified why it happened, start with that explanation and ask what would change upstream.
- **Career** gives room to a path, training, role or scope the report raised. Put this week's actual work beside the aspiration; a level decision itself may belong elsewhere, but the report's own next step can remain a question here. A manager-proposed development check with no report topic stays a modest open question rather than a fictional ambition.
- **Status** fits a live piece of work whose current position is genuinely unknown. Ask about its specific next decision or date. Finished work is not status; attach it to earned praise if there is praise, otherwise close it in the context.

Use one item for one underlying signal, even if the draft repeats it under two names. Zero dated instances behind "seems withdrawn" or "workload worry" are not a coaching pattern: remove the verdict, cite any signals that cut against it and say what observation would justify revisiting it. A blocker can explain a tense thread without proving the report has a behaviour problem. Decide borderline calls by the outcome the conversation must produce: a manager move, a single repair, an ongoing skill or a future path.

## Keep the other conversation elsewhere

Pay, health and family circumstances, accommodations, formal HR matters, decisions about promotion, third-party complaints and a process someone outside the one-on-one owns go to `## Elsewhere`. Give the venue, who will arrange it and a concrete next step, without writing the talking points or disclosing details beyond the input's own brief wording. When an outside process also blocks the report's work, the work can stay as an Unblock while the process discussion is booked separately. An unfinished action assigned to the manager is not a parked issue: it stays visible under `## Before you sit down`.

## Use the record precisely

Do not turn "I'll ask on Friday" into "asked on Friday": even after Friday, a conditional escalation is still a plan until its completion is recorded. Ask whether it occurred. Copy dates only from the particular source that supplies them. Do not infer an owner or deadline. An absent log is not an empty completed log. Count all rows including manager DONE rows; only unfinished manager rows are owed. Calculate a completion share only when the supplied log makes its denominator clear. For comparable amounts compute (current - baseline) / baseline, showing both values and the sign. Rates and percentages change in percentage points when subtracting them. If a source explains only part of a gap, leave the rest explicitly unresolved. Do not divide unrelated counts.

Before sending, audit each past-tense action against proof it happened: an escalation promised for today is still not a completed escalation. Then test every question: could it be asked unchanged of another employee? If yes, put this week's ticket, thread, figure or the report's words into it. Would it sound fair if the report read the printed page? If not, replace the insinuation with the observable event. Write questions as words the manager could speak to "you"; when the answer might expose a dependency, say briefly what the manager should listen for.

## The page the manager carries

After the reading, write a 350-650 word meeting page; count and trim before sending. Title it `# <report's name>: <the week in a short phrase drawn from the evidence>`. An opening paragraph brings together what moved, what waits on whom, the log's position and the one or two figures that actually matter. The page sounds like a prepared colleague, not a worksheet.

Under `## In the room`, number the agenda in useful conversational order: earned recognition first, then manager-owned unblocks, single feedback and repeated coaching, live status and career with protected room for the report's own question. Head each item `**N. Topic - Call**`. In at most three sentences give the signal and the manager's move; make coaching and career questions speakable, evidence-specific and in quotation marks. One occurrence gets at most one light question; a recurring behaviour gets two questions about the pattern. Do not spend a numbered item on a closed task or an item sent to another venue.

`## Elsewhere` holds routed subjects with venue and booking step, or one quiet "Nothing to route" line if none. `## Before you sit down` lists each open manager-owned log row with its recorded status and what it holds up: bring it, or give the report a real date. When no manager row is open, say so. For a thin draft, also name each record to fetch and its likely holder without pretending it has already arrived. Finish with `**Why this order.**` in a few natural sentences explaining the changed labels, dropped impressions and routed matters using their deciding signals. Do not add a second policy essay.

With a thin packet, including a draft with zero other inputs, the page still has numbered, speakable questions for every surviving topic. A proposed workload check is a fair Status question about the named work, not a diagnosis; a manager-proposed career check stays a modest Career question, never an invented report-owned aspiration. Explain what the question alone cannot establish, then what to listen for; no invented recognition, incident, pattern or figure. Keep the page within the length range through useful preparation, not repetition.

## If the meeting already happened

Return only a note of at most 180 words, beginning `# <report's name>: not preparation for a future one-on-one`. Say what identifies the supplied record as a past meeting and why replaying it as fresh prep would mislead. List its recorded actions with owners and deadlines exactly as given, manager first; their present completion is unknown unless the input says otherwise. Ask for the current state of those actions, this week's messages and figures, and a new draft to prepare the next meeting. Mention a sensitive matter only in the source's own minimal words and route it privately, with no talking points. No reading section or prospective agenda belongs in this note.

Read `references/three-weeks.md` once with read_reference before answering. It demonstrates a complete week, a draft that arrived almost alone, and the past-meeting note. All names and events there are invented; use only the supplied packet's facts for a real reply. Do not announce the method before the first heading.
