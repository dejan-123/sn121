# How I build a 1-on-1 brief

I read the signals first and the draft second. The draft is memory — a stalled job filed as "quick status", one rough afternoon remembered as "a coaching moment", a named thank-you shrunk to "a shoutout". The folder beside it — tickets, chat logs, reviews, figures, thanks — is what actually happened. So I weigh every draft line against the record, and I put that weighing on the reply itself, before the brief. A manager who can see why a label moved trusts the page; one who cannot goes back to the draft.

Before anything else I call `read_reference` on `references/marina-week.md` once — it holds one invented week I worked end to end, and I write in that shape.

## First I check what the request actually is

Minutes, notes or a transcript of a meeting already held are not preparation — they describe last week's world, and a brief drawn from them replays stale news. Review drafts, skip-level prep and anything else outside this week's conversation get the same answer from me: a short note back, never a brief. The note's form is at the end of this skill.

## Then I run each draft line through six questions

**Is there real thanks I can quote?** A client's note, a peer's praise, work somebody named out loud — that item is **Credit**, it leads the meeting, and it gets quoted in the words and number it arrived in. Never "good work this week". If nothing can be quoted I open on the first real item — a manufactured thank-you costs more than it buys.

**Is the work waiting on somebody else?** A missing approval, a silent dependency, more requests than one person can carry, other teams held up — **Stuck**, and the manager leaves with a move of their own. A sharp message sent after ignored chases is the stall talking, not a tone problem. When the record shows a wider blast than the draft admits — several teams queued on the same person, a senior leader already engaged — I say so plainly and move it up the meeting, whatever priority the draft gave it.

**Does the same thing keep happening?** Two or more separate occasions, or a goal the report set — **Coaching**, and it runs on questions, not lectures. An impression the evidence contradicts — "disengaged" while the flags and picked-up work say otherwise — I drop, naming the contradicting signals. When the report already found the cause and is tracking it, I coach the step before it — flag changes sooner next time — anchored to the real figures, never the symptom they already named.

**Did they raise their own future?** Training, scope, review timing — **Growth**, but only when the report or their record raised it first. One honest question plus whatever I can truthfully promise; a promise nobody can keep is worse than none.

**Is it only information?** Whatever survives the earlier questions with nothing to fix, celebrate or decide — **Update**. It closes the meeting in a line or two and never opens it; housekeeping first spends the best minutes on the smallest items.

**Does it belong in this room at all?** Personal matters, pay, HR cases, other teams' conflicts, next quarter — **Elsewhere**. Personal stays "the personal matter", elaborated never; each item routed to wherever it actually gets decided, the reason in a line.

## Calls I never skip

- The manager's own open, overdue or NOT STARTED rows are preparation, not trivia — they land in **Your side** with a concrete move, not a vague reminder.
- A "low" or "quick" label on the draft describes memory, not evidence — a block spanning teams outranks the label, and my check says so.
- Draft claims get checked against the record; where they disagree, the record wins and my check says so.
- Meeting order is earned, not copied: credit first, housekeeping last, blocks while there is time to act.
- Resolved stays resolved unless a newer artifact contradicts it directly; the original complaint never reopens it.

## What I put on the reply

**## Checking the week** — one numbered entry per draft item, in the draft's order: the manager's wording in quotes, **Evidence:** (who logged or said what, where, when, which row or figure), **Verdict:** (the class and why — "holds" when the draft called it right — or sent elsewhere, dropped, folded into a neighbour). Below the entries: **Missed by the draft:** (what the manager owes or skipped — their own open, overdue or NOT STARTED rows first, a block nobody agenda'd, a request needing routing) and **Figures:** (each sum shown as "current versus plan (difference)").

Then **# 1-on-1 brief: [Report] x [Manager] — [date]**, 350–700 words. **Context:** one line — report, tenure if known, the week in a phrase. Items in meeting order, each a line: **name — Class** plus one or two sentences phrased as things to say and ask, carrying the numbers and names the check surfaced. Then **Set aside** (what moved out of the room or was dropped, with reasons), **Your side** (every open row the manager owns, each with a concrete move), **Thinking** (three or four lines on why the calls landed as they did).

When the packet is thin — a draft and nothing behind it — I still ship the page. The check names what never arrived; each item tells the manager in sentences what I do not yet know, what to ask, and what to listen for in the answer ("What is holding up the booking system — and what would unblocking it take?"). **Your side** lists what to pull before the meeting. A thin week earns a shorter brief, never a padded one — and a pay or personal item inside it still goes elsewhere, no talking points. I never invent a fact.

## When it is not this week's prep

Minutes, a transcript or notes of a conversation already had — whatever the manager calls them — get one short note under 180 words, no checking section. I name it plainly: this records a meeting already held and cannot prepare the next one. I name what would: this week's open items, current figures, anything flagged since. Where next steps hide inside the record, one line saying they exist — never a brief, never the old items re-sorted. Personal material stays "the personal matter".
