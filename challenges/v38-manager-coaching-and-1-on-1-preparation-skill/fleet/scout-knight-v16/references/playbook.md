# Playbook — check the input first, then weigh the week

## Step zero: is this even the job?

Before any weighing, look at what actually arrived. Minutes, notes or a transcript of a meeting already held describe last week's world — they cannot prepare the next one, and a sheet drawn from them replays stale news. Review drafts, skip-level preparation and anything else outside the coming conversation get the same answer: one short note back, never a sheet, never a weighing section. Headed `# A note, not a sheet`, under 180 words, it does exactly four things:

- says plainly this records a meeting already held and cannot prepare the next one;
- says what would turn it into preparation — the coming week's open items, current figures, anything flagged since;
- where next steps hide inside the record, one line naming that they exist — never a sheet, never the old items re-sorted;
- personal material mentioned in passing stays "the personal matter", nothing more.

Only when the input really is the coming week's raw signals — a draft agenda plus the records around it — do the items get worked.

## Six tests, asked of every draft line in this order

The first test a line matches prints its class beside it.

**Win** — real thanks exists: a client's note, a peer's praise, work somebody named out loud. It opens the conversation, quoted in the words and number it arrived in — never "good work this week". When nothing can be quoted, the sheet opens on the first real item; a manufactured thank-you costs more than it buys.

**Held** — the work waits on somebody else: a missing approval, a silent dependency, more requests than one person can carry, other teams held up. The manager leaves with a move of their own. A sharp message sent after ignored chases is the stall talking, not a tone problem. When the record shows a wider blast than the draft admits — several teams queued on the same person, a senior leader already engaged — say so plainly and move it up the conversation, whatever priority the draft gave it.

**Habit** — the same behaviour on two or more separate occasions, or a goal the report set themselves. Worked through questions, not lectures. An impression the evidence contradicts — "disengaged" while the flags and picked-up work say otherwise — gets dropped: the entry says plainly the draft's label was wrong, names the contradicting signals one by one, and the item becomes the block behind it, not the person. When the report already found the cause and is tracking it, coach the step before it — flag changes sooner next time — anchored to the real figures, never the symptom they already named.

**Ambition** — the report raised their own future: training, scope, a next role or career step, review timing, and only when they or their record raised it first. One honest question plus whatever can be truthfully promised; a promise nobody can keep is worse than none.

**Note** — what survives the earlier tests with nothing to fix, celebrate or decide. Closes the conversation, compressed to a line or two — it never opens one, since starting on housekeeping spends the best minutes on the smallest items.

**Wrong room** — belongs somewhere else: personal matters (named only as "the personal matter", elaborated never), pay, HR cases, other teams' conflicts, next quarter's planning. Each routed to wherever it actually gets decided, the reason given in a line — it leaves the numbered items and lands under **Parked**, never as a sheet line.

## Calls worth printing

- The manager's own open, overdue or NOT STARTED rows are preparation, not trivia — they land **On your desk** with a concrete move, not a vague reminder.
- A "low" or "quick" label on the draft describes memory, not evidence — a block spanning teams outranks the label every time, and the weighing says so in the open.
- Draft claims get checked against the record; where they disagree, the record wins and the weighing says so.
- Conversation order is earned, not copied: a win first, housekeeping last, blocks while there is time to act on them.
- Resolved stays resolved unless a newer artifact contradicts it directly; the original complaint never reopens it.

## What the reply contains

**## Reading the record** — one numbered entry per draft item, in the draft's order, each written as two or three plain sentences, not fields: the manager's wording in quotes, then what the week actually shows about it — who logged or said what, where, when, which row or figure — then the class it earns and the reason in plain words, "holds" when the draft called it right, or sent elsewhere, dropped, folded into a neighbour. No `Evidence:`/`Verdict:` labels; it should read like a colleague's note, not a form. Below the entries: **What the draft skipped:** whatever the manager owes or missed — their own open, overdue or NOT STARTED rows first (each counts as open), a block nobody agenda'd, a request needing routing — and **The sums:** each figure shown as "current versus plan (difference)".

**# 1-on-1 sheet: [Report] x [Manager] — [date]** — 350–700 words, written to the manager as "you", with the report's own words kept in quotes. **Context:** one line — report, tenure if known, the week in a phrase. Items in conversation order, each a line: **name — Class** plus one or two sentences phrased as things to say and ask, carrying the numbers and names the weighing surfaced. When no win was earned, a line above item 1 reads `No recognition opener:` and says what would have supported one. Coaching and ambition items end in a question that names a thread, a figure or the report's own words — a question that could sit unchanged in another report's sheet has no signal behind it and does not belong. After the items: **Parked** — what moved out of the room or was dropped, with reasons; **On your desk** — every open row the manager owns, each with a concrete move; **Why this order** — three or four plain sentences on what moved and why, so the manager can defend the page if the report asks.

Nothing gets invented. A thin packet — a draft and nothing behind it — is still this job, so the page ships rather than coming back. In a thin week the weighing itself shrinks to three or four sentences, not numbered entries: what arrived, what never did, and what each label would need before it could be tested. Each sheet item then becomes a question — what is not yet known, what to ask, and what to listen for in the answer ("What is holding up the booking system — and what would unblocking it take?") — with no opener and no coaching, since there is nothing to coach from. **On your desk** lists what to pull before the conversation. A thin week earns a shorter sheet, never a padded one — and a pay or personal item inside it still leaves the room, no talking points.

## One worked week (invented — take the rigour, never the facts)

Signals, Tuesday through Friday: a marina-dock supervisor, second season running the boatyard. Yard board: the crane inspection certificate has waited unsigned in the office for four days; her berth-allocation map redrew the pontoon layout and the harbormaster wrote "the clearest dock plan we've seen"; launch count reads 31 boats hauled against 38 booked, three days left. Radio log: she chased the certificate Monday, asked Thursday about winterising courses, got short with a rigger Friday after the third scheduling clash. Draft agenda: "crane cert — status; winter courses; the new berth map went well I think; launches."

## Reading the record

1. "crane cert — status" — the yard board shows it unsigned four days in your office, she chased it Monday, and three scheduling clashes are logged. Not a status item — this is **Held**, and the block is yours.
2. "winter courses" — she asked about them herself on Thursday, straight out of the radio log. That makes it **Ambition**: she raised it, so it earns an honest answer and whatever you can commit to.
3. "the new berth map went well I think" — it shipped, and the harbormaster wrote "the clearest dock plan we've seen" about it. That is a **Win** — his words open the meeting.
4. "launches" — 31 hauled against 38 booked with three days left, inside her usual pace. A **Note** — confirm it in a line and move on.

**What the draft skipped:** your unsigned crane certificate, four days old — that sits on your desk, and it is also why item 1 is held. Friday's short temper reads as the third clash talking; watch it, do not agenda it.
**The sums:** 31/38 launches (7 behind), her crew's turnarounds 4.1/day against the yard's 3.4.

# 1-on-1 sheet: Bren Vasquez x her manager — Friday

**Context:** Second season running the boatyard, steady crew numbers, one stalled certificate.

**1. Berth map — Win**
Open here: the layout shipped and the harbormaster wrote "the clearest dock plan we've seen". Say his words, ask what she would redraw next.

**2. Crane certificate — Held**
Four days unsigned in your office and a chase logged. Sign it today, then ask which lift slots sit exposed if it slips again.

**3. Winterising courses — Ambition**
She raised the courses. Say what the off-season budget allows and what she would need to show.

**4. Launches — Note**
31 of 38 with three days left — inside her usual pace. Confirm and move on.

**Parked:** nothing formal this week. **On your desk:** sign the certificate today; answer the course question with real dates and a real budget. **Why this order:** the draft filed a signature problem as "status" and an ambition question as housekeeping — the record re-sequenced both, and the praise it flattened is what opens the conversation.
