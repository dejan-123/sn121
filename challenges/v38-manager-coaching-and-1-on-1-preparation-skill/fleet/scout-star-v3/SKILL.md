# Fill the slot, don't fill a template

A weekly 1-on-1 is a short slot of shared time. The manager arrives
with a draft list; the record decides what earns a place on the
slate. Nothing earns a place because a checklist says it should —
only because a fact in front of you makes it worth both people's
minutes.

Call `read_reference` on `references/worked-weeks.md` before
composing. It shows three full inputs worked end to end, including
one that should not become a slate at all. Learn its reasoning;
never reuse its people or sentences.

## First check: is this even a week?

A slate needs *current* material — this week's thread messages,
action states, figures, and the manager's topics. If the packet is
only notes or a transcript of a meeting that already happened, it
documents the last slot, not the coming one. A due date in old
minutes is not proof the item is open now. Reply briefly: name the
input as past-meeting notes, list each follow-up it recorded with
its owner and stated date, say their current states are unknown,
and ask for this week's signals plus any fresh topics. Keep
personal disclosures in old notes private. If new signals arrive
alongside the notes, build the slate from the signals and treat
the notes as background. Skip-level prep and rating drafts want
their own documents — say so and stop.

## Hold each topic up to the record

Count every entry: the manager's proposed topics, whatever the
report submitted, and open actions on both sides. For each, find
the concrete observation behind it. A label like "unreliable" or
"great work" is a claim waiting for its fact, not a fact. Keep the
topic; swap the label for what the record supports. Combine two
entries that share one cause, but note both. Add a topic only when
a supplied signal demands it — never to reach a count.

Sort ownership before assigning effort. Work stalled on another
team's approval, or on the manager's own unfinished commitment, is
not evidence about the report's habits — it is a decision the
manager owns, and it belongs early. When one person is the
approval point for several teams, that is load design: if a draft
filed it "quick" or "low" but half the org waits on it, raise it
and say why. If the report already proposed a redistribution, the
question is what the manager can authorize. When someone pushed a
justified request in a public channel while also handling it
politely in private, unblock the work *and* keep one short
question about the venue — reading both messages prevents both
mistakes.

Choose the lightest honest conversation:

- A named save, shipped result, or specific thanks earns the
  opening. An empty wish to "say something nice" earns nothing —
  start with the work instead.
- Progress, dependencies, and handoffs are status; a completed,
  praised task is credit, not another status line.
- A single observed slip, already repaired, is one feedback
  question about its effect and safeguard — not a pattern.
- A repeated behavior, or a skill someone is working on, is a
  coaching question tied to the named incidents. When the report
  already named the cause, build the preventive habit together.
- A development interest the report voiced — however briefly —
  gets unhurried time in the room, even in a sparse week. Ask
  which direction draws them; do not script their ambition, and
  do not convert it into a promotion or pay promise.

Pay rulings, health and family matters, formal complaints, and
policy-wide disputes get one line naming the proper venue and its
owner — zero scripted content in this slot. Career talk stays
here; the pay ruling does not. On thin inputs, asking for status
honestly is fine; asserting performance is not.

## Make the numbers carry weight

Work every comparison the input gives you: this figure against
that one, with the operation visible — `26 confirmed against 22`,
`up 7 points, from 72% to 79%`, `4 of 6 actions closed`. Attribute
unclosed work to its owner. Compute date gaps only against a
known meeting date. A past-due action inside old minutes is
history, not current delinquency. Conflicting records get a
question, not a comfortable guess.

## Compose the slate

Write for the person running the meeting. Name the report and
role, then a short paragraph on the week's hinge — the facts
that change what this slot should do, with worked figures. Then
the conversations in order, each with its plain category
(Credit, Work, One-off, Growth) or everyday wording, the signal
that put it on the slate, and a usable line or question — three
sentences at most. Open with earned credit; put manager-controlled
blockers ahead of routine updates; keep report-raised development
unhurried. Finish with the manager's own obligations and a single
elsewhere-note only when one exists. A sparse week makes a short
slate.

Last pass: every proposed topic appears, was folded into another
named topic, or was sent elsewhere with a reason. No private
content scripted. No invented fact, name, number, or owner.
