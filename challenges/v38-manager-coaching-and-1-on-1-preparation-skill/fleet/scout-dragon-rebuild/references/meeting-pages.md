# How the page should feel

These people, teams, and figures are invented. The examples show how
to decide what the manager can actually do with the available record.
They are not facts to borrow for a new report. Notice the short read
of the week, the distinction between the employee's and manager's
actions, and the lack of a mechanical table.

## A full week: the apparent performance problem is a dependency

Input for a Thursday meeting with Nadira Bell, a service designer at
Orchard Transit:

The manager lists "check progress on the journey-map pilot"; "coaching
on abrasive replies"; "say something positive if there is time"; and
"career later." The open-action list says Nadira's annotated map is
overdue from Tuesday, the manager's accessibility review request is
overdue from Monday, and Nadira's interview guide was filed on time
last Friday. The project board reports 18 rider interviews processed
this week against a trailing average of 15. Five maps are waiting on
the accessibility team's review of a component. In a Monday team
thread Nadira asks for that review a second time and adds, in the
public channel, "If this is not done by today, I am escalating it."
On Tuesday she tells the manager that she is covering the dispatcher
queue while a colleague is away. On Wednesday a research lead, Asha,
posts that Nadira's revised interview guide uncovered a recurring
onboarding problem before the pilot launch. Nadira herself asks for
time to discuss a senior-service-design role.

A usable page:

# Thursday with Nadira Bell, Service Designer

**Read before the meeting.** Output is up: 18 rider interviews
processed versus a 15-interview trailing average, three more or 20%
above that baseline. The five waiting maps share one accessibility
dependency; the map due Tuesday is blocked on a review the manager
requested and has not secured. The manager's own request is also
overdue. Treat the delay as a decision to unblock, not evidence that
Nadira has stopped doing the work.

**Start with the earned win.** Asha's Wednesday post credits the
revised interview guide with catching an onboarding problem before
launch. Name the guide and the result; this is a better opener than
generic praise. Her completed guide is not an open status item.

**Unblock the pilot — status, not an attitude review.** The journey
map and the proposed "abrasive replies" item share the same real
obstacle: five maps are awaiting the accessibility review, and
Nadira asked twice. Tell her the review request on the manager's side
is overdue. "Which part of the pilot can proceed while I secure the
review, and what should I escalate today?" Her Tuesday coverage note
also makes the shorter replies a possible workload effect rather
than evidence of a behavior pattern. There is one narrower feedback
point worth checking, however: Monday's public escalation may have
put avoidable pressure on the team. "Your Monday post raised the
right dependency; would a direct escalation to me land better next
time?" Do not recast a justified escalation as bad attitude.

**Make time for her career question.** Nadira raised the senior-role
path herself. "Your interview-guide work caught the onboarding issue
before launch; which parts of a senior role do you want to grow
into?" The manager should bring the level guide, not imply a
promotion is already decided.

**Manager to carry away.** Obtain the accessibility review and own
the overdue request. Resolve whether dispatcher coverage needs to
be redistributed; do not charge the resulting delay to Nadira.

Why this works: the manager's four filed topics are all handled,
although two become one main status conversation with a narrow
feedback question. The recognition has an external source. The
arithmetic shows its baseline. There is no character judgment
masquerading as coaching. The page does not tell the manager to
draft a disciplinary script.

## A sparse week: less material means a shorter page

Different invented input: Friday's meeting with Eli Okafor, a
documentation specialist at Winter Quay. The manager supplies only
"general status," "nice work this week," and "salary adjustment."
There are no action states, work metrics, named messages, or report
topics. No one has told us what Eli achieved. Do not invent a result
or convert the salary item into weekly talking points.

# Friday with Eli Okafor, Documentation Specialist

**Read before the meeting.** There are no current work figures,
action-item states, or named messages in the request. Pull the
documentation queue and ask Eli for their own topics before walking
in. No recognition opener is supported by what was supplied.

**One live conversation — status.** The manager's "general status"
question is legitimate, but no answer is on file. "What moved in the
documentation queue this week, and where do you need my help?" This
is a request for information, not a claim that anything slipped.

**Outside this meeting.** "Nice work" has no named instance: ask the
manager what accomplishment they mean before offering praise.
The salary adjustment belongs in a separate compensation
conversation, owned by the manager with the relevant compensation
process; no pay talking points are drafted here.

Do not inflate this into five agenda items. The missing information
is the useful finding. When a report raises an actual development
goal, it belongs in the weekly conversation; a pay decision itself
does not.

## A different request: last week's meeting notes alone

Another invented input: "Please prep next week's one-on-one with
Mina Cho," followed solely by minutes from a meeting that happened
last Thursday. They report a 91% service level last month, a
discussion about coverage, a personal disclosure about a parent,
and two agreed actions: the manager will contact scheduling by
Tuesday; Mina will send proposed coverage changes by Wednesday.
The request provides no current action states, work figures,
messages, or new meeting agenda. The correct artifact is a short
handoff, *not* a brief or a reconstructed agenda:

These are notes from last Thursday's meeting, not this week's
signals. They tell us what was discussed but not whether coverage
or service levels have changed since then. I would not write next
week's agenda as though those topics were still open.

The follow-ups to check are your scheduling contact, due Tuesday,
and Mina's coverage proposal, due Wednesday. Please send their
current states, any newer service or staffing figures, and the topics
either of you wants to bring next week; I can then prepare the
one-on-one. Keep the family matter in a private support
conversation, without repeating its details in a general prep page.

This answer neither discards the useful actions nor invents their
completion. If the manager had also supplied current work evidence,
a brief could use that evidence while treating the old minutes as
background.

## Change one fact, change the conversation

Use these variations as reasoning checks, not text to paste:

- If Nadira's replies were repeatedly sharp across several weeks
  and the messages named their effects, a coaching conversation
  could be warranted. Ask about the specific repeated pattern
  rather than calling her abrasive as a person.
- If the public message had been a polite private request, even
  the narrow feedback question would disappear. Being right about
  the dependency is not itself a conduct problem.
- If the accessibility review had arrived but Nadira still had not
  filed the map, the status question would shift to her next action.
  The original hold would not excuse an unrelated later delay.
- If her manager had labeled the five waiting maps "low priority"
  but several teams could not launch without them, put the
  dependency early in the meeting and name the blocked teams.
- If she had already proposed a workable escalation route,
  ask which part the manager can authorize; do not spend the
  session telling her what she already diagnosed.
- If Asha's post did not exist and there were no other positive
  signal, omit the opener rather than substituting generic praise.
- If the senior-role topic had been absent, do not manufacture
  career ambitions from the manager's vague "career later."
- If the disclosure in Mina's minutes suggested a need for
  flexibility, the manager can arrange a private support
  conversation; the weekly prep still must not script questions
  about her family's circumstances.
