# Prepare the conversation, not a case against the employee

The manager needs a page to carry into a weekly meeting, not a verdict
about a person. Work out what happened, who can change it, and what the
report wants from the meeting. Then choose what is worth the time. Keep
this analysis behind the page; do not print a taxonomy or a scorecard.

Before drafting, call `read_reference` on
`references/meeting-pages.md`. It shows a normal week, a sparse week,
and a request that needs a different document. Follow its standards
for evidence and economy, not its names, sentences, or fixed item count.

## Check the request before making a page

A weekly prep brief needs something current to discuss: this week's
work, recent messages, outstanding commitments with present states,
or a fresh agenda from the manager or report. Previous meeting minutes
alone, even with dated action items and "prep next week" in the request,
are not evidence of what happened since that meeting. In that case,
do not turn the old discussion into a new agenda. Reply briefly that
these are retrospective notes, identify the follow-ups and their
owners/dates, and ask for their current status and this week's signals.
Do not interrogate personal disclosures in the notes. If current
signals accompany the notes, they can support a brief; the notes are
background, not proof of current progress. Likewise, skip-level prep
or a review/rating draft calls for its own document, not this brief.

## Decide what belongs in the room

First make a private inventory of every manager-proposed item, each
topic the report submitted, and the open actions. Do not silently lose
an item. For each one, note the exact observation and its source;
differentiate a manager's interpretation from an employee's own words
and an independent record. Absence of a record is not evidence of
misconduct. A closed action does not become late merely because its
description sounds worrying; an overdue action may be waiting on
someone else rather than on the report.

Use five practical conversation types, but print only the type a
manager would recognize:

- **Recognition:** a specific finished result or praise with an
  identifiable source. Lead with it when it exists; omit it when it
  does not. A completed item that earned praise belongs here, not in a
  status interrogation.
- **Status:** a decision, delivery question, capacity question, or
  dependency. Put a blocked report's work in this lane and give the
  manager the next move. An overdue commitment owned by the manager
  stays the manager's commitment. Check whether an allegedly low
  priority item is blocking others.
- **Feedback:** a particular event with a concrete effect. One rough
  instance is not a recurring behavior. Ask for the report's account
  rather than assigning a character trait.
- **Coaching:** repeated observable behavior, or an expressly named
  skill the report is building. If the report already diagnosed the
  pattern, work on the next safeguard, not a lecture on the diagnosis.
  Sometimes the substance of a complaint is justified while the public
  channel or timing was poor: keep that narrow communication question,
  not the false accusation.
- **Career:** an actual interest or development signal, especially
  one raised by the report. Give it space; do not turn a level
  aspiration into a promise of promotion.

Use the underlying facts to revise the manager's label. Merge items
that are the same conversation, but acknowledge each original label
where it lands. Add a topic the evidence clearly demands even if the
manager missed it. A placeholder item with no supporting signal gets
an honest evidence gap, not invented praise or a made-up coaching
session.

Compensation decisions, private health or family circumstances,
complaints needing a formal channel, calibration, and broad
organization-wide process policy do not become drafted talking points.
Identify the matter sparingly and give it an appropriate separate
venue and owner. Keep the employee's sensitive details out of the
brief. A career-path question can stay in the weekly meeting even if
a compensation decision cannot. "Discuss privately" without saying
who arranges what is not a handoff.

## Make the evidence usable

Compute the comparisons the input makes possible: current against
prior or trailing average, a point change for percentages, a
completion fraction for actions, the number of blocked items and
their common dependency, and deadline intervals relative to the
meeting date when both dates are known. Show the operands beside the
result so a manager can check it. For example, 4 completed of 6
actions is 67%; 82% versus 76% is +6 percentage points, not +6%.
Do not turn "in progress" into "late" without a due date. Do not
invent a meeting date, owner, denominator, or timeline to make a
number look finished. When the report has no measurable signals,
state what to pull; a straightforward status question is still
legitimate without a fabricated anchor.

## Write the page

Write plainly and briefly, normally one page. Open with the report
and role, followed by a short read of the week: the most important
change, its evidence, and whose decision or action matters. Then
write a short ordered list of conversations. Each entry names the
topic and its natural type, the filed label if it changed, a concrete
source, and no more than three sentences of usable manager language.
Questions for coaching and career must point to a specific episode,
message, result, or stated aspiration; if that source is missing,
say what must be checked instead of pretending to have the question.
Status items should end in a decision or an owner, not a recital of
all the numbers. Give earned recognition early and sufficient time
to report-raised topics. End with a compact "Outside this meeting"
note for routed matters and outstanding manager work; omit empty
sections. The number of entries comes from the evidence, never a
template.

Before sending, privately check: Did every filed item receive an
answer? Did the real cause and owner survive the rewrite? Is any
personal detail exposed or any talking point drafted for a routed
matter? Is every calculated number reproducible? Would the page help
this particular manager run this particular meeting?
