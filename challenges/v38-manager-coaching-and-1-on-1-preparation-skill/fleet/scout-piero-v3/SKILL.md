# Write forward, from what actually happened

This skill produces the page a manager reads five minutes before
sitting down with their report. Everything on the page must point
forward — at a decision, an unblock, or a real conversation — and
everything must trace backward to a fact in the packet. A label
the manager typed is a suggestion, not a conclusion.

Read `references/case-notes.md` via `read_reference` first. Three
packets are worked there end to end, including one that produces
a request-for-signals rather than a page. Absorb the reasoning,
then write in your own words.

## When the packet isn't this week

A forward note needs present tense: current action states, recent
messages, this week's figures, live topics. Minutes or a
transcript of a 1-on-1 that already took place are backward-looking
— even when the manager says "prep next week," they show what was
agreed then, not what has happened since. Respond with a short
gap report instead of a page: name the input as prior-meeting
minutes, carry each recorded commitment with its owner and date,
state plainly that current status is unknown, and ask for this
week's signals and topics. Never convert an old due date into a
present overdue item. Personal details in old minutes stay out of
the page. If fresh signals arrive beside the minutes, write
forward from the signals and use the minutes as background. A
skip-level request or a formal rating needs a different document —
name it and stop.

## Sort what each topic really is

List the manager's topics, the report's own submissions, and open
commitments in both directions. Behind each topic, find the
specific fact — message, ticket, figure, or the report's own
sentence. Without one, the topic stays but its claim shrinks to a
question; with one, the label gets corrected to what the fact
supports. Merge topics sharing one cause while keeping each
visible; add a topic only when the record insists.

Charge stalled work to whoever holds the key. A task waiting on
another team's input or on the manager's unfinished promise is a
handoff to break, not evidence about the report — and it belongs
early because others wait on it. If one person reviews for many
teams, that is a resourcing choice the manager makes: filed as
"quick" or "low" while four teams wait, it goes near the top and
the reorder is said plainly. A report who already proposed a fix
gets asked what they need to run it, not why they are slow. Read
the full thread on tone: a legitimate block pushed publicly while
a courteous private request stayed open deserves the unblock
*and* one focused question about whether the public channel was
wise — one without the other is half a judgment.

Match the weight of the moment:

- Something worth naming — a shipped result, a caught problem, a
  specific thanks — opens the page. A wish to praise with no
  source opens nothing.
- Work in motion gets the question that moves it next; completed
  and already-credited work doesn't need a second pass.
- One observed slip, already fixed, gets a short what-next-time
  question — never a diagnosis of character.
- A repeated pattern or an openly developing skill gets a
  question anchored to the instances; if the report named the
  cause, design the early-warning habit with them.
- An ambition the report voiced gets real time in the room,
  even in a thin week — ask their direction, supply none of it,
  and promise no level or raise from an informal talk.

Compensation rulings, health and family specifics, formal
complaints, and org-wide policy need other rooms — one line naming
venue and owner, nothing scripted. Career talk stays in this room.
Thin packets make short pages: an honest "where does it stand?"
is useful; an invented standing is not.

## Show the arithmetic

Every comparison the input offers gets worked, not quoted —
both operands and the operation: `7 of 9 sites (was 5)`,
`+13 points: 58 to 71`, `three holds, one shared dependency`.
Date gaps compute only against a supplied meeting date. Missing
denominators, owners, and deadlines stay missing — named as
gaps, never filled with a guess.

## Compose the page

Open with the report's name, role, and a tight paragraph: what
moved, what is waiting, and the one decision the manager must not
leave without. Then the conversations in order, each carrying a
plain everyday label, the fact that earned its place, and a line
the manager can actually say — three sentences at most. Earned
credit opens; manager-held handoffs lead; the report's
development question keeps unhurried room. End with the
manager's own to-dos and, only if needed, one line for other
rooms. No internal vocabulary, no audit table, no padded length.

Before finishing: every proposed topic is placed, folded into a
named sibling, or sent elsewhere with a reason; no private
content is scripted; no fact, name, or number was invented.
