# Give the manager a run of show

Treat the meeting as scarce shared time. The manager walks in
with guesses about what to cover, but the report may be waiting
for a decision the manager has not made. Allocate the opening
to a real achievement, the middle to work the manager can
unblock, and protected space to a development request. Omit
segments with no factual reason to exist; never allocate time
to a stereotype about the employee. The final note tells the
manager how to conduct this conversation, not how the sorting
algorithm worked.

Call `read_reference` on `references/conversations.md` before
answering. It demonstrates one full week, a week with too little
evidence, and the different response needed for old meeting minutes.
Do not transfer invented names or wording into a real brief.

## Choose the correct job

Only create a current one-on-one prep page when there are current
signals or a present agenda with usable action states. Pasted notes
from a conversation that already took place are a record of the
last meeting, even when headed "prep next week." On their own
they cannot prove what happened afterward. In that case respond
without an agenda: identify the old notes, list concrete follow-ups
with stated owner and due date, say their current states are unknown,
and request this week's signals and fresh topics. Keep any
health or family mention private. If the packet contains newer
signals alongside the notes, build from the newer record. Also
redirect skip-level preparation and performance-review/rating
requests to the document they actually require.

## Place each issue in the run of show

Keep an off-page tally: every proposed topic, the employee's
own requests, and both parties' promised actions. Reconcile the
tally before sending. If the manager says "motivation" but the
messages say "waiting on a permit," preserve the topic while
changing the conversation. If two labels name one dependency,
give it one slot; explain the merge in natural language.

**Opening, if earned.** Quote the source and result behind a
real contribution. A completion alone is not automatically a
celebration; a named save, impact, or colleague's specific
thanks is. With no such fact, start with the work instead of
offering synthetic praise.

**Decisions before diagnoses.** Identify who controls each next
step. An employee with an overdue deliverable may be waiting
on a different team's approval, or on the manager's overdue
promise. Do not put that delay on the employee. If one
reviewer has become a queue for several groups, ask the
manager to choose a handoff or a capacity plan; do not
schedule "quick feedback" on the reviewer's speed. A
proposed rotation from the reviewer is a starting option,
not evidence of poor engagement. Move the issue earlier if
other people's work depends on it, regardless of its filed
priority.

**Behavior at the correct size.** For a single misstep,
prepare one feedback question about its effect and repair.
Repeated observations or a skill someone is developing
can justify coaching. When the employee has already
identified the problem, find a preventive habit together.
The decision on an escalation and the way it was delivered
can be different: solve a justified block while asking
briefly whether public pressure was the right channel.

**Development without promises.** A real aspiration from
the employee receives unhurried time and a question
connected to their work. Separate that conversation from
pay and promotion rulings that require another process.

Mark every proposed topic as in-room, combined with a named
topic, or handled elsewhere. Do not make up agenda entries
for the sake of balance. A straightforward status question
can collect missing information; an evaluation of
performance cannot be asserted without a signal.

Some topics consume the wrong room: compensation decisions, private
health or family facts, formal complaints, review-cycle judgments,
and broad policy debates. Name them minimally, specify the right
separate conversation or channel and its owner, and draft zero
talking points about their substance. Do not conflate an ordinary
career-path question with a compensation ruling.

## Spend figures honestly

When numbers are supplied, use them to explain the week, not to
decorate the page. Show current and baseline, the calculation
and the unit: 8 of 10 actions complete = 80%; 91% after 86% is
up 5 percentage points (not five percent). Attribute the
remaining work to its owner and blocker. Compare due dates to
the meeting date only if the date is known; do not make up
deadlines, denominators, or a meeting date. If something is
closed in the action log, leave it closed unless a later
independent signal shows it is not. If inputs disagree, say
what needs confirming rather than choosing a flattering story.

## Hand over the run of show

Write the report's name and role, then a brief opening read
of the week: the few facts that alter the meeting, with
worked comparisons and the manager's immediate decision.
List only the conversations that earned time. Give each
an ordinary Recognition, Status, Feedback, Coaching, or
Career heading, the source that warrants it, and a usable
line or question. If the manager filed a different label,
note the correction without an audit table. Coaching and
career questions must attach to a particular observation
or an aspiration the report expressed; don't put
universal icebreakers in those slots. No more than three
sentences of talking points per item. End with commitments
the manager owns and, if needed, a short separate-venue
note. A thin week should look thin.

Check before sending: each filed topic accounted for (kept,
merged, routed, or omitted with a reason); no private talking
points; no wrong owner; no invented result. The brief is for
this week's conversation, not a permanent diagnosis of the
employee.
