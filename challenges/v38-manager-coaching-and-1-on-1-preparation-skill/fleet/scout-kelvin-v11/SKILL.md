A page is ready when a manager could defend every line of it against the week's record. That is the test this skill applies — and it is why the checking is printed above the brief rather than kept private. A draft written from memory retains the headline and discards the evidence; the record restores the evidence, and printing the check lets the manager verify each call for themselves. Material that is not this week's prep — old minutes, a transcript, a review draft — fails the test entirely and gets a short note back.

## The readiness test

Before the brief is written, these all have to hold:

1. **Every draft item has a call.** One of six — Applause, Blocked, Recurring, Path, Notes, Not this room — and the evidence behind the call is written out: who said or logged what, where, when, with the row or figure. When the draft had it right, the check says "holds" and moves on.
2. **Nothing rests on memory alone.** Draft claims are checked against the record; where they disagree, the record wins and the check says so. Numbers are current versus plan with the difference — "41 of 50 (9 behind)".
3. **Statuses are read correctly.** Open, overdue and NOT STARTED all count as open; resolved stays resolved unless a newer artifact contradicts it directly. The original complaint never reopens a closed row.
4. **The manager's rows are on the page.** Every open row they own lands in Before the meeting with a concrete move — a NOT STARTED requisition is preparation, cleared before the report is asked for anything, above all a row an agenda item rests on.
5. **The fine calls are made.** Frustration that began as a chase on somebody else's row is the block talking — Blocked, not Recurring. Many messages on one problem still count as one; Recurring wants separate dated occasions. A suspicion with no signal under it gets dropped. A first slip the report caught themselves earns a quick word, not a slot. Applause opens only with a quote; when nothing quotable exists, a line above the first item admits it.
6. **Nothing invented.** A thin packet still ships: the check says what is missing and the brief asks instead of asserting — the unknowns, the questions, and what an honest answer contains.

## What the six calls mean

- **Applause** — real praise exists: a note, a thanks, work praised by name. Opens the meeting, quoted with the giver's words and the number. Never "good week".
- **Blocked** — somebody else holds it: missing approval, silent dependency, more than one person can carry. The manager leaves with a move of their own; sharp messages after ignored chases are the block talking, not a tone problem. Where the record shows the block reaching wider than the draft's label allows — several teams waiting, a director already in it — that goes in the call and the item climbs above its written priority.
- **Recurring** — the same behaviour in two or more separate situations, or a goal the report set. Coached with questions, not lectures. An impression the evidence contradicts — "disengaged" while the log shows early flags — is dropped, the signals named. When the report already found the cause and tracks it, coach the step before it — flag changes sooner next time — anchored to real figures, not the symptom they found.
- **Path** — the report raised growth, training, scope or review timing themselves. One honest question and whatever can be promised truthfully.
- **Notes** — nothing to fix, celebrate or decide. Last, compressed to a line or two.
- **Not this room** — pay, HR, another team's conflict, next quarter's planning: routed where it gets decided, a line saying why. Personal matters stay private — "the personal matter", elaborated never.

## The page, laid out

**## Against the record** — the check, printed. One numbered entry per draft item, in the draft's order: label in quotes; **Against:** what the record shows; **Call:** the class and the reason — or parked, dropped, folded into another. Then **Missed:** — what the manager owes or the draft skipped — and **Figures:** the arithmetic, worked from its operands — "1.8% against a 2% allowance is 0.2 under" — operands shown, points not percent.

**# 1-on-1 brief: [Report] x [Manager] — [date]** — 350–700 words. **Context:** one line — report, tenure, the week in a phrase. Items in meeting order, **name — Call, ~N min** with a sentence or two written as things to say and ask, carrying the real numbers and names. Then **Set aside** (parked or dropped, with reasons), **Before the meeting** (the manager's open rows, each with a move), and **Thinking** (three or four lines on why the calls landed as they did).

## A worked week (invented — take the reasoning, never the facts)

The record for the week: a brewery cellarman, seven years in. Board: the fermenter-2 glycol fault is open with the engineer, two callouts unanswered; the seasonal batch he re-yeasted drew "cleanest profile of the year" in the head brewer's tasting note; a grain-order approval sits NOT STARTED on the manager's list. Log: he flagged the glycol fault Monday, re-flagged Thursday, asked Wednesday about the quality-lab rotation. Draft agenda: "fermenter update; seasonal batch; lab rotation; grain order."

## Against the record

1. "fermenter update" — **Against:** engineer callouts x2 unanswered, his Monday flag. **Call:** **Blocked** — yours to chase; commit a visit date.
2. "seasonal batch" — **Against:** head brewer's note: "cleanest profile of the year". **Call:** **Applause** — open with the words.
3. "lab rotation" — **Against:** he asked Wednesday about quality-lab time. **Call:** **Path** — he raised it; say what the rota allows and what it leads to.
4. "grain order" — **Against:** your approval row, NOT STARTED. **Call:** yours — off his draft, onto your own list.

**Missed:** the grain-order approval — your row, five days sitting. **Figures:** cellar losses 1.8% against a 2% allowance (-0.2).

# 1-on-1 brief: Tom Hale x his manager — Friday

**Context:** Seven years in the cellar; the head brewer wrote up his batch.

**1. Seasonal batch — Applause, ~3 min**
The head brewer wrote "cleanest profile of the year" — say the words, ask what he changed in the re-yeast.

**2. Fermenter-2 — Blocked, ~7 min**
Two engineer callouts unanswered. Tell him when you will chase it and the visit date you are holding them to.

**3. Lab rotation — Path, ~10 min**
He raised quality-lab time. Say what the rota can carry and where it leads.

**4. Numbers — Notes, ~3 min**
Losses at 1.8% against a 2% allowance. One line.

**Set aside:** the grain order moved to the Before the meeting list. **Before the meeting:** chase the engineer today; approve the grain order before Friday close. **Thinking:** the readiness test caught two wrong labels — a supplier block is not an update and a tasting note is not "batch" — plus the approval the draft owed and never listed.

# Not a 1-on-1 prep request

When what arrives is minutes, a transcript, or notes of a conversation already had — whatever its label — the reply is one short note, under 180 words, with no check section:

- Say what it is: the record of a past conversation, which cannot prep a future one.
- Where open actions survive inside it, write the follow-up — owner, action, due date — in a line or two; never a brief, never a re-sort.
- Say what could: this week's open items, current figures, anything flagged since.
- Personal material stays "the personal matter", nothing more.
