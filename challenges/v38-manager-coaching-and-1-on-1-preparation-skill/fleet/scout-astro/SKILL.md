# Notes for the chair

A manager chairs their weekly 1-on-1. The chair's job is to
spend a short meeting on the right business in the right order —
and to know which business belongs to a different meeting
entirely. This skill writes the chair's private notes: the
openers, the questions, the decisions to walk out with, and the
one-line referrals for business that isn't this meeting's.

Before drafting, call `read_reference` on
`references/chair-practice.md`. It works three packets in full,
including one where the right response is to return the packet.
Borrow the judgment; compose fresh each time.

## Business or minutes?

Chair's notes need this week's material: live threads, action
states, current figures, topics from either seat. Minutes or a
transcript of a 1-on-1 already held are the record of a meeting
that is over — they say what was agreed then, nothing about now.
Return the packet instead of drafting: identify it as
prior-meeting minutes, restate each recorded commitment with
owner and date, say their current states are unknown, and ask
for this week's signals and fresh agenda items. An old deadline
proves nothing about today; never present it as overdue now.
Personal disclosures in minutes stay out of any draft. Fresh
signals beside old minutes are usable — chair from the signals,
cite the minutes as history. Business addressed to a skip-level
or a formal rating needs its own paper; name it and decline the
agenda.

## What belongs on the floor

Itemize the manager's topics, the report's submissions, and
open commitments on both sides. For each, locate the grounding
fact — a message, ticket, figure, or the report's own words.
A characterization without a fact becomes a question, not an
assertion. Merge items sharing one cause but keep each visible;
add business only when a supplied signal requires it.

Decide who can move each item before scheduling it. Work stalled
behind another team's input or the manager's own unfinished
commitment is chair business — the unblock — not evidence about
the report, and it leads because others wait. A single reviewer
holding several teams' queue is a staffing decision: when the
draft files it "quick" or "low" but half the floor waits, move
it up and say why on the page. If the reviewer proposed a
rotation, the chair's question is what to authorize. Read tone
complaints against the whole record: someone who pushed a
justified block publicly while keeping a courteous private
thread earns the unblock *and* one precise venue question —
either half alone is a worse call.

Match the floor time to the moment:

- A named result or specific thanks opens; an unsourced urge
  to compliment opens nothing.
- Live work gets the question that unblocks it; finished and
  already-credited work doesn't get a second item.
- One observed misstep, already corrected, gets a single
  repair question — not a development program.
- A repeated pattern or openly developing skill gets a
  question anchored to the instances; when the report named
  the cause first, build the early-warning habit together.
- A direction the report voiced gets real floor time even in
  a sparse week — ask their direction, never assign it, and
  never turn conversation into a level or pay promise.

Compensation decisions, health and family matters, formal
complaints, and broad policy get a referral line — proper
meeting, named owner, nothing scripted. Career talk stays;
the pay ruling goes. Thin packets make short notes: a genuine
"where does it stand?" is good chairing; a fabricated position
is not.

## The arithmetic check

Work every figure the packet offers: both operands, the
operation, the result — `7 of 9 (was 5)`, `58 to 71 = +13
points`, `three holds, one dependency`. Date gaps compute only
against a meeting date the packet provides. Missing numbers
stay missing — named as gaps, not invented.

## Write the chair's notes

Name the report and role, then a short read of the week —
what moved, what's waiting, the one decision the chair must
leave with — figures shown worked. Then the items in order:
plain everyday labels, the grounding fact, and a line the
chair can actually say, three sentences at most per item.
Earned credit opens; chair-controlled unblocks lead; the
report's development topic keeps its floor time. Close with
the chair's own to-dos and one referral line only when a
referral exists. No internal vocabulary, no audit table,
no padding for a thin week.

Final pass: every drafted item is on the floor, folded into a
named sibling, or referred elsewhere with a reason; no private
matter scripted; nothing invented.
