# Three rehearsal inputs

Everything here is invented. These are illustrations of judgment
and proportion, not material to repeat in a real manager's brief.
The conversations are different lengths because the inputs are.

## A crowded week with the wrong owner on the manager's list

Etta Ruiz is a Warranty Quality Lead at Westfold Appliances. Her
manager's draft lists "inspection-packet progress"; "low-priority
quick feedback: too many sign-offs on Etta's desk, she needs to
respond faster";
"commend her for last week's event"; and "promotion check if time."
The action log records Etta's audit checklist completed Monday,
her recommendation on vendor code still in review, and the
manager's proposed reviewer rota not yet circulated. This week
Etta completed 12 inspection packets against 9 the previous week.
Six packets wait on the vendor's component code. A quality channel
shows that four departments have asked her for sign-off this week.
Her Tuesday note says: "I can't be sole sign-off for all four teams.
Could we pilot a rotation? I can train a second reviewer." On
Wednesday, a regional lead named Arun publicly thanks her for
catching a faulty seal before the shipment left. Etta herself
asks what evidence she would need for a senior quality role.

The manager's usable page:

# Etta Ruiz | Warranty Quality Lead | Weekly conversation

**What's changed.** Output is moving: 12 inspection packets completed
versus 9 last week, three more or 33% above last week. Six packets
are waiting on a vendor component code; that hold is not Etta's
delay. The more urgent decision is how to stop four departments
depending on one sign-off queue. This is a high-priority
management decision, despite the manager's low-priority label.
Etta has already proposed a reviewer rotation; the manager
has not yet circulated their own rota proposal.

**1. Recognition - the seal catch.** Arun's Wednesday quality-channel
post credits Etta with catching a faulty seal before shipment.
Name the incident and its consequence; this is the "commend her"
item the manager could not otherwise ground.

**2. Reviewer capacity - Status, not speed feedback.** The manager
filed this as low-priority feedback on Etta's responses. Four
departments are waiting for one reviewer, so it is high priority:
their work cannot advance until the review load is redistributed.
Etta has offered to train a
second one: "Your Tuesday rotation proposal sounds practical.
Which sign-offs should we move first, and who could I authorize
to train with you?" Circulating the rota is the manager's action,
not Etta's unexplained failure. Put this before a routine packet
update because four teams depend on it.

**3. Vendor code - Status.** Six packets cannot clear before the
vendor supplies the missing code. "Which of those six can be
processed in parallel, and do you want me to escalate the vendor
request?" Do not describe six waiting packets as six neglected
packets.

**4. Senior role - Career.** Etta asked for this herself; talk
about it in this meeting. "Your
seal catch and your proposed reviewer-training plan are concrete
evidence; what parts of the senior quality role interest you most?"
Bring the level guide to a follow-up for any formal decision,
instead of promising a promotion from these signals alone.

**Manager to do.** Circulate the reviewer rota and decide who can
share sign-off responsibility. Help move the vendor code request.

The distinction in item 2 is not a matter of nicer wording.
If the manager kept the "respond faster" label, the meeting
would wrongly ask Etta to work harder at a queue the organization
created. The report's own proposal changes the next question
from "why are you behind?" to "what can I authorize?"

If Tuesday's note had instead said she had ignored an agreed
handoff to the second reviewer on three occasions, and the
reviewer confirmed it, there could be a coaching pattern.
That is not what the supplied record says.

If Etta had made a justified but pointed escalation in a public
quality channel, while a later private request was calm, the
approval still needs unblocking. Also keep a small, separate
feedback question about whether the public pressure was the
right venue; the private message does not erase the public one.

## A single slip, not a personality story

Different invented input: Owen Salazar, a Warehouse Planning Analyst
at Saltbrick Goods. The manager writes "coaching: inaccurate orders."
One order was sent with an obsolete transit code last Monday; the
incident log says it is the first such mistake. Owen spotted it
the next morning and sent the corrected order himself. The
manager's replacement checklist for the new codes is overdue.
Separately, another team lead, Mabel, posted that Owen's revised
stock report prevented an unnecessary shipment. His own topic is
whether he could mentor new analysts. There is no record of
repeated order mistakes.

# Owen Salazar | Warehouse Planning Analyst | Weekly conversation

**What's changed.** One wrong transit code was corrected by Owen
the next morning. It is the first recorded incident, not a
pattern of inaccurate orders. The replacement checklist that
would have caught it is still owed by the manager.

**1. Recognition - the stock report.** Mabel credited the revised
report with stopping an unnecessary shipment. Tell Owen exactly
which work helped.

**2. Order review - Feedback, not broad coaching.** The manager's
label "inaccurate orders" overstates one corrected incident.
"You spotted and repaired Monday's obsolete code the next morning.
What check would catch the old code before send next time?" The
manager must finish the replacement checklist; it is the missing
process control, not a reason to call Owen careless.

**3. Mentoring - Career.** Owen asked about supporting new
analysts. "Your stock report was useful to Mabel's team.
Which part of that work would you most want to teach?"
This is a development question, not a promise of a new title.

**Manager to do.** Deliver the updated checklist and agree how
the next order will be checked.

If the log instead showed the same uncaught error on several
orders after the new checklist was available, then a repeated
behavior question might be warranted. One fixed mistake is
not enough to make that call.

## When there is very little to work with

The manager of support rep Samir Dube writes:
"How's his migration task? Give him a shoutout; he mentioned
compensation and maybe development." No action states, work
figures, named praise, or employee-submitted topic came along.
The page should be correspondingly short:

# Samir Dube | Support Representative | Weekly conversation

**What's changed.** There is no current status for the migration
task, no named achievement to recognize, and no evidence of
which development path Samir means. Ask for the task board
before the meeting; do not declare the migration on track.

**1. Migration task - Status.** "Where has the migration reached,
and what would you need from me to move it?" The question
collects information; it does not pretend there was a status
signal.

**2. Development - Career, if Samir still wants it.** The only
source is the manager's secondhand mention. "You had mentioned
development; is there a direction you want to explore?" Do
not fill in a career ambition for him.

**Outside this meeting.** There is no basis for the manager's
suggested praise. Identify a real accomplishment before
opening with recognition. The compensation topic needs a
separate conversation owned by the manager through the
appropriate pay process, with no pay script here.

No padded fifth item and no invented number improves this.

## Minutes from a prior conversation are not a weekly run sheet

A manager says "prep my next meeting with Talia Ngo" and pastes
only last Tuesday's notes. They say the team discussed an
installation backlog; the manager agreed to request an updated
safety checklist by Friday; Talia agreed to draft an installation
plan by Monday; Talia mentioned a personal matter requiring
some scheduling flexibility. There is no update after the meeting
and no current status of either action. Do not write a
fictionalized next meeting. A good response is:

These are notes from the meeting already held, not an update on
this week's work. I can carry forward two follow-ups: your
safety-checklist request was due Friday, and Talia's installation
plan was due Monday. The notes do not tell us whether either
has happened.

Please send the current status of those actions, any new
installation figures or team messages, and what you or Talia
want to cover next time. Then I can build the one-on-one run
sheet. The personal scheduling matter belongs in a private
support conversation, not prepared talking points.

This short response keeps the owners and dates supplied without
claiming the actions are still overdue or that the old discussion
is a current meeting agenda.
