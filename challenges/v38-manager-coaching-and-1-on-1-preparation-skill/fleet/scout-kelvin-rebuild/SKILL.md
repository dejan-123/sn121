# A worklist for the manager, not a report card on the employee

A good brief changes what the manager will say and what the manager
will do. Start from the work and its owner, not the adjective attached
to the employee in a draft agenda. The manager should leave with an
order for the meeting, specific questions, and commitments they own.

Read `references/run-sheet.md` with `read_reference` before drafting.
It models a crowded week, a sparse week, and a request that should
not produce a brief. Use its decisions, not its facts or prose.

## Step zero: is there a current week?

A draft agenda plus current work, recent messages, or action states
can support a brief. Minutes or a transcript from a meeting *already
held*, with no evidence of what changed afterward, cannot. Even if
the manager writes "prep next week's one-on-one," the old minutes
only tell us which follow-ups were agreed, not whether they happened.
Respond with a short handoff instead of an agenda: identify the
material as prior meeting notes, carry forward the actions with
owners and stated dates, and ask for current states, work signals,
and fresh topics. Do not infer that an action with a past due date
is still open without a current status. Personal circumstances in
old notes stay private. If fresh signals accompany old notes, use
the signals to make the brief and treat the notes as history.
Skip-level prep and review or rating drafts likewise need their own
artifacts, not a weekly one-on-one run sheet.

## Make the worklist privately

Count the manager's proposed topics, the employee's submitted topics,
and open actions. For each, ask: What exact observation supports it?
Who can act on it? Is it worth time in *this* meeting? Do not lose a
filed item just because it was mislabeled; merge overlapping topics
and retain a brief trace of each. Add a clearly signaled topic the
manager missed; do not add a topic solely because a template expects
it. A claim of "disengaged," "poor attitude," or "late" is not an
observation. Check the thread, action owner, figures, and dependencies
before accepting it.

When work waits on another team, distinguish a report who failed to
act from a report who has already asked for the missing approval.
Surface the manager's own overdue deliverable and the escalation they
can make. Do not turn an upstream hold into employee coaching. One
person reviewing an unsustainable queue for several teams is a
capacity/design problem; elevate a supposedly minor feedback item
to a high-priority, manager-owned resourcing decision when the
downstream impact demands it. Say explicitly when the manager filed
it low priority and why multiple blocked teams make it urgent.
If the report already proposed a rotation or fix, start from that
proposal. Read every relevant message before judging communication:
a justified escalation in a public channel may warrant one narrow
feedback question about venue, even if a later private message is
constructive. Keep the upstream unblock as the main status item;
neither the public venue nor the valid request cancels the other.

Use the smallest warranted conversation type:

- Recognition needs an actual result or attributable praise. Put it
  early when supported; don't manufacture it for a pleasant opener.
- Status is a work update, blocker, capacity decision, or next owner.
  Completed, praised work usually belongs in recognition, not another
  status check.
- Feedback addresses one observed event and its impact without
  pretending it is a persistent trait. If it is a first slip already
  acknowledged, focus on the repair or process safeguard.
- Coaching concerns repeated behavior or an explicitly developing
  skill. Ask about the named pattern. If the employee has diagnosed
  it, plan an early-warning practice, not a lecture.
- Career begins with a real development interest, particularly one
  the report raised. Keep an anchored career question inside this
  weekly meeting, even when the input is thin; ask which direction
  interests them rather than inventing their ambition. Protect time
  for it, but never promise a title or compensation change from an
  incomplete record. Book a separate formal promotion or pay decision
  only after this in-room development discussion, not instead of it.

Pay decisions, health and family details, complaints requiring a
formal process, and organization-wide policy do not get weekly
talking points. Record only the minimum necessary to arrange the
appropriate separate venue and identify who arranges it. A request
to discuss a career path stays in this meeting even if details
still need confirming; a pay ruling does not. Where there is no
supporting signal, say what is missing rather
than drafting an invented achievement or concern. With thin input,
an honest question asking for status is useful even without an
evidence anchor; an assertion about performance is not.

## Check the figures before writing

Show the operands for comparisons that matter: current versus prior,
percent change versus percentage-point change, actions completed
out of actions filed, dates against the meeting date when it is
known, and blocked items with a shared dependency. For example,
6 of 8 actions done = 75%; 76% to 82% = +6 percentage points
(about 8% relative). Do not invent a due date, denominator, meeting
date, owner, or upstream ETA. A due date in the past is not proof of
current delinquency if the only record is historical notes.

## Write the run sheet

Use plain prose and only as much space as the week needs. Open with
the report's name, role, and a short "What's changed" paragraph:
the important facts, computed differences, and one decision the
manager must make. Follow with the actual meeting order: specific
item headings using Recognition, Status, Feedback, Coaching, or
Career, and brief lines that state the source and what to say or
ask. Note a manager label only where correcting it matters. For a
coaching or career item, the question must connect to a named
incident, thread, result, or stated aspiration. Keep each set of
talking points to three sentences at most. Earned recognition goes
first; manager-owned bottlenecks come early; employee-raised career
topics get real time. Close with the manager's follow-through and
separate venues for sensitive matters. If nothing needs routing,
leave that section out. No internal ledger, gate labels, audit
table, or invented fixed agenda length in the answer.

Before replying, verify that every proposed topic is either in the
order, merged with another named topic, or explicitly routed or
omitted with a reason. Check that the wrong owner is not charged,
numbers are reproducible, and no private detail or unsupported claim
has entered the meeting script.
