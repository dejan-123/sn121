# Auditor's notes — review comments on diligence memos

Margin comments from an outside reviewer who has signed off on two
dozen acquisition files. They explain why the ledger is run the way
it is — what survives review and what gets sent back.

## On statuses

The fastest way to lose a reviewer is to carry a label the evidence
doesn't support, and the second fastest is to change a label
without showing the work. A filing that reads Resolved should still
say Resolved in the memo — beside the line that shows what the
record actually did. The reviewer doesn't want to re-audit the
file; the memo has to show the audit was already done.

## On the base

Every restatement is defensible or it isn't, and the difference is
a citation. "Revenue does not transfer" with no contract reference
is an opinion; "the grant's own terms restrict it to nonprofit
holders" is a fact. A reviewer will check two or three of these and
generalize the result to all of them — the cost of one unsupported
cut is the credibility of every other one.

## On numbers

Reviewers multiply two things and no more: whether the band equals
the multiple times the base, and whether the ask divided by the
base produces the quoted gap. A memo that shows eleven calculations
is asking to be audited; a memo that shows three has already been
audited. Where the base restates into two bases, showing the ask's
implied multiple on each is the comparison — that is what the
restatement was for.

## On the recommendation

The sentence that earns its space is the one naming why the
rejected calls don't fit. "We request more data" tells the reviewer
nothing about judgment; "we request more data because both open
items are documents the seller can produce, not walls" shows the
memo ran the other two calls and lost them. A pass that names what
would reopen the file reads as discipline, not caution.

## On conditions

A reviewer reads conditions as the only part of the memo that
describes the future. "Obtain documentation" isn't a condition —
it's a wish. A condition that closes is a named paper, a named
issuer, a named point in the timeline, and a named consequence for
absence. Invented escrow sizes and invented deadlines are worse
than no figures at all: they fabricate the one section that is
supposed to be the most factual.

## On what gets sent back

The memos that return for rework fail in patterns: a row silently
dropped; a status upgraded or downgraded without a bridge; a range
that doesn't foot to its own multiples; a proceed over an open
must-address; a pass whose blocker is a pending permission someone
could simply request. None of these are subtle — and none of them
survive a reviewer who reads the input next to the memo.
