# Chair's notes — how these memos get read

Notes from the chair's side of the table, kept over two dozen
acquisition reviews. They describe how a committee actually consumes
a memo — which is why the contract is shaped the way it is.

## On the first line

Committee members read the verdict line before they read anything
else, and most of them never fully recover from it. A memo that
hides its call until section four spends the whole document
fighting a verdict the reader already formed from the cover email.
Say it first, say it plainly, and let the document earn it back.

## On tables

A risk table is not a summary — it is an audit trail. When a member
spots an input row missing from the memo's table, the memo loses
its license to opine on everything else. The reader cannot tell
whether the analyst weighed the row and found it light, or never
saw it. Carrying every row, visibly, is what buys the right to
disagree with one.

## On disagreement with the filing

Committees are allergic to quiet repair. A row that says Resolved
in the input and Unresolved in the memo with no bridge reads as a
mistake, not a finding. The bridge is one clause — "filed Resolved;
the record shows the renewal letter was never countersigned" — and
it is the single highest-value sentence in the document.

## On arithmetic

Two numbers are checked: whether the multiple times the base makes
the range, and whether the ask divided by the base makes the quoted
gap. A member with a phone calculator will run both in under a
minute. Every other number in the section is prose wearing a
costume — the moment the memo shows a fourth calculation, someone
finds the error in it and the two correct products die with it.

## On price

An expensive ask is a negotiation memo, not a kill memo. Committees
have watched good companies get passed for being priced rich and
then watched a competitor buy them at a smaller discount than the
memo said was impossible. The ask belongs in the valuation as a
comparison; the recommendation belongs to the record.

## On conditions

"Obtain further documentation" is not a condition — it is a wish.
A condition that closes is a named piece of paper, a named issuer,
and a named point in the timeline. The member who has sat through
three failed closes reads the conditions first after the verdict
line, because they are the only part of the memo that describes the
future.

## On restraint

The hardest memo to write is the short one. A committee that reads
"proceed" over a clean file still wants to see the audit — the rows
carried, the base restated, the range shown — because the trust in
the verdict comes from seeing that the machine ran, not from the
verdict itself. And the inverse failure is real: a memo that hunts
for blockers to justify its own caution teaches the committee to
discount its caution next time, when it might matter.

## On what does not survive

Fabricated specifics are fatal in a way vague ones are not. A
committee forgives "the artifacts do not state the renewal date."
It does not forgive a date that proves, on checking, to have been
composed rather than found. The memo that names its gaps honestly
earns the right to be believed where it is confident.
