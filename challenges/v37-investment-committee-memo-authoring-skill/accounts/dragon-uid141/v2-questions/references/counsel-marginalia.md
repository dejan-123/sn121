# Counsel's marginalia — questions a skeptical reader asks back

Notes a deal counsel leaves in the margin of diligence memos. They
explain why the examination is run the way it is — the questions
that get asked after the memo is written.

## "Which input row did you drop?"

Counsel counts rows. A finding present in the table and absent from
the memo is not a judgment call — it is an unread page. Every row
carries, or the memo explains why it does not; silence reads as
oversight, never as restraint.

## "Where does Resolved become open?"

In the record, and nowhere else. A status the memo disagrees with
stays filed — the disagreement is quoted beside it, with the fact
that earns it. Counsel is not checking whether the memo's statuses
are right; counsel is checking whether the memo's statuses are
*shown*.

## "Show me the multiplication."

Two products and a division — that is what gets checked. A
valuation that shows its two products and one implied multiple is a
valuation that can be verified in a hallway conversation. A
valuation with eleven figures is a valuation with eleven places to
be wrong, and counsel only needs to find one.

## "What is the ask measured against?"

The right base, or the question is planted on purpose. An ask at
5.6x of gross reads reasonable beside an ask at 9.8x of the
run-rate — and the spread between those two readings is the whole
point of restating the base. Both readings are written, because the
denominator is the argument.

## "Why not the other two?"

A recommendation that does not argue down its alternatives argues
nothing. Counsel wants the sentence that says why proceed fails
(the open must-address), why pass fails (the record can settle it),
or why request-more-data fails (the record cannot exist). Whatever
the call, its rejection of the other two is what makes it a
recommendation rather than a guess.

## "Who signs, when?"

Every condition answers three questions: which paper, whose hand,
which point in the timeline. A condition without all three is a
hope with a deadline it never set. Counsel has closed enough deals
to know the only part of the memo that becomes a contract is the
conditions page.

## "What did you make up?"

Nothing survives invention. A fabricated date, percentage or escrow
size reads worse than a named gap — the gap is diligence, the
invention is fraud-adjacent. `[to confirm: …]` is an honest mark;
a composed figure is a mark on the analyst.
