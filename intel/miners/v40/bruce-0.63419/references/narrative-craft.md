# Narrative craft: making the benchmark read as advice

The table carries the numbers; the prose carries the decision. A reader should be able to stop after the first two sentences and still know what to do about the open roles.

## Choosing the first sentence

Find the fact this particular company most needs, then say it with its number:
- An open priority role whose band trails, or whose band max sits below a clean offer.
- A pattern in the company's own hiring (offers landing high in the band, repeated declines).
- A role that looked fine on the raw pool but changes once non-comparable points are removed.
- Where every band is fine, say so plainly and name what deserves watching.

Avoid openers that could start any report ("Here is the benchmark table", "Based on the data provided"). Name the company or the role in the first line; two companies should never get the same first five words.

## The table

One row per internal role, including deferred ones. Keep cells short: shorthand money ($166.4k), each cited point as ID, type, month and figure rather than a description, a deciding reason of a few words. Columns that usually earn their place: role and level, band midpoint, evidence used, market reference with its basis (offer or range midpoint), gap, position, tier with reason. Add a column only if this company needs it (a band-max check for a hard-to-fill role, a currency column for a multi-country team); for an executive reader, fold the evidence trail into one closing line.

## Where caveats go

A caveat that applies to every row (all figures base salary, the window, the anchor metro, "ranges are aspirational") is said once, near the table. Row cells hold only what is specific to that row. Repeating the same warning on five rows hides the one row where the warning actually matters.

## The exclusion ledger

Give the total (for example "9 of 16 set aside") and each excluded ID with its type or month and a reason of a few words. Group by reason when that is shorter ("E-04 and E-14: off-stage"). The reader should be able to reconcile the count without rereading the input.

## Audience variants

- **Comp analyst or HR partner:** IDs inline, conversions shown, ledger complete, next steps operational (adjust midpoint, approve upper-third offers, gather a specific point).
- **Executive or board:** decision and cost first, then a three- or four-row summary; IDs and ledger move to a short closing line. Translate gaps into what they mean for hiring speed or attrition.
- **Hiring manager:** the role they own first, what offer they can realistically make inside the band, what approval they need if not.
- **Recruiter:** where to land offers, which candidate claims to treat with caution, which evidence gap to fill from the next debrief.

## Length tactics

Aim for roughly 450 words, under 5,000 characters including the table. When over: shorten the headline to one sentence, merge ledger reasons, cut adjectives from tier reasons, drop any sentence that restates a table cell. Never drop a role row or a conversion step to save space.

## Closing

End on the next concrete action for the most urgent role or the single evidence gap that would change a verdict. Not a summary, not an offer to help further. Different situations produce different closings: a midpoint move, an approval rule for offers, a request for a base/variable split, a policy question about remote pay.

## Style notes

- Numbers from the input or computed from it, nothing remembered.
- Percentages to one decimal place; negative means the band is under the market reference.
- Plain verbs: trails, matches, exceeds, cannot be called yet.
- Headings only if the reply is long enough to need them, and then specific to the content ("Why the sales row moved"), never a fixed set reused across reports.
