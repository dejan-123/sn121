# Tiers and verdicts: borderline calls

The tier answers one question: if the company acted on this row, how likely is the read to hold up when the next candidate negotiates? The deciding reason is the single fact that set the tier, written so a reader could challenge it ("one posted range, no offers" is a reason; "limited data" is not).

## Why offers outrank ranges, and why one of anything is thin

A posted range is what an employer says it might pay, set before it meets anyone, often widened to satisfy disclosure laws or to keep options open. An accepted offer is the price at which a specific employer and candidate actually agreed. That is why a range's top is never quoted as the market, and why an offer sitting inside a same-stage range earns High: the two tell one story from different sides.

A lone accepted offer earns Medium and no more. It is a real price, which is why it outranks any number of postings, but it is one negotiation: it may carry a competing bid, a relocation premium or a hiring manager who stretched, and nothing in the pool shows whether it is typical. Posted ranges alone stay Low however many agree, because several employers advertising the same level is a pattern of intentions; that is the posted-range cap, and only an offer lifts a row past it. A single voluntarily posted range with no offer on either side, none external and none of the company's own, is deferred: an advertisement with nothing to test it against cannot place a band, so name it as context and say what would unlock a call. A single range posted under a pay-transparency mandate is the exception: the law requires a good-faith span, so it carries a Low call under the posted-range cap. The one exception is a bracket: a partial from the round below that reads lower and a partial from the round above that reads higher confirm the lone range from both sides, so it reads Low under the posted-range cap. A partial on one side only, or partials with no comparable range between them, brackets nothing. At the top, High also needs the company's own recent offers landing in line with the external anchor; two agreeing external points with nothing internal to test them stay Medium, because the band has not yet been seen hiring at that level.

## Borderline cases

- **Two offers that disagree by more than about 10%.** Look for a reason in the notes (one is a stretch hire, one is a different scope). If none, anchor on the median, tier Low, and say they conflict.
- **An offer outside the only comparable range.** Above the range: the market may have moved past what that employer posted; anchor on the offer, tier Low because the two disagree, say the range looks stale. Below the range: check level and date before trusting either.
- **Two ranges whose midpoints differ by more than about 10%.** Average the midpoints only if both are clean; tier Low and note the spread.
- **The earliest month the window keeps carries the verdict.** Keep it, and do not upgrade the tier on its strength alone; the month before it stays out however strong it looks.
- **The verdict changes depending on one excluded point.** That is exactly why the screen exists; do not let it back in. Mention it only if the reader would otherwise reach for it (for example a large public-company figure they have already seen).

- **A gap just inside the line (-4.6% to -5.0%).** Rounding should not decide a verdict on its own. When the anchor is a comparable accepted offer and the company's own recent accepted offers already land above the band midpoint, recruiters are paying past it to hire, so read the row as lagging and say why. When the anchor is a posted range, the gap rests on an advertisement and stays competitive under the posted-range cap, own offers or not; with no own offers, it stays competitive too. A gap of -4.5% or smaller is plainly competitive.

## Internal offers and declines

Your own accepted offers are where the band meets reality. If they cluster in the top third of the band, recruiters are already stretching to hire even when the midpoint gap looks small; say so. A declined offer is a floor: the candidate's alternative was worth more than that figure. What a candidate says about a competing offer ("their number was well above ours") is a claim, not data; mention the pattern, do not compute a market figure from it. Two or more declines near the same number, on a priority role, are a stronger signal than a modest midpoint gap and belong in the headline.

None of this creates a market reference or lifts a tier. A role with internal offers but no comparable external point is still deferred for position; you can report the internal pattern alongside the deferral.

## Band edges

Range penetration of an offer = (offer - band min) / (band max - band min).
- A comparable external offer at or above band max: the band cannot hire at market. Lead with it if the role is open.
- Internal offers above about 75% penetration: the band works only by spending its headroom on new hires, which squeezes existing staff (compression risk).
- Band midpoint well above market (beyond +5%): not automatically good news; check whether the role's level is mapped correctly before recommending anything, and note the cost.

## Position language

Use the four verdict labels tied to the computed gap: competitive, lagging, above-market, deferred; "materially lagging" is fine in prose beyond -10%. Do not soften a -8% to "roughly competitive", and do not call a deferred role competitive because nothing contradicts it. When the tier is Low, the position is a lean, and the sentence should sound like one ("the single comparable range points slightly above the band"). Against a lone range, also say where the band midpoint falls inside it: a midpoint in the range's lower third is where many hires land, so it reads as a lean behind rather than a clear lag.

## Calls that are easy to get wrong

- **Counting independence.** A range and an offer from the same employer are one voice, not two; count them once. A survey cut with a stated method and date is one point, however large its sample.
- **Own data on a thin pool.** With one external point, the company's offers cannot raise the tier: the read would then rest on what the company chose to pay. Referral and internal-transfer offers, and counteroffers made to keep staff, are not market tests at all.
- **Wide ranges.** A range spanning more than about 40% from min to max has a weak midpoint; say so in the cell.
- **Midpoint and max can disagree.** If offers clear the midpoint but stay under max, the band hires only by placing people high, so new hires start to out-earn tenured staff at the same level. Report the midpoint gap and the max coverage as two facts.
- **Stated midpoints.** Many bands are deliberately asymmetric (a midpoint set at the target percentile with a longer upper tail); recomputing it from min and max erases that design and shifts every gap. Use the snapshot's midpoint, and compute one only when none is given, saying so.
- **Claims are not points.** A manager's "we keep losing people on pay", an exit-interview remark, or one lost candidate's story is a question for the benchmark, never an input to it.
- **Snapshot gaps change thresholds.** A company that pays to lead the market (say a 65th-percentile target) reads a zero gap as lagging; one that lags by design reads a small negative gap as on target. Without the stated philosophy, say the verdict assumes a match-the-median stance. Pay data with no effective date may already trail a market that moved since.

## Deferral that still helps

A deferral should tell the reader what single piece of evidence would unlock a call: a same-stage offer in the anchor metro, the base/variable split for an OTE figure, the role's remote policy, a supplied exchange rate. If off-stage or off-metro points exist, one clause of context is fine; a number derived from them is not.

## Priority changes emphasis, not method

Priority-1 open roles go first in the narrative and get the concrete next step. The screen, the anchor and the tier rules are the same for every row; a role does not get looser evidence rules because it is urgent.

When you finish reading, start the memo itself: its opening characters are the bold words Evidence screen, and nothing about your reading or your plan comes before them.
