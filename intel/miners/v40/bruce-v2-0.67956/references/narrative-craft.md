# Narrative craft: making the benchmark read as advice

The screen carries the reasons, the table carries the numbers and the prose carries the decision. A reader who skips the screen should be able to stop after the lead's first two sentences and still know what to do about the open roles.

## Choosing the lead

Find the fact this particular company most needs, then say it with its number:
- An open priority role whose band trails, or whose band max sits below a clean offer.
- A pattern in the company's own hiring (offers landing high in the band, repeated declines).
- A role that looked fine on the raw pool but changes once non-comparable points are removed.
- Where every band is fine, say so plainly and name what deserves watching.

Avoid lines that could start any report ("Here is the benchmark table", "Based on the data provided"). Name the role and its number in the lead's first sentence, and give the screen's opening sentence this company's own stage, anchor and window.

## The table

One row per internal role, including deferred ones. Keep cells short: shorthand money ($166.4k), the anchor as ID, type, month and figure rather than a description, a deciding reason of a few words. The columns that earn their place: role and level, band midpoint, anchor with its basis (offer or range midpoint), gap, verdict, funnel, tier with reason. Add a column only if this company needs it (a band-max check for a hard-to-fill role, a currency column for a multi-country team); for an executive reader, fold the funnel into one closing line.

## Where caveats go

A caveat that applies to every row (all figures base salary, the window, the anchor metro, "ranges are aspirational") is said once, near the table. Row cells hold only what is specific to that row. Repeating the same warning on five rows hides the one row where the warning actually matters.

## Where exclusions go

Exclusions live in the evidence screen and nowhere else: one short line per point with its class and every failing reason, so a reader can audit any row in seconds. The funnel cell in each row carries counts only (retrieved, comparable, partial, dropped), and the prose never walks the list again. Keep screen lines terse: an ID, a type, a figure, a class and a reason, never a sentence of justification. The two or three exclusions that actually moved a verdict (an offer just outside the window, an OTE figure with no split that looks like the strongest evidence, a public-company range far above the rest) earn one clause in the lead or the next step, with their figures.

## Audience variants

- **Comp analyst or HR partner:** IDs inline, conversions shown, funnel complete, next steps operational (adjust midpoint, approve upper-third offers, gather a specific point).
- **Executive or board:** after a compact screen, decision and cost lead the prose, then a three- or four-row summary; IDs and funnel counts move to a short closing line. Translate gaps into what they mean for hiring speed or attrition.
- **Hiring manager:** the role they own first, what offer they can realistically make inside the band, what approval they need if not.
- **Recruiter:** where to land offers, which candidate claims to treat with caution, which evidence gap to fill from the next debrief.

## Length tactics

Aim for 650 to 950 words with the screen and the table; even an eight-role roster stays under 6,000 characters. When over: shorten the lead to two sentences, cut adjectives from tier reasons, merge caveat sentences, drop any sentence that restates a table cell, and keep the why-notes to the decisions a reader would actually question. Never drop a role row or a conversion step to save space.

## Closing

End on the next concrete action for the most urgent role or the single evidence gap that would change a verdict. Not a summary, not an offer to help further. Different situations produce different closings: a midpoint move, an approval rule for offers, a request for a base/variable split, a policy question about remote pay.

## Style notes

- Numbers from the input or computed from it, nothing remembered.
- Percentages to one decimal place; negative means the band is under the market reference.
- Plain verbs and labels: trails, matches, exceeds, cannot be called yet; competitive, lagging, leans behind, above-market, deferred.
- No heading lines: each part opens with a bold phrase inside its first sentence, and everything after that phrase is specific to this company.
