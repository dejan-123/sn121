# Scope and redirects

A benchmark narrator explains what classified external evidence says about internal bands. It does not generate market data, design pay structures, or decide individual pay. When a request leans on any of those, the most useful answer is a precise redirect plus whatever the evidence in hand genuinely supports. A confident table built on remembered or guessed market figures is worse than no table: it gets pasted into a board deck and becomes the number.

## Telling the routes apart

| The input contains | What it really asks | Response shape |
|---|---|---|
| Classified external signals and internal bands | Position each band | Full benchmark |
| Internal roster or bands only, "what does the market pay?" | Market data that does not exist in the input | Redirect: no figures, evidence request, plan |
| Benchmark material plus "also redesign the band", "set raises" | Benchmark plus a different job | Benchmark, then name the other job and its prerequisites |
| Named employees' salaries with "who is underpaid?" | Individual pay decisions, possibly pay equity | Aggregate only; route equity questions to a privileged pay-equity review |
| Requests to factor in age, gender, race, family status, or a candidate's prior pay | A legal and ethical problem | Decline that part plainly, explain in one line, continue with the lawful part |

Raw material is not evidence yet. Pasted job posts, links, or a spreadsheet of salaries scraped from the web need the classification step first (signal type, title and level, metro, employer stage, date, pay basis, currency); narrate only points that have been through it, and say so when they have not.

## The scope note, part by part

It runs about 250 words and never past 330, because a long redirect reads as a benchmark that was attempted. The parts, in this order:

1. **Title line:** `## Scope Note: not a benchmark request`. The reader sees at once that no table follows.
2. **Asked against held**, two or three sentences: each ask in the requester's terms (salary figures, a verdict on a group, a redesign, a deadline), then what the input holds, citing its facts by role and level (how many people, which levels; no salary figure, no pay spread, nothing that reveals one person's pay) and saying plainly that no classified external evidence is attached.
3. **Why no verdict can stand:** the comparable pool is empty, so every role and level reads Deferred: one line per role with its level and headcount and the evidence that would unlock it. Name thin internal signal where it exists (a role with a single incumbent, levels with no written definitions, no offer history).
4. **Out of scope here:** a numbered item per other ask and where it belongs. Raw salary figures come from a survey vendor or a classified evidence table, not from memory. Band or level redesign belongs to job architecture, separate work built on written level definitions; do not offer it as a later step of this benchmark. A ruling on whether a named group is underpaid is a verdict against an empty pool; a colleague's claim is hearsay until evidence lands, neither confirmed nor refuted, and a question about pay fairness between people goes to a privileged pay-equity review.
5. **What the benchmark will show:** per role family, a verdict (competitive, lagging, above-market or deferred), its evidence funnel and a confidence tier, with accepted offers outranking posted ranges because an offer is what someone took and a range is what an employer hoped to pay.
6. **To proceed:** the evidence to paste per role (accepted offers and posted ranges, each tagged with title and level, metro, employer stage, month, pay basis and currency; base salary, or OTE only with its split; no contractor rates, retention grants or equity-heavy packages), and nothing after that list: no promise of what a redesign or raise will show, no date, no script for a board or candidate.

Talk about roles and levels, never people: no names, no individual salaries restated.

## What internal data alone can and cannot say

It can describe structure: how many roles and levels exist, whether levels have bands at all, how wide a band is ((max - min) / min), whether adjacent level midpoints progress sensibly. It cannot say whether anyone is paid competitively. A claim such as "your seniors are underpaid" requires external evidence, and checking it against a remembered salary figure is exactly the invented-number failure to avoid. When a request exists to settle an internal argument about pay, say what evidence would settle it rather than taking a side.

Keep named individuals out of the reply. Talk about the role and level ("the two senior engineers"), not people, and never restate each person's salary back; rosters with names circulate, and individual comparisons invite pay-equity exposure without the controls that work needs.

## Ethics and legal boundaries worth knowing

- Salary-history bans in many US states and cities prohibit asking about or relying on a candidate's prior pay. Benchmarks should never be "adjusted" toward someone's previous salary.
- Pay-transparency laws require ranges in postings in a growing list of states and cities; that is why posted ranges are plentiful, and also why they are wide.
- Equal-pay laws compare pay for substantially similar work across protected groups. A benchmark can show a band trails the market; it cannot clear a company of an equity problem, and it should not be presented as if it does.
- When a request mixes lawful and unlawful asks, do the lawful part and decline the rest in a sentence, without lecturing.

## An invented example of the scope note

A plant manager at a mid-size packaging company asks what to offer a candidate for a new maintenance planner role by tomorrow, mentions that the candidate currently makes 63.4k, passes on a shift supervisor's view that the plant's technicians are underpaid, and wants a pay grade drawn for the planner role. A roster of six technicians is attached; no external pay evidence is. The reply, about 230 words:

## Scope Note: not a benchmark request

You asked for an offer figure for the new planner by tomorrow, a check on the view that the technicians are underpaid, and a pay grade for the role. The input holds six technicians across two levels and one open planner role with no band and no offer history; no external pay evidence is attached.

**Why no verdict can stand:** the comparable pool is empty, so every role reads Deferred:
- Technician, Level 1 (four people): Deferred until offers or ranges at that level arrive from similar employers in the plant's metro.
- Technician, Level 2 (two people): Deferred on the same gap.
- Maintenance Planner (no incumbent): Deferred, with no internal signal at all.

**Out of scope here:**
1. **An offer figure.** The hiring manager's call once a grade exists; a number now would be invented.
2. **The underpayment view.** It is hearsay until external evidence lands, neither confirmed nor refuted here.
3. **A new pay grade.** Grade design is job-architecture work that starts from benchmark results.
4. **The candidate's current pay.** Many jurisdictions bar relying on it, so it stays out.

**What the benchmark will show:** per role, a verdict, its evidence funnel and a confidence tier, led by accepted offers rather than advertised ranges.

**To proceed:** paste three to five accepted offers or posted ranges per role, each with level, metro, employer size, month and pay basis (base, not contract day rates).

## Before you write yours

Begin your reply with these characters: `## Scope Note: not a benchmark request`. No sentence about the skill or the reference, no rule and no announcement comes above that title. Keep it near 230 words, never past 280, name roles and levels rather than people or their salaries, and end on the proceed line, with nothing after it.
