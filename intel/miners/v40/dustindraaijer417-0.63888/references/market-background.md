# Market pay background for a new compensation analyst

These notes explain where pay data comes from and why practitioners trust some of it more than the rest. They are background for the benchmark method, not a substitute for it.

## Bands and what the midpoint means

A salary band sets the minimum, midpoint and maximum a company will pay for one level of one job family. The midpoint is the company's statement of where it wants to sit against the market for a fully competent person in the role; the spread around it (often 30 to 50 percent from minimum to maximum, wider at senior levels) leaves room for experience and growth. Compa-ratio, an individual's pay divided by the band midpoint, tells you where people sit inside the band; a cluster of recent hires near the maximum is the usual sign that the midpoint has fallen behind the market, because the company is buying talent at the top of a range it set for experienced staff.

Companies choose a market policy: lead (pay above the market to win scarce skills), match, or lag (pay below and compensate with equity, mission or flexibility). A benchmark measures where each band actually sits; whether that position is acceptable is a policy question for the people who own pay.

## Where market data comes from

- **Salary surveys** pool what participating employers report they pay. They are broad and methodical when the provider is named and the cut is clear, but the data is months old by publication, the participant mix shapes the answer, and a survey with no named source or method cannot be weighed at all.
- **Posted ranges** are what employers advertise. Pay-transparency laws in several US states and cities now require them, and employers comply by posting wide ranges that cover every level they might hire; where posting is voluntary, the employers who choose to post are often those proud of their pay. Either way a posting shows intent, not what was paid.
- **Accepted offers** are the closest thing to a market price: a candidate and an employer agreed on a number. They are scarce and noisy one at a time, but a few of them in the same metro, stage and level say more than many postings.
- **Recruiter figures** are useful for direction but reflect negotiation: a recruiter quoting a number is trying to move it.
- **Contractor rates** price a different engagement. The hourly rate carries the cost of benefits, payroll tax, unpaid gaps between engagements and the contractor's own overhead, so turning it into an annual salary overstates what an employee would earn.

## Why geography, stage and level must match

Pay varies more between metros than intuition suggests: two cities a few hours apart can differ by more than the tolerance a benchmark uses to call a band competitive. Companies handle remote work in one of two ways: they anchor remote roles to a metro's pay tier, or they pay a single national rate. Which policy the company follows decides whether a national posting tells you anything about its bands.

Company stage changes the shape of an offer. Early companies pay less cash and more equity; later ones pay more cash and grant smaller, more liquid equity. A base salary from a company two funding rounds away is part of a different bargain, which is why stage distance matters even within one city.

Titles are a weak guide to level. The same title means different scope at different companies, slash titles combine two jobs, and a senior manager title usually sits a level above a manager band. Matching on the content of the job, not its name, is the analyst's first discipline.

## Pay elements and a common basis

Total cash adds bonus or commission to base; on-target earnings for sales roles assume quota is met, and the split between base and variable varies widely by role and company. Sign-on and relocation money is paid once to close a hire, and retention or acquisition grants are paid once to keep someone through an event. None of these is the going base rate for the job, so benchmarks compare base to base and convert only when the components are stated.

## From a benchmark to a decision

A lagging band on a priority open role is the most urgent finding: the company is losing candidates now and its existing staff are underpaid against the people it is trying to hire, which turns a hiring problem into a retention problem. A competitive band that is already filling at its maximum is a warning that the next review will show a lag. A verdict resting on postings alone is a reason to gather offers before moving money, because budgets moved on advertised ranges tend to overshoot. Where the evidence cannot support a verdict, saying so, and naming the evidence that would settle it, protects the company from an expensive guess.
