# Worked benchmarks: one full reply, one note, eight short cases

Every company, person, source and figure below is invented for teaching. Take the reasoning and the form; none of the names or numbers belong in a real reply.

**The input, in short.** Kestrel Ledger, a Series C accounting-software company of 210 people headquartered in Minneapolis, with remote roles priced to the Minneapolis tier. Evidence retrieved 2026-04-06; the stated window says points before January 2025 are out. The input notes these postings are voluntary.

Sixteen external points, K-01 to K-16; fourteen come from one job-board feed, one from a recruiter's call, one from a survey snippet. The reply below says what each did.

# Kestrel Ledger compensation benchmark: the P1 security band lags, field marketing deferred

Basis: the Minneapolis tier, Series C, base salary, evidence dated January 2025 or later under the window stated for the 2026-04-06 retrieval. Of 16 points (K-01 to K-16), 9 survive (3 of them one stage away) and 7 are out. Senior Security Engineering, your P1 opening, lags: your midpoint sits 4.3% under the market, and both recent hires landed above the $165,900 midpoint. Supply Planning lags by 6.5% on a single posting, Account Executive and Technical Writing are competitive, and Field Marketing is deferred for want of a like-for-like point.

## What sits behind each call

**Security Engineering (Senior).** Two points match the role on every line: an accepted offer at $172,900 (K-02, January 2026) and a posting at $157,700–$190,100 (K-01, February 2026, midpoint $173,900). Averaged, they put the market at $173,400 and your midpoint 4.3% under it, and both recent hires needed more than the midpoint ($172,600 is $6,700 above it, $176,300 is $10,400 above), so I read the band as lagging. A Milwaukee posting (K-03) prices another metro and a recruiter's spoken figure (K-04) has nothing written behind it, so neither counts.

**Account Executive, Enterprise (Senior).** The one Series C offer was quoted at $189,800 with its $61,700 commission target named, which leaves $189,800 − $61,700 = $128,100 of base (K-06, December 2025), and your midpoint sits 1.6% over it, inside the competitive band, so that reading stands. A Series B posting with a $133,200 midpoint (K-07), a stage earlier, would move the anchor to $130,650 and the gap to −0.4%, competitive either way. Your two recent hires sit either side of the midpoint: $128,300 is $1,800 under it and $131,200 is $1,100 over. The OTE posting (K-05) gives no base split, so it cannot be compared at all.

**Supply Planning (Manager).** Only one posting survives, $112,300–$134,100 (K-09, midpoint $123,200), dated January 2025, the first month inside the window, and your midpoint sits 6.5% under it. There are no recent Supply Planning hires to test the call against, so one accepted offer should confirm it before the band moves. A posting from November 2024 (K-08) predates the window, and a contractor's $71 hourly rate (K-10) is not salary.

**Field Marketing (Senior Manager).** Nothing at Series C and this level survives; see below.

**Technical Writing (Senior).** Two Series C accepted offers count, at $109,300 (K-14) and $106,400 (K-15, listed as Senior Technical Writer / Documentation Lead, which includes your role). Their $107,850 average leaves the midpoint 2.5% under the market, competitive, and both offers sit above the midpoint, so the tier is High. An undated survey figure (K-16) is set aside, and the $108,200 decline gave no reason, so it says nothing about pay.

## Deferred verdicts

**Field Marketing (Senior Manager).** Both surviving points come from Series D, a stage later than yours, so neither is like-for-like and they cannot carry a verdict between them. The Series D posting (K-11) centres on $146,800 and the Series D offer (K-12) is $152,700; a Director posting (K-13) is a level up. What would settle it is one accepted offer or posting for a Senior Field Marketing Manager at a Series C company priced to Minneapolis, base salary, dated January 2025 or later.

## Signal caveats

- Two of the six points behind an anchor are posted ranges, which show what employers advertise; the four accepted offers show where pay cleared.
- Where a family has both, the posting midpoint sits above the offer, so postings run high in this pool and a lag resting on postings alone may read deeper than it is.
- Every posting is voluntary, per the input, so these are employers that chose to publish a range.
- All six anchor points come from one job-board feed, so any bias in that feed runs through every row.

---

How the arithmetic runs, for checking: Security (173,900 + 172,900) ÷ 2 = 173,400, and (165,900 − 173,400) ÷ 173,400 = −4.3%. Account Executive (130,100 − 128,100) ÷ 128,100 = +1.6%; with the adjacent posting, (128,100 + 133,200) ÷ 2 = 130,650 and −550 ÷ 130,650 = −0.4%. Supply Planning −8,000 ÷ 123,200 = −6.5%. Technical Writing (109,300 + 106,400) ÷ 2 = 107,850, and −2,650 ÷ 107,850 = −2.5%. Multiplying back is a quick test: 173,400 × 0.957 is about 165,900.

## Worked reply 2: a request that cannot be benchmarked

**The input, in short.** Tidewell Showrooms, a Series C furniture retailer of 150 people in Spokane. The finance lead writes that two showroom managers have asked for more, wants to know what showroom managers earn, and asks for a commission plan for them before the April pay run. Attached is a payroll extract with four managers' salaries and last year's commission; nothing comes from outside the company.

---

# Tidewell Showrooms compensation benchmark: not built yet, no external evidence classified

**What you asked for and what arrived.** You want market pay for showroom managers, a view on whether the two who asked are behind, and a commission plan before the April pay run. What arrived is four people with salary and last year's commission. That is individual pay rather than bands, and there is no external evidence beside it.

**Why there is no verdict yet.** A benchmark sets your band midpoints against market points that pass a like-for-like test on metro, stage, level, pay basis and date. With no points there is nothing to test. A typical manager salary from general knowledge would be a figure nobody can source, and that kind of number ends up in a pay conversation. So I can neither confirm nor rule out that the two managers are behind, and the extract alone cannot settle it.

**The commission plan.** Commission sits on top of a base, and the benchmark's anchor is what sets that base, so the plan follows it. Once the verdict exists, a draft plan can be built on it.

**What to send.**
- Points for showroom managers, base pay, from employers priced to Spokane at Series C first; a stage either side supports a call but cannot carry one.
- Accepted offers weigh most, since they show where pay cleared; a posted range shows what an employer advertises. Two like-for-like points, one of them an offer, make a firm call.
- For each point: source, type (accepted offer, posted range or survey), date, metro, company stage, title and level, and base pay with any commission or sign-on listed apart.
- Your own recent offers for this role, accepted or declined, with any reason a candidate gave; they test the managers' case most directly.
- Your band for the role: min, midpoint and max, and the metro you price to.
- The window to apply; without one, the last twelve months.

Until then the role is Deferred: no external points and no band. Once the data arrives, the benchmark can be ready before the April pay run.

## Short case A: a band in another currency

A Series C company based in Calgary bands its Field Service (Level 3) team in Singapore at S$91,300 / S$101,200 / S$112,400. The pool holds a Singapore Series C posting at S$97,300–S$110,900 (mid S$104,100), the same posting again from a second job board, and a Sydney offer in Australian dollars. The repeat counts once. The Sydney point fails on metro before currency even arises, and with no exchange rate in the input it could not be converted anyway. The gap is (101,200 − 104,100) ÷ 104,100 = −2.8%: Competitive, Low, on a single posting. The row stays in Singapore dollars.

## Short case B: a national range, kept for one role and out for another

A company prices its Solutions roles nationally and its Operations roles to Atlanta. For Solutions Architecture (Level 5), midpoint $171,600, a national offer at $172,900 and a remote-US posting centred on $174,100 both fit: the anchor is $173,500 and the gap −1.1%, Competitive, Medium: one offer, one posting. For Workplace Operations (Lead), midpoint $88,400, an Atlanta posting centred on $90,100 is kept and a remote-US range centred on $84,700 stays out, because that band is priced to a metro: gap −1.9%, Competitive, Low. The paragraph adds a clause: with the national range in, the anchor would be $87,400 and the gap +1.1%, so the call stands either way. An input that leaves open which roles are national is read like the second case.

## Short case C: a small gap with no hiring signal

A Quality Engineering (Senior) band has a $120,300 midpoint. Two like-for-like postings centre on $123,200 and $126,400, so the anchor is $124,800 and the gap is −4,500 ÷ 124,800 = −3.6%. No recent hires, no declines. The market data alone cannot separate competitive from lagging here, and there is no hiring evidence to settle it, so the call is Competitive at the low edge, Low: postings only, nothing from your own hiring behind them. One hire that needs more than the midpoint would tip it.

## Short case D: one exclusion decides the call

A Release Engineering (Senior) band has a $150,300 midpoint and the company's last two hires came in at $153,700 and $156,400. Kept: a like-for-like offer at $156,900 and a posting centred on $158,300, so the anchor is $157,600 and the gap is −7,300 ÷ 157,600 = −4.6%, decided as Lagging by the hires above the midpoint. A third posting centred on $150,700 is dated before the window. The paragraph behind the call adds one clause: kept, it would pull the anchor to $156,900 and the gap to −4.2%, still Lagging, so the call does not hang on it. Had it flipped the verdict, the clause would say that instead, because the reader then knows the exclusion is carrying the call.

## Short case E: two levels, one of them unbanded

A snapshot lists Embedded Software at Senior (band $138,200 / $151,300 / $164,700) and at Principal with "band under review" and three salaries. The Senior row is benchmarked as usual. The Principal row stays in the table as Deferred, "no band supplied", even though the pool holds two Principal postings: there is no internal midpoint to compare them with, and an average of three salaries is not a band. The deferred section names what would settle it, the Principal band's min, midpoint and max, and notes the postings are ready to use once it exists. No Principal posting is used for the Senior row either.

## Short case F: hires above the band's top

A Data Governance (Manager) band runs $112,300 / $124,200 / $136,400. The company's last two hires were $139,300 and $141,200, both above the maximum. Kept: two like-for-like offers at $136,700 and $138,100, so the anchor is $137,400 and the gap is −13,200 ÷ 137,400 = −9.6%: Lagging, High. The paragraph behind the call adds that recent hires already sit above the band's maximum, so the band has stopped covering the market for this role; that is a structure problem for later, named once, not redesigned here.

## Short case G: an adjacent point cannot overturn a clear reading

A Customer Education (Senior) band has a $110,300 midpoint. One like-for-like offer came in at $112,200, so the gap is −1,900 ÷ 112,200 = −1.7%: inside the competitive band, a clear reading. A posting a stage later centres on $121,600; with it the anchor would be $116,900 and the gap −6,600 ÷ 116,900 = −5.6%. A later-round company usually pays more, and an adjacent point settles near calls only, so the call stays Competitive, Low: one offer. The funnel adds "with adjacent: −5.6%", so the reader sees how far the later-stage posting sits. Had the offer alone read −4%, the posting would have settled it.

## Short case H: titles read word by word

A Depot Senior Supervisor posting does not match a Depot (Supervisor) band: the extra rank word makes it another level, and that is the first reason recorded, ahead of any date or source problem. A Lead Customs Adviser offer does match a Customs & Trade (Lead Adviser) family, because the family's own name covers the title; it becomes adjacent only on a line the input itself flags.

## Before sending

- Every figure in the reply is in the input or computed from input figures in view; no market number comes from memory, and none of the invented ones above appear.
- Each point is counted once, in one row, with one reason; in = kept + out, and the rows add up to the pool in the opening.
- A conversion uses only the input's own numbers, and subtracts what was folded in (a bonus, a commission target) rather than adding it.
- The window boundary is read as written: the named month is inside.
- No verdict rests on adjacent-stage points alone, and a deferred family has no verdict anywhere in the reply.
- Each gap is checked by multiplying back: anchor × (1 + gap) is about the midpoint.
- A pool-wide caveat appears once, under Signal caveats, never in a row, and its direction is read from this pool's own figures.
- Each gap sentence makes your midpoint the subject ("your midpoint sits 4.3% under the market"), because the anchor is what the gap divides by.
- Every own hire, decline and "no recent offers" in the snapshot is accounted for in its family's paragraph.
- Each own hire is written with its distance from the midpoint ("$6,700 above it"), and each anchor with its figures in order.
- A point one stage away is treated the same way in every family, and the tier counts like-for-like points only.
- A flip clause appears only for a point that failed one line, never for one that failed two.
- No skill wording reaches the reply: no lines, zones or edges, no "joins as adjacent", no "consistent with, not counted".
- The word count is in range; a point's metro or stage is written out only where it differs from the basis.
- A band in another currency stays in that currency.

The reader receives your reply exactly as written, from its first character: it starts with `# ` and the company name. A sentence about this skill, these notes or what you are about to do would land in their report as well.
