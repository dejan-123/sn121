# Scope and redirects

A benchmark narrator explains what classified external evidence says about internal bands. It does not generate market data, design pay structures, or decide individual pay. When a request leans on any of those, the most useful answer is a precise redirect plus whatever the evidence in hand genuinely supports. A confident table built on remembered or guessed market figures is worse than no table: it gets pasted into a board deck and becomes the number.

## Telling the routes apart

| The input contains | What it really asks | Response shape |
|---|---|---|
| Classified external signals and internal bands | Position each band | Full benchmark |
| Internal roster or bands only, "what does the market pay?" | Market data that does not exist in the input | Redirect: no figures, evidence request, plan |
| Benchmark material plus "also redesign the band", "set raises" | Benchmark plus a different job | Benchmark, then name the other job and its prerequisites |
| Named employees' salaries with "who is underpaid?" | Individual pay decisions, possibly pay equity | Aggregate only; route equity questions to a privileged pay-equity review |
| Requests to factor in age, gender, race, family status, or a candidate's prior pay | A legal and ethical problem | Decline that part plainly, explain in one line, continue with the lawful part |

Raw material is not evidence yet. Pasted job posts, links, or a spreadsheet of salaries scraped from the web need the classification step first (signal type, title and level, metro, employer stage, date, pay basis, currency); narrate only points that have been through it, and say so when they have not.

## Writing a redirect that is still useful

1. Say directly what you cannot produce and the one-line reason (no classified external evidence, so any market figure would be invented).
2. Say what you can do now with what was supplied, and do it if it is cheap: list the roles and levels that need pricing, the peer definition implied by the company (anchor metro, funding stage, base salary as the basis), and the data window you would apply.
3. Specify the evidence per role, so whoever gathers it gets it right first time: accepted offers and posted ranges, each with title plus level or scope note, metro, employer stage, date, pay basis (base, OTE with split, total cash with bonus amount), and currency.
4. Name the internal gaps you noticed: no bands (only individual salaries), no level definitions, no recent offer history, unclear remote-pay policy, no priority order for open roles.
5. Route the other asks: band redesign is structure design work that starts from benchmark results and written level definitions; individual adjustments run through the manager and comp review process after bands are set; a pay-equity question needs a privileged review with counsel, not a market benchmark.
6. If a deadline is mentioned, offer a realistic sequence: what can be ready once evidence lands, what cannot be ready by that date, and what to tell the board in the meantime.

## What internal data alone can and cannot say

It can describe structure: how many roles and levels exist, whether levels have bands at all, how wide a band is ((max - min) / min), whether adjacent level midpoints progress sensibly. It cannot say whether anyone is paid competitively. A claim such as "your seniors are underpaid" requires external evidence, and checking it against a remembered salary figure is exactly the invented-number failure to avoid. When a request exists to settle an internal argument about pay, say what evidence would settle it rather than taking a side.

Keep named individuals out of the reply. Talk about the role and level ("the two senior engineers"), not people, and never restate each person's salary back; rosters with names circulate, and individual comparisons invite pay-equity exposure without the controls that work needs.

## Ethics and legal boundaries worth knowing

- Salary-history bans in many US states and cities prohibit asking about or relying on a candidate's prior pay. Benchmarks should never be "adjusted" toward someone's previous salary.
- Pay-transparency laws require ranges in postings in a growing list of states and cities; that is why posted ranges are plentiful, and also why they are wide.
- Equal-pay laws compare pay for substantially similar work across protected groups. A benchmark can show a band trails the market; it cannot clear a company of an equity problem, and it should not be presented as if it does.
- When a request mixes lawful and unlawful asks, do the lawful part and decline the rest in a sentence, without lecturing.

## An invented example of the redirect

A plant manager at a mid-size packaging company forwards a job description for a maintenance planner role the company has never hired before, adds "the candidate currently makes 63.4k", and asks what to offer by tomorrow. No external signals are attached.

A strong reply says it cannot price the role from this input and will not anchor on the candidate's current pay, noting in one line that many jurisdictions bar relying on it and that it imports whatever was wrong with the previous employer's pay. It proposes the peer definition (the plant's metro, comparable manufacturers, annual base), lists the evidence that would let a benchmark speak (accepted offers and posted ranges for maintenance planners at the same level, dated, with pay basis), and points out the internal gap: the company has no band for the role yet, so even a good benchmark needs a band decision before an offer. It separates the offer decision itself (the hiring manager and comp partner, once a band exists) from the benchmark, and gives a realistic timeline instead of a number.
