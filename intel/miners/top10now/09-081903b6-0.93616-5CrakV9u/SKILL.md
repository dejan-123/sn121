# Who moves an enterprise deal, and how to tell

An account record is full of titles, and titles mislead. The person who decides a purchase is rarely the one with the grandest job name; it is the one whose actions keep the evaluation alive, plus whoever holds the budget line above the deal's size. A useful map separates three things the record mixes together: the seat a person holds, the part they are playing in this evaluation, and the state of our relationship with them today.

## Evidence that counts

Weigh each contact by what they have done, from strongest to weakest evidence. Strongest: they arranged a meeting with someone else, they built something internal for us (a business case, a briefing, a budget request), they shared data we did not ask for, they took our material to their own leadership. Middle: they ask detailed questions, send follow-ups, answer quickly, confirm requirements. Weakest: they attended, they were copied, they said something positive once. A single warm conversation followed by silence is the weakest kind of signal, even from a chief officer.

Three readings experienced sellers make that a newcomer misses:

- **The spending line decides the buyer.** If a contact names the largest amount they can sign and the deal is larger, the buyer is the person above that line. An eager champion below the line is still essential, but as the route to the buyer, not as the buyer. Where nobody has stated a limit, go by what the record says (a champion naming whose sign-off the deal is waiting for is evidence), do not spend a move asking people what their limit is, and do not appoint a buyer or signer from a title: a finance head whose team has to accept the cost is a gate, not the signer, and when the record only says that leadership will want a final look, the signer is unknown, which is a gap to close through the champion.
- **A delegated deal has a messenger.** When an executive hands the project to a colleague ("you own it from here"), that colleague becomes the channel to her, and her own silence afterwards is expected, not a warning about the colleague.
- **A finished review stays finished.** If a reviewer wrote that their requirement is met, that gate is closed even if an older stage label still calls it pending. On a small purchase, a narrow check the checker has already called fine is finished too, and her not writing again afterwards is no signal: she has nothing left to do.

## Relationship strength, 0 to 5

Score the relationship as it stands now:

- **5**: drives the evaluation: started the contact or keeps it moving, answers fast, and has already introduced us to colleagues or built internal material for us. Waiting for someone else's approval does not take this score away.
- **4**: engaged and responsive, but nothing of theirs has yet moved anyone else; untested. A promise to push that produced nothing belongs here, with direction "stalling".
- **3**: solid inside their own lane, such as a technical reviewer who answers and follows up.
- **2**: thin: one good meeting, a procedural exchange, a narrow requirement already checked off, or warmth that has gone quiet (one answer to several of our emails, weeks without a word), whatever the title.
- **1**: cold: copied, present but silent, our messages unanswered.
- **0**: no contact at all.

Compare what comes back with what we send: many of our emails against one short reply means the relationship lives on our side only, and the evidence cell should show those numbers when the record gives them. Where the record gives no counts, describe acts and dates instead of inventing figures; where it gives no "today", quote the date of the last act and never work out how many days or months ago it was.

## What is missing depends on the deal

Gaps are the people or confirmations this particular deal still needs, judged from its size, stage and authority lines. Large deals above a champion's limit need the person who signs; deals with a named blocker need the person who can clear it. A small team purchase that sits inside one buyer's own limit, with no procurement or legal step mentioned, has essentially no gaps: write that, add that one relationship is normal at that size, and do not go looking for finance, legal, security or executive contacts, or for more contacts inside the team. A record that says no procurement or legal step has come up has answered the question. Roles mentioned without a name (a chief executive known only by title, "our head of engineering", unnamed people at a demo) are gaps to close through whoever mentioned them; they never get invented names and never get a row. On a large deal at mid or late stage, name what the record leaves open (who signs, finance, procurement, security, legal: whichever it has not shown) as gates still to come and roles to ask the champion about, never as contacts. If every line to the people who decide runs through one person, even when a few other names are in the record, the deal is single-threaded on that person: say so with that word, first among what is missing.

## Order of moves

The first move is whatever unblocks the deal soonest. Look in this order: something we owe them (an unanswered email of theirs, a document they asked for); the condition a decision-maker or our champion has named; a short session with whoever signs, which the person who has access is asked to set up and lead within a stated time; then broadening the contacts, starting with an introduction to people the record itself says sat in a meeting unintroduced (never people the record does not mention). Check who wrote last: when the latest message is theirs and unanswered, the silence is ours, the map says so, and answering it is the first move. That answer should carry what the decision-maker still needs and offer a short meeting the champion hosts; "any update?" moves nothing. Never write directly to a silent buyer or to an executive who has gone quiet, not even a short courtesy note: ask the person they trust whether and how to bring them back in, with a reason drawn from their own stated concern. Working contacts are different: a procurement, security or technical contact who has told us what they need is answered by us, directly, with the finished document; only silent executives and people we have never met are approached through someone else. A reviewer who has stopped answering is not chased with another message either: go through the active colleague who works closest to them. Attribute each concern to the person who raised it. Skip moves that only confirm what nothing in the record doubts.

## Sponsor

A sponsor is an executive with authority over the deal who is acting for it now. A champion without that authority is not a sponsor, and neither is an executive who endorsed the project once and then went quiet: she is the candidate. If nobody qualifies, say there is no active sponsor today, name the candidate, and say through whom to reach her. On a small deal the buyer is the sponsor.

## Requests this skill does not answer

A request for a reporting-line chart, a drafted email or a forecast is not a map request, and neither is a list of names with no activity behind them. Answer the seller directly, in about 200 words: open with one plain sentence saying what was asked and that you have not built it from this input; say in a sentence or two what that artefact needs and where it comes from (reporting lines and influence are not visible in titles); then say what you can build instead and why it serves this deal better (who can sign at this size, who is driving, who can block, where coverage is missing, the next move); and end with a short list of exactly what to paste (meeting notes with dates and attendees, email activity per person, any approval limits people have stated, the gates still open). Rank nobody, guess no hierarchy, and single out no one as the likely champion.

## Writing the map

First read references/field-notes.md, on its own, once. It contains a worked map and three short cases. Then send the map as your reply and nothing else: read the record silently, and let the reply's first characters be "**Stakeholder Map —" (for a request this skill does not answer, the plain opening sentence of the redirect). No analysis block, no remark about the skill or its instructions, and no "---" line comes before it.

The map is a briefing note to the seller, written to them directly ("you", "your champion") in a colleague's voice. Only the title line "**Stakeholder Map — <Account> | <deal value>, <stage>**" is bold; nothing after it carries a bold label. It runs in this order: an opening paragraph (who moves the deal, what holds it up, the fact that overturns the obvious reading, what delay costs) that hands over to the table; the stakeholder table with columns Name | Title | Role in this deal | Strength (0–5) | Direction | What they have done, for named people only, one row each (a row needs a person's name, so "the COO" or "the managing director" is never a row, even when she signs); one sentence saying which roles have no named person and therefore no row; a paragraph on what is still missing for a deal this size; a line in your own voice saying in what order you would work and why, then numbered moves in full sentences, each naming the person, the signal behind it and what it waits on; and a last paragraph on the executive sponsor, argued from the record like advice from a colleague, not issued as orders. Nothing follows that paragraph.
