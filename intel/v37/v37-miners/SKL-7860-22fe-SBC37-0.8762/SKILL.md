# Committee memo: write it so the usual failures cannot happen

A diligence pack is built by people who each saw one slice of the deal. One analyst tiers and labels the risks, another writes up the numbers, a third pulls multiples. Memos go wrong when they trust one slice where the slices disagree. These failures are predictable, and each one is prevented by a single habit. Follow the seven guards below and the verdict, the range and the conditions will follow from them.

## The guards

**1. A label is not a closure.** The failure is copying the status column. The habit: for every top-tier finding, look for what proves it is closed, such as a signed consent, a regulator's closure letter, an awarded contract or an audited figure. None of these is a closure: a deferral, counsel's assurance that a matter is essentially settled, a contract out for re-tender, a pending application, or a fact that is simply disclosed (customers with no term commitment). When the label and the evidence disagree, say so in the risk table and in the recommendation ("labelled resolved; the narrative shows X; treated as open"). Do not re-tier silently. If a finding looks under-tiered, for example an injunction threat over a core module filed as manageable, say so and treat it as a gating item for further diligence.

**2. The worst open finding sets the ceiling on the vote.** The failure is approving because the business looks attractive. The habit: the highest-tier finding that is still open on the evidence sets the most the committee can approve. If any gating finding is open, there is no unconditional approval. The call then becomes one of two things. Approve subject to conditions precedent when each blocker has a defined, checkable event that can close it before signing. Decline or defer at current terms when no buyer action can close the blocker, or when it strikes at the very thing the thesis is buying. The reverse failure is just as real. When every gating item is genuinely closed and only manageable risks remain, approve and price those risks rather than inventing a no-go.

**3. Value the business you actually buy.** The failure is multiplying the seller's number. The habit: rebuild the base from the narrative. Strip out one-off project revenue, income that will not transfer to this buyer, pass-through volume reported gross, run-rate from customers already lost, add-backs that are really recurring spend, and revenue restated under a policy no auditor has signed. Show the bridge: the reported figure, each deduction with its source, and the resulting diligence base. If earnings are negative after honest adjustment, earnings multiples cannot be applied; say why and value on revenue.

**4. A comp set is a choice you must defend.** The failure is averaging the whole table. The habit has four steps:
- **Match the multiple to the base.** Value a net-of-costs base with a net multiple, and a recurring base with a recurring multiple.
- **Exclude by a stated rule.** For example, drop control-premium acquisitions mixed into trading comps, hypergrowth outliers, and rows showing NM for the multiple in use. Name every row you keep and every row you drop.
- **Position the target against the comps you keep.** Compare growth, margin, retention and scale, and say which way each pulls. Below-peer growth and margin point to the lower half; superior retention points upward.
- **Flag non-comparability.** Where the pack warns that comps are not like-for-like (for example, revenue mix not broken out), say how much that lowers confidence.

Write the range as low multiple × base to high multiple × base, with the products shown, so that anyone can reproduce it. Cross-check with a second multiple when the data supports one.

**5. Measure the ask on the same basis.** The failure is writing "the ask looks rich" and stopping there. The habit: divide the ask by the diligence base to get its implied multiple, set that against the selected range, and state the gap in dollars and percent. If the seller quotes the ask on a different base, restate it on yours.

**6. A condition is a closing event, not a wish.** The failure is "complete confirmatory diligence". The habit: each condition names the finding it closes, the evidence or counterparty that closes it, and when that must happen relative to signing or close. It also says what follows if it fails: a walk-away, a price reset, an escrow or a specific indemnity, sized from the input wherever the input gives a figure. Manageable risks get covenants, holdbacks or a 100-day plan, not conditions precedent.

**7. Silence is a finding.** The failure is filling gaps with plausible numbers. The habit: use only the figures, dates and names the inputs contain. Where the pack does not quantify an exposure, give a deadline or name an owner, say so plainly and make getting that information a condition. Derived arithmetic (sums, percentages, implied multiples) is welcome and should be shown. Invented inputs are not.

The thesis ties the guards together. It says why the asset is worth owning and names the one risk most able to break that reason. That risk must head the risk table and drive the recommendation.

## Writing the memo

Output only the finished memo: no preamble, no restated instructions, no working notes. Open with a short decision paragraph of two or three sentences giving the recommendation, the defended range against the ask, and the one thing that must happen. Then write five numbered sections:

1. **Thesis**: 120–200 words of prose, naming the primary risk.
2. **Risk table**: columns for finding (brief), tier as given, status as labelled, status on the evidence, and consequence (blocks the vote / moves the price / structure / post-close). Follow it with a few sentences on any contradictions.
3. **Valuation**: the base bridge, the comp table with inclusions and exclusions and the reasons, the target's positioning, the range arithmetic and the ask comparison. 250–450 words including tables.
4. **Recommendation**: the verdict first, then the reasons that carry it, then honest caveats on what would change it. 100–200 words.
5. **Conditions**: numbered, each tied to a named finding.

Length bounds stated in the request override these.

Write like a senior deal professional. Let the reasoning carry the memo and cite the input's specifics in every paragraph. Never title paragraphs after the guards, and never reuse one sentence frame across findings; each finding gets the words its own facts need. Before finishing, check every number against the input and recompute every product in the range.

## Miniature (invented)

A harbour-scheduling software vendor reports $26.0M of LTM revenue, which includes $3.0M of one-off hardware resale. The base is therefore $23.0M of recurring revenue, growing 15%. Its top-tier finding is a data licence from the port authority, labelled resolved, but the narrative shows only an unsigned draft extension, so it is treated as open.

The comp table has five rows. A strategic take-private at 7.9x is excluded as a control premium. The four survivors span 4.1x–5.3x with median growth of 21%. Slower growth puts the target at 4.1x–4.7x, which gives 4.1 × 23.0 = $94.3M up to 4.7 × 23.0 = $108.1M. The $121M ask implies 5.26x recurring revenue, about 12% above the top of the range.

The memo opens: "Do not approve at the ask. Authorise a revised indication up to $108M, with the executed licence extension as a condition precedent to signing. Without it, the thesis's core data feed is not secured." The conditions are:

1. The executed licence extension before signing; walk away if the port authority refuses.
2. The price reset to the recurring base.
3. The hardware resale excluded from any earn-out baseline.
