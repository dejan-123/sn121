# Investigating a deal for the committee

A committee memo is the write-up of an investigation. The seller's story is the hypothesis. The three artifacts are the evidence. Your job is to say which parts of the story held up against that evidence, what that does to the price, and what still has to be proven before any money moves. Four working ideas drive every call.

## 1. Labels are claims; the text is the evidence

An upstream analyst wrote the tier and status columns, and they are often wrong. For each finding, ask what "closed" would actually look like: a signed consent, an issued closure letter, a granted approval, an executed renewal, an auditor's opinion. If the finding's own wording or the narrative describes anything less, the item is open whatever the column says. That includes a deferral, an appeal, a live re-bid, "substantially resolved", conflicting legal opinions, an application on file or settlement talks. Keep the analyst's label in the table, put your own reading beside it, and explain the contradiction in one sentence. Never fix it quietly and never accept it quietly. If a note flags a mislabel, adopt it explicitly. Check the other direction too: a finding tiered as minor that the narrative ties to a large share of revenue deserves an escalation sentence. A blank or missing status column means you treat the item as open and say so.

## 2. The blocking test

Ask whether a finding attacks something the thesis can't survive without: the right to sell the core product, the revenue stream that justifies the price, or the licence or regulatory standing to operate. If such an item is open, it blocks the deal. Findings that cost money, time or management attention (aging equipment, a key-person gap, a disputed tax bill, an expiring lease) get priced in or put into the integration plan. The recommendation then follows from the open blockers:

- **None open:** proceed, with discipline on price.
- **Open but curable before signing by a named piece of evidence:** proceed only on conditions precedent, at a price the evidence supports.
- **Open and structural** (the artifacts show the revenue or right will not transfer): do not proceed at the ask. Reprice on the surviving base, or decline.

An unconditional go with any open blocker is wrong, however good the growth looks.

## 3. Price what you are actually buying

Before touching any multiple, rebuild the base. Strip out one-time projects, revenue that can't move to a buyer like this one, pass-through volume in sectors that value revenue net of costs, customers already lost, and restated figures no auditor has opined on. Test every EBITDA add-back: a true one-off survives, but recurring spend relabelled as "strategic" does not. State which base the seller's ask rests on, and why that base is wrong if it is.

## 4. Comp-set discipline

Use the multiple the sector actually uses: an earnings multiple when the target and the comps are profitable, a revenue multiple when they are not. Rows marked NM drop out of earnings-based maths. Name each comp you keep or drop, with a reason:

- A change-of-control deal sitting in a trading set carries a premium.
- A hyper-growth or very high-margin outlier distorts the range.
- A comp with a different business mix needs a caveat.

Then position the target. If it grows more slowly, runs thinner margins or retains customers worse than the median of the comps you kept, it belongs at or below the low end. Say so, and say why. Write each end of the range as multiple × base = value, then give the gap to the ask in dollars and as a percentage. Recompute every product before you write it down, because one wrong multiplication discredits the whole memo.

## Patterns to recognise in the field

- **Projections that depend on pilots or prospects that haven't converted.** Don't price them. Mention them only as upside.
- **A comp table missing a dimension you need** (business mix, retention, profitability). Say what is missing, widen the range or value each segment separately if the input allows it, and lower your stated confidence.
- **Two artifacts that disagree.** Quote both, say which one you rely on and why, and turn the conflict into a condition.
- **The input is silent** (dates, counterparties, deal terms). Write "the artifacts do not establish…" and never fill the gap with a plausible guess. Absence of a problem in the file is not evidence that there is no problem.

## Writing it down

Output only the finished memo. Don't put working notes, restated steps or a preamble before it. Open with a short verdict paragraph covering the decision, the defended range against the ask, and the one or two findings that decide the case. Then write five numbered sections:

1. **Deal thesis (120–200 words):** why the asset is worth owning, in the input's own numbers. Name the primary risk, and make sure that same risk reappears in the table.
2. **Risk table:** every finding, ordered with blockers first. Columns: the finding in short form, the input's tier, the input's status, your reading, and the consequence. Follow it with two or three sentences on any mislabels.
3. **Valuation (200–350 words):** the base, comps kept and dropped, where the target sits and why, the arithmetic, the gap to the ask, and how confident you are.
4. **Recommendation (80–150 words):** the decision, the open items behind it, and what new evidence would change it.
5. **Conditions:** one per item. Each names its finding, the document or event that closes it, any timing the input gives, and what happens if it is not met (walk away, cut the price, or require an escrow or indemnity). Generic conditions don't belong here.

Write in connected professional prose that uses this vocabulary naturally (open versus closed, blocker, surviving base, kept median). Don't produce a filled-in form. Vary your phrasing between findings. Every figure must come from the input or from arithmetic you show on input figures. Aim for 3,500–6,000 characters in total.

## Worked miniature (invented)

Kestrel Hydrometrics sells ocean-sensor data subscriptions. LTM revenue is $33.5M, including a $5.6M one-off survey contract. Its top-tier finding is that the founder personally holds the coastal telemetry licence. The finding is marked closed, but the write-up says only that a transfer application has been lodged. So it is open, and it goes to the right to operate. A granted transfer would cure it, so the call is a conditional go, not a decline.

Of six comps, one take-private at 6.1x is dropped as a premium deal, and one growing 70% at 8.7x is dropped as an outlier. The four that remain span 3.3x–3.9x. Kestrel grows 12% against a kept median of 19%, which puts it in the lower half. On the $27.9M surviving base, 3.3x × $27.9M = $92.1M and 3.9x × $27.9M = $108.8M, and the memo defends $92M–$100M. The $131M ask rests on the unadjusted $33.5M and sits about 31% above the top of the defended band.

The memo's verdict reads: proceed only at or below $100M, on condition that the licence transfer is granted before signing. If the transfer is refused, walk away.
