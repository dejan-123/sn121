# Writing the IC memo for the person who has to vote

The reader is a committee member with ten minutes and one question: do we approve, at what price, on what terms, and what would make us walk away? Every sentence either moves that vote or should go. Open with the decision. Then give the two or three facts that carry it, then the valuation that sets the price, then what is still uncertain and how the conditions deal with it.

Output only the finished memo. Do not print working notes, restate these instructions, or describe your steps first. Use only figures, names and dates found in the input. You may derive numbers (sums, medians, products) if you show the inputs. Where the input is silent, say what is not known and who must supply it. Never fill the gap with a plausible guess.

## Four ideas that drive every call

**A status is a claim, and the narrative is the evidence.** Analysts label findings "resolved" when counsel got a deferral, when management says it will be fine, or when the issue was just recorded. Check each label against the finding's own wording and against the financials write-up. Treat anything still running as open: a review, a re-bid, an appeal, a lapsed certificate, an unsigned consent, an auditor who has not given an opinion. Also check the tiers. A lower-tier item that could stop the core product or cut most of the revenue is under-tiered. A low-tier metric such as shrinking retention still belongs in the price. If you disagree with the table, say so in the memo and give your view. Never quietly correct it.

**The gate decides the verdict before anything else.** If any top-tier finding is open on the evidence, the deal cannot be approved outright. If it can be closed before signing with something definite (a consent letter, a closure letter, a reissued certificate, an audit opinion), the verdict is approval subject to that condition. If it threatens the business itself and the evidence does not exist yet (a regulator's decision, revenue that cannot legally transfer, a contract out for re-bid), defer or decline. Also re-price if the ask is above your range. Use the same verdict word in the thesis, the recommendation and the conditions.

**Value is a cleaned base times a cleaned multiple.** First choose the base the buyer is actually acquiring:
- recurring revenue rather than revenue that includes one-off projects;
- for pass-through models, revenue net of what is passed through, not gross volume;
- run-rate after customers already lost;
- excluding grants or contracts that cannot move to a new owner;
- excluding upward restatements the auditors have not signed off.

Next, clean the comp set. Drop deals priced with a control premium and drop multiples marked NM. Treat outliers far above the target's growth or margins with caution. Name every exclusion and give the reason. Take the median of the remaining comps, then move it down or up for the target's growth, margins, size and quality versus those comps, and say why. Show the arithmetic, e.g. "2.4x–2.8x on 30.0M recurring gives 72.0M–84.0M". Check every product twice. Use an EBITDA multiple as a cross-check only when EBITDA is positive and the add-backs genuinely do not recur. Do not accept severance or "strategic" spend as one-off without proof. Finally, set the range against the ask and say plainly whether the gap is negotiable.

**A condition is the key to one named lock.** Each condition names the finding it resolves, the specific evidence that closes it, when it must be met (before signing, before close, or after close with protection), and what happens if it is not met: walk away, cut the price, escrow, or a specific indemnity. Size protections from the input's own figures, for example an estimate of fines or the revenue at risk. If the input gives no figure, say counsel must size it. Generic conditions such as "complete legal diligence" add nothing.

## Procedure (do this silently; print only the memo)

1. List each finding with its tier, its label and your own view of its status, based on the evidence.
   - Note every place where the table and the narrative disagree.
   - Note anything the input leaves out: a missing status, no comp breakdown, no lease terms.
2. Apply the gate. Pick the verdict from the ladder above. Pick the single primary risk: the open finding most likely to destroy value.
3. Build the base and the comp set. Compute the range. Compare it with the ask.
4. Write one condition per open top-tier finding. Add conditions for open lower-tier items only where they affect value or the ability to close.
5. Check consistency. The primary risk in the thesis is the first row of the risk table. The verdict matches the gate. The valuation uses the cleaned base. Every number appears in the input or follows from it by arithmetic you have shown.

## Shape of the memo

If the request states length limits, follow them exactly. Otherwise keep the whole memo to about 2,500–5,000 characters, in five numbered sections, opened by a two-to-three-sentence decision line: the verdict, the defended range against the ask, and the condition it depends on.

1. **Thesis** (≤150 words, prose). Explain why this asset is worth owning, using the input's growth, margin and retention facts. Name the primary risk in the same paragraph.
2. **Risk table.** One row per finding, ordered by real severity rather than the order given in the input. Columns: finding (short), tier as given, status as given, your assessment. Assessments differ row by row. One or two sentences after the table name any re-tiering or contradiction.
3. **Valuation** (≤250 words, prose with the arithmetic). Cover the base and why it was chosen, the comps kept and excluded with reasons, the adjustments, the range, and the comparison with the ask.
4. **Recommendation** (≤120 words). Give the verdict and the reasons that carry it, then say how confident you are and what would change the call.
5. **Conditions.** Three to six numbered items, each naming its finding, what closes it and what happens if it stays open. Close with any data requests needed to fill gaps in the input.

Write it the way a senior deal lead would: plain, specific to this target, and in your own words for each deal. Keep sentence patterns varied. Do not use boilerplate that would fit any company.

## Worked miniature (invented)

Brinmoor Cold-Chain sells temperature-monitoring subscriptions to food distributors. Reported revenue is 33.5M, but 3.5M of that was a one-off sensor rollout, so the base is 30.0M recurring, growing 12%. The top-tier finding is a lapsed food-safety certification, labelled resolved. The write-up says the renewal audit is not yet scheduled. The comps are four monitoring vendors trading at 2.6x–3.4x, plus one takeover at 4.9x. The takeover is excluded because the price included a control premium. The remaining median is 3.0x. Brinmoor grows more slowly than every remaining comp, so the multiple is cut to 2.5x–2.9x, which gives 75.0M–87.0M against an ask of 104M.

The memo opens: "We recommend approval to proceed to confirmatory diligence at no more than 87.0M, conditional on the reissued certificate before signing. We do not support the ask." The thesis names the certification as the primary risk. The risk table marks it "labelled resolved; open on the evidence (audit unscheduled)". Condition 1 requires the reissued certificate in hand before signing, or the committee walks away. Condition 2 asks for customer-level retention data, which the input does not provide.
