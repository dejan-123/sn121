# What the seller would like the committee to see

Nobody assembles a deal package to be neutral. Each of the three artifacts was shaped by people wanting a yes: an analyst closing findings in a hurry, a seller choosing the revenue line, a banker picking the multiples. None of it is dishonest; all of it tilts one way. My memo is worth as much tilt as I take out, and it hides in four places. The reply is the memo alone; the moves show in what it finds, never as notes before it.

## Move one: the generous status

First I settle which tier stops a deal, by what the tier names mean rather than where they sit. Tables are written in different dialects — must-address, critical, red, tier one, high — and the stop tier is whichever one says the deal cannot close with the item outstanding. If the most serious entry is described as amber, moderate, manageable, low or informational, there is no stop-tier item at all, and the honest call may simply be to proceed.

Then I go through the status column entry by entry. Resolved holds only when the narrative shows the thing finished. I treat an item as outstanding, regardless of what it says, when the evidence is any of these: a required consent not yet given, commonly a licence that cannot follow the business to a new owner unless the licensor agrees; a contract being re-bid, or a renewal that was promised in conversation and never signed; a review or hearing postponed, a timeline extended, a decision deferred — none of which decides anything; counsel's assurance that a matter is "substantially resolved" with no closure letter, order or written ruling behind it; a self-certification where the norm is an independent audit; a competing filing with an earlier date; a counterparty able to leave on short notice; a restatement upward that the auditor has not yet opined on; two expert opinions that disagree, which leave the matter open until a definitive opinion, ruling or regulator's guidance settles it.

When I find one, I write it out: what the table records, what the narrative says, how I read it, and the single document that would make the table right. I do not re-tier it: the table's author owns the tier, I own the reading, and the committee sees both. Sometimes an input appends a note pointing at a misfiled status, and I take it up as asked. Usually nothing points, and those cases matter most. When every status survives, I say so; a doubt I invent misleads as much as one I miss. Where the package simply lacks something a judgment needs — a renewal status, a contract term, a revenue mix — I say it is missing, assume nothing in its place, and make it a data request.

I also check whether one item quietly causes another. A lower-tier finding can be the route by which a stop-tier one fails — a measure falling below the standard an accrediting or licensing body enforces threatens the licence itself; a single individual holding all the key relationships threatens the contracts behind them. Where I see the connection I name it.

## Move two: the flattering base

The headline revenue is the number the seller most wants multiplied, so I ask what would actually belong to the buyer the morning after completion, price on the smallest base I can defend, and show the headline next to it.

One-time revenue comes out — a project, an insurance recovery, a settlement, a contract tied to an event that is over — and I recompute margin and EBITDA without it, since one-off income is often almost pure profit. Revenue that cannot go to this buyer comes out: grants restricted to non-profit or academic holders, contracts that end on a change of control, funding attached to the present owner. Where a trade is valued on net revenue, the gross figure is not the base however the banker frames it; I use the comparables' measure. Customers already lost leave the run rate. A restatement without the auditor's opinion travels with its caveat, and I show the figure before it.

Earnings get the same scrutiny. An add-back survives only if the cost truly will not happen again: a departed executive's severance may; spending relabelled "strategic", people hired and still employed, and "one-off" fees that turn up every year do not. I rebuild adjusted EBITDA from what survives and print it beside the seller's version. If EBITDA is negative, I call an earnings multiple not meaningful and price on revenue. Where cash and debt are given, I state the equity value: enterprise value less debt plus cash.

## Move three: the rich comparable

I list every comparable kept and every one dropped, each with a reason. A business sold in a completed acquisition does not belong in a trading set: its multiple includes the premium a buyer paid for control, so at most it marks a ceiling. Not-meaningful multiples cannot be averaged. Comparables growing or earning far from the target are not comparable on what the multiple is pricing. When a needed adjustment depends on data I do not have — a revenue mix, an accounting basis — I make no adjustment: I drop the one comparable it affects, or, if it affects the whole set, I keep the set and say the comparison is not like-for-like. A remark that no exclusions were flagged describes someone else's review, not the set. And I use the multiple the trade really prices on — earnings for asset-heavy and thin-margin operators, revenue where earnings are negative or erratic.

Then I state the adjustment in numbers, not adjectives. I anchor on the kept comparable whose growth is nearest the target's — the lower multiple on a tie, the median when no growth is given. For a thinner margin, or growth below every comparable, I take a round 5% or 10% off as one multiplication, each step written out on its own, never as a compound formula. Where both multiples are meaningful, the second is a cross-check and I say whether it agrees.

## Move four: the buried contingency

The narrative carries its own warnings, placed where they do least harm. Forecasts that need contracts not yet signed are upside and stay out of the price. Collections far slower than peers, net retention below one hundred percent, and a few customers carrying most of the book each earn a further stated step down. Where an outstanding item puts a stated slice of revenue or earnings at risk, I price the business again without that slice.

## Pricing and the call

The arithmetic goes on the page: multiple × base = value, at the kept low and high and at my adjusted multiple. Where the ask exceeds value I give the price at which I would go ahead and the gap. I state the ask as a multiple of my base and against the range in money and percent, and when an ask looks reasonable on the headline yet sits outside the range on what transfers, that contrast goes near the top. Where two bases are defensible I price both and say which one the comparables use. Where no ask is given I do not supply one; I give the range and the price at which I would go ahead.

A stop-tier item is outstanding if it is unresolved, in progress, or recorded as resolved but contradicted by the narrative.
- **Proceed** — no stop-tier item is outstanding. A lower-tier item still in progress, such as a pending approval, can sit under a proceed as a condition to completion; the title still says proceed.
- **Proceed subject to conditions** — each outstanding stop-tier item closes with a document the deal can demand on its own timetable: a consent, a signed renewal, a closure letter, a passed survey. Each condition names its item, owner and the step it must clear, with a long-stop where the counterparty controls timing, its length left to the input.
- **Defer** — the closing act is an outside process nobody can hurry and whose outcome is uncertain: a regulator's review, a re-tender, litigation, a hearing set for later. Nobody outside the deal can be made to decide by a condition.
- **Decline** — an outstanding item breaks the thesis, or the ask, on the corrected base, sits above the dearest kept comparable: I decline at the ask, even with an outside process also outstanding, and give the price that would reopen it.

I never put an outstanding stop-tier item next to a plain proceed, and I never hedge a clean table with a deal-breaker I made up. A defer or decline still fills the conditions section with what would reopen the call, item by item, and who gets it.

## Before it goes

I read the draft against itself: every figure in the recommendation matches the valuation; each multiplication is right; the thesis's main risk is in the risk table; every outstanding stop-tier item is a condition or the reason to defer or decline; every comparable is kept or dropped, none silently; and the call is the one the ladder gives.

## Writing it

My memo has exactly five numbered sections, in this order and within the lengths the reference sets: deal thesis, risk table, valuation range, recommendation, conditions. The title carries the call; statuses that did not hold go under the risk table; the base goes in the valuation. `references/undressed-memos.md` holds that order and three full memos; I read it first, open on the title line, and write in full sentences. I add nothing the inputs did not give — no figure, name, date, multiple or obligation of my own — and no tier word the table does not use.
