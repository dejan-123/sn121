# Investment committee memo: write backwards from the vote

A partner reads this memo tonight and votes in the morning. The memo needs to tell them which vote is available, at what price, and what must be true first. Answer the questions below privately, in order. Each answer changes what the memo says. Output only the finished memo. Do not show the questions, scratch working or a restated method.

## The questions, in order

**1. Which findings are genuinely still open?** Every status in the register is an upstream analyst's claim, not a fact. A blocking finding is closed only when the input points to a closing artefact: a signed consent, a regulator's closure letter, an auditor's opinion or an executed renewal. Read the write-up and any analyst notes against each label. Each of these means the finding is open, whatever the label says:
- a deferred review
- counsel calling something "substantially" done
- a belief management cannot document
- a contract still under re-bid
- a consent that is merely expected

When you overrule a label, show it in the table and give the reason in one sentence. The committee must see the conflict, not a silent fix. Whatever tier vocabulary the input uses, map it to three working classes: blocks signing, priced or structured away, and monitor after close. Keep the input's label beside yours. If a lower-tier item's stated impact is existential, flag it for upgrade rather than quietly re-tiering it.

**2. What vote does that leave?** Any open blocking finding rules out an unconditional proceed.
- **Curable by a document before signing:** the vote is proceed subject to conditions precedent, or hold until the document is delivered.
- **Not curable by paper:** revenue that dies with the change of owner, a regulatory status that could switch the business off, or IP that is expiring or contested. The vote is re-price, re-structure or decline.
- **Several open blockers, or one touching most of revenue:** this points towards decline at the asked terms.
- **Every blocker truly closed and the price inside your range:** say proceed plainly and do not invent caution.

**3. What earnings base is being bought?** A sound multiple on the wrong base is the commonest silent error. Rebuild the base from the write-up before applying any multiple:
- Remove one-off project revenue.
- Reject uplift from a restatement no auditor has signed.
- Test each add-back. Severance for a departed executive may be one-off; "strategic" spend usually recurs.
- Separate income that will not transfer to the buyer, such as grants or personal licences.
- Deduct customers who have already left.
- Where volume passes through the business, use revenue after pass-through costs, because that is what peers are priced on.

If a needed split is missing, say so and value on the conservative base the input supports.

**4. Which peers belong in the set?** Give a reason for every inclusion and every exclusion. Drop deals that carry a control or strategic premium when the rest are ordinary, and drop outliers whose growth or margin sits far from the target's. Choose the metric the profile allows: revenue multiples when EBITDA is negative or the peers show NM, and EBITDA multiples as a cross-check when both sides earn profit. If peer business mix differs and no breakdown is given, say the comparison is imperfect.

**5. Where does the target sit, and what does that say about the ask?** State the included low, high and median. Position the target on growth, margin, retention and risk against those peers, and say which way each factor pushes. Show base x multiple = value for both ends of the range, and recompute each product before writing it. Then compare the ask to the range: give the gap and the implied multiple of the ask on your base. If the seller quoted the ask on an inflated base, restate it on the true base.

**6. What makes each open finding safe?** Each condition names:
- its finding
- the evidence that closes it
- when it must arrive (before signing, at close, or within a period after close)

Risks that can be sized but not closed call for price mechanics: an escrow or holdback sized to the exposure stated in the input, an earn-out on at-risk revenue, or a specific indemnity. Never invent amounts, dates or counterparties. Where the input gives no figure, say that sizing awaits diligence.

**7. Does it hang together?** The thesis's primary risk must be the first row of the risk table and the first condition. The vote must match the ceiling from question 2, and the valuation must use the base from question 3. Every number must be in the input or derived from it.

## Writing it down

Open with one bold line giving the recommended vote, where the price sits and the item it hinges on. Then five headed sections:

1. **Thesis.** Why this asset, what the buyer actually gets, and the primary risk, in one paragraph of about 150 words.
2. **Risk table.** Columns: finding, input tier, your class, assessed status and why it matters. Blockers come first. One sentence after the table explains any relabelling.
3. **Valuation.** Peer selection, base, band, positioning, arithmetic and comparison to the ask, in prose plus at most one small table.
4. **Recommendation.** The vote, the reasons, and what would change it.
5. **Conditions.** A numbered list, each tied to a named finding, with its closing evidence and timing.

Obey any section lengths the request states. Otherwise aim for about 3,000 to 5,000 characters. Write like a deal lead briefing partners: full sentences with reasons attached, and honest confidence ("the input does not show..."). Absence of evidence is a gap to name, not a clean bill. Let the deal set the emphasis: a regulatory overhang reads differently from a revenue-quality problem, so never reuse one phrasing skeleton across sections or deals.

## Worked miniature (invented)

Kestrine Freight Software reports $26.0M revenue, of which $3.0M is a one-off migration fee, leaving a recurring base of $23.0M growing 14%. The register marks the hosting-licence change-of-control consent as top tier and "closed", but the write-up says the licensor's letter is unsigned. The ask is $97M. Peers trade at 3.1x, 3.6x, 4.0x and 6.5x. The 6.5x is a take-private carrying a control premium, so it is excluded. The kept band is 3.1-4.0x, median 3.6x. Kestrine grows below the peer median, so we hold 3.1-3.6x: $23.0M x 3.1 = $71.3M and $23.0M x 3.6 = $82.8M. The ask is 4.2x the recurring base, above every kept peer.

In memo voice: "We recommend holding signature until the licensor's consent is executed. The register shows it closed, but the write-up confirms the letter is unsigned, so we treat it as open. At $97M the seller is pricing one-off fees as recurring. On the recurring base the defensible range is $71.3-82.8M, and we would counter at the top of it." Two conditions follow: an executed consent before signing, and an audited split of migration fees before close.
