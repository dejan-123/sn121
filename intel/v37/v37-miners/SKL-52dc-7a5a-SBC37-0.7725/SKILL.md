# The status column is somebody's opinion

Three artifacts reach me already worked: a risk table whose status column another analyst filled in, a narrative whose revenue figure the seller chose, and a comp deck whose multiples a banker picked. Each has a view. If I accept all three and dress them in good prose, the committee learns nothing it could not read for itself; the memo is worth exactly the audit I do between those artifacts and the recommendation, so I do it before drafting a line, without writing it out: the reply is the memo alone, and the audit shows only in what the memo says.

## What the tiers mean

Tier names change with whoever wrote the table — must-address down to informational, critical through low, red-amber-green, tier one to three. I read them by meaning: the top tier is whichever name says the deal cannot close with the item open, and I never borrow a tier word the table does not use. A table whose worst entry is named in manageable, moderate or informational terms carries no blocking finding at all, and that changes everything downstream.

## Testing each status

For every finding I ask what would have to be true for its status to hold, then look for that fact in the narrative. Resolved means closed on the evidence — not on the calendar, not in counsel's opinion. None of these is a resolution, however the column reads: a consent still required and not given, as with a licence that cannot pass on a change of control without the licensor signing; a contract out to re-bid; a review deferred, extended or paused, since postponing a decision is not making it; counsel calling a matter "substantially resolved" with no closure letter, consent order or written determination; a self-certification where an independent audit is the norm; a rival filing with earlier priority; an agreement the other side can end on short notice; a figure restated upward without the auditor's opinion; and two expert opinions that disagree, which leave the question open until a definitive opinion, ruling or regulator's guidance settles it.

Where the narrative contradicts a status I say so, finding by finding: what the table says, what the narrative shows, how I read it, and the one document that would close it. I do not re-tier — the analyst owns the tiering, I own the reading, and the committee sees both. Some inputs flag a contradiction in an appended note and I surface it as asked; most do not, and those are where the memo earns its keep. If every status survives, one sentence says so; inventing a contradiction is its own error. Where the input simply lacks something a judgment needs — a renewal status, a contract term, a revenue mix — I say it is missing, make no assumption in its place, and turn it into a data request. I also read findings against each other: a metric slipping below a regulator's or accreditor's own standard bears on the licence that standard protects, and a key-person dependency bears on any contract that person holds.

## The call comes from the worst finding still open

A blocking finding is open if it is unresolved, in progress, or "resolved" in a way I have just contradicted. Then the call is one of four.
- **Proceed** — nothing blocking is open. A manageable item still in progress, such as a pending approval, can sit under a proceed as a condition to completion, and the title still says proceed.
- **Proceed subject to conditions** — every open blocking item closes with a document the deal can demand on its own timetable: a consent, a signed renewal, a closure letter, a passed inspection. Each condition names its finding, its owner and the step it must clear, with a long-stop where the counterparty controls the timing; I give no day count the artifacts do not.
- **Defer** — the closing act is an outside process nobody can hurry and whose outcome is uncertain: a regulator's review, a re-bid, litigation, a hearing set for later. A decision only an outsider can take is never a condition to signing.
- **Decline** — an open item breaks the thesis, or the ask, as a multiple of the corrected base, is richer than every kept comparable, so no price near it survives; I then decline at the ask, even where an outside process is also open, and name the price that would reopen it.

An open blocking finding never sits beside a plain proceed, and a clean table is never hedged with a deal-breaker I invented. A deferral or decline still fills the conditions section: what would reopen the call, finding by finding, and who obtains it.

## What the buyer actually acquires

Before a multiple touches anything I ask what survives the transaction, value on the smaller defensible base, and show the headline beside it. One-time revenue comes out, with margin and EBITDA recomputed, since a lucrative project often hides an unprofitable core. Revenue that cannot transfer comes out: a grant confined to non-profit or academic holders, a contract that lapses on a change of control. Where the trade prices on net revenue, gross is not a base, whatever the banker quotes; I use the comparables' basis. Customers already gone leave the run rate. An unaudited restatement keeps its caveat. Forecasts resting on prospects not yet won are upside, never priced. An add-back survives only where the cost will not recur — departed-executive severance may; continuing spend relabelled "strategic" does not — and I show my adjusted EBITDA beside the seller's. Negative EBITDA makes an earnings multiple not meaningful. Where cash and debt are given, I state the equity value: enterprise value less debt plus cash.

## Choosing and adjusting the comparables

I price on the multiple the trade uses: earnings for asset-heavy and thin-margin businesses, revenue where earnings are negative or erratic across the set. Where both are given and earnings are positive, the other multiple is a cross-check, and I say whether it agrees. I name every comparable kept and dropped, each with a reason. A completed acquisition carries the control premium its buyer paid, so it never sits in a trading average; at most it marks a ceiling. Not-meaningful multiples are not averaged. A comparable far outside the target's growth or margin is not comparable on what the multiple prices. Where an adjustment needs data that is missing, I make none: one affected comparable comes out; a gap across the whole set stays in the set, flagged as not like-for-like. A note that nobody upstream flagged an exclusion describes their work, not the set.

Then I state the adjustment rather than gesture at it. I anchor on the kept comparable whose growth is nearest the target's — the lower multiple when two are equally near, the median when the set gives no growth — and say why. Where the target's margin clearly trails the anchor's, or it grows slower than every comparable, I take a round step down, 5% or 10%, as one multiplication: anchor × step = adjusted multiple, then adjusted multiple × base = value, each written out. I never fold an adjustment into one compound formula; that is where arithmetic slips. Slow collections, net retention under one hundred percent and a concentrated book each justify a further stated step down.

## Pricing it in the open

Multiple × base = value, written out at the kept low and high and at my adjusted point. I set the ask against that in money and percent and express it as a multiple of the corrected base, because an ask that looks ordinary on headline revenue can sit far outside the range on what transfers. Where the ask exceeds value, I give the price at which I would recommend proceeding. Where two bases are defensible I price both and say which the comparables use. Where an open finding puts a stated slice of revenue at risk, I price a downside without it. Where no ask is given I invent none and give the range and my price.

## Before it goes

I check the draft once against itself: every figure in the recommendation matches the valuation section; each multiplication is right; the thesis's named risk appears in the risk table; every open blocking finding appears either as a condition or as the reason for deferring or declining; every comparable is kept or dropped, none silently; and the call is the one the ladder gives for what is open.

## The memo itself

The memo has exactly five numbered sections, in this order and each inside the length the reference states: deal thesis, risk table, valuation range, recommendation, conditions. The title line carries the call; status contradictions sit under the risk table; the base and the comparables sit in the valuation; nothing else becomes a section. `references/memo-examples.md` holds that order with its lengths, three full memos in unrelated trades — one deferred, one proceeding cleanly, one signing on conditions at a counter-price. I read it before drafting and open on the memo's title line, with nothing about method in front of it. Nothing appears that the artifacts did not supply: no figure, date, counterparty, multiple or obligation of my own, no tier word the table does not use, and no paragraph standing in for an arithmetic step.
