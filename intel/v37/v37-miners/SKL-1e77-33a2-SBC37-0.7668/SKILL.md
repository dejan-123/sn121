# The investment committee memo as an investigation

A diligence pack is not a set of facts to transcribe. It is three witnesses who sometimes disagree. The memo tells the committee three things: which version of the story survives cross-examination, what the asset is worth on the evidence, and what must still be proven before money moves. Five principles drive the work, and each one changes something visible in the memo.

## 1. The thesis is a hypothesis with a named way to die
Start by stating in one sentence why this asset is worth owning: its growth engine, margin, moat or strategic fit. Then find the single finding that would most directly falsify that reason. That finding is the primary risk. The thesis names it, and the risk table carries it as its first row. A thesis that praises growth while the register shows the growth asset cannot transfer is internally broken. So is a thesis whose named risk never appears in the table. The thesis paragraph therefore says three things: why to buy, what kills the case, and whether that test is still open.

## 2. A status is a claim, not a finding
Read every status cell against the financial write-up and any analyst notes. "Resolved" means the exposure is gone and something documents it: a signed renewal, a consent letter, a regulator's closure letter, an auditor's opinion. Some situations stay open whatever the cell says:
- a regulator's review that was deferred rather than closed
- counsel calling a matter "substantially" done with no closing document
- a contract re-bid still being contested
- a license that needs a counterparty's consent when ownership changes, where that consent is not in hand
- a customer base marked fine while it sits on month-to-month terms

When the artifacts conflict, never quietly pick the favorable one. Quote both, say which you rely on and why, and treat the item as open for the verdict. Do not overcorrect either. Where the input shows real closure, keep the item closed. Keep a bounded, priceable problem in the lower tier.

Tier honestly. A deal-breaker can remove a large share of revenue, the right to operate, or the core IP. A manageable risk has a cost you can price or a fix you can schedule. If a tier looks wrong, keep the analyst's label visible and state the tier you would use.

## 3. Rebuild the base before touching a multiple
The seller's number is also a hypothesis. Recompute the revenue or earnings the business will actually carry after close:
- Remove one-off project revenue.
- Value pass-through volume businesses on the revenue they keep.
- Carve out streams that cannot follow the business to a new owner.
- Treat unaudited restatements as unverified.
- Reject add-backs that recur or carry vague labels.
- Take out customers already lost.

If earnings are negative or flattered, value the business on revenue and say why. Divide the ask by your honest base to get its implied multiple. The gap between that multiple and the comps is often the headline of the valuation section.

## 4. The comp set has a chain of custody
Name every comp you keep and every comp you drop, with a reason. Common reasons to drop one are a strategic or control-premium takeout, growth or profitability far outside the target's, or a different business model. Place the target against the kept comps on growth, margin, mix and retention (where the data gives it), then say whether it earns the bottom, middle or top of their band. Show the arithmetic: low multiple × base = low value, high multiple × base = high value. Keep the range tight enough to decide with. Where the comp data lacks something you need, such as the hardware/software split or profitability, say how that limits confidence instead of assuming it away. Use only the multiples in the table. Never invent a comp, a median or a peer figure.

## 5. The verdict is whichever hypotheses still stand
Choose the verdict from what remains open:
- **Proceed.** Every top-tier finding is genuinely closed and the ask sits inside your range. Attach ordinary protections.
- **Proceed only on conditions, or hold.** A top-tier finding is open but a specific document or event can close it before signing. Make that closure a condition precedent, set the price at the defended range, and protect the residual exposure.
- **Decline, or hold at the current price.** An open finding would destroy the thesis and cannot be closed before signing, or the ask sits far above the defensible range. Say what would reopen the deal.

Never write an unconditional proceed over an open top-tier item. Never reflexively decline a clean deal either. Each condition names the finding it closes, the evidence that closes it, and what happens if it fails: a walk right, an escrow or holdback, a price step-down or a specific indemnity. Use only deadlines that appear in the input.

## Writing it down
Output only the memo: no preamble, no working notes, no restated procedure. Open with a two-to-three-sentence bottom line giving the verdict, the defended range against the ask, and the finding that decides it. Then write five sections in order: Thesis, Risk Assessment, Valuation, Recommendation, Conditions.

If the request sets length bounds, obey them exactly. Otherwise use these limits:

| Section | Length |
|---|---|
| Thesis | 80–150 words |
| Valuation | at most 250 words plus the arithmetic lines |
| Recommendation | 100–150 words |
| Conditions | three to six, each one or two sentences |

The risk table is the only table. Give it five columns: the finding in brief, the analyst's tier, the status as you verified it, your assessment, and why it matters to the thesis. Order it with deal-breakers first and the primary risk on top. Write every other section as connected prose in which each reason leads to the next, and vary sentence shapes so the sections do not read as filled-in boxes.

Copy every number exactly as given and recheck each multiplication before finishing. Where the inputs are silent, write plainly that they do not establish the point, and name the document that would. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Aim for 3,000–5,500 characters in total.

## Miniature (invented case)
The target is Brisane Coldchain, a maker of refrigerated-freight sensors. The register marks its rail-safety certification "resolved", but the write-up says the certification lapsed and a renewal hearing has been set. Revenue is $27.6M, which includes a $2.7M one-off retrofit job, and the ask is $121M. The comps trade at 3.1x, 3.4x and 3.9x revenue, plus a 6.2x strategic takeout.

A finished bottom line might read:

> We recommend holding at the seller's price. The defended range is $79.7M–$89.6M, against an ask of $121M, and the certification lapse that the register marks resolved remains open: a renewal hearing is not a renewal. Recurring revenue is $24.9M once the retrofit job is removed, so the ask implies 4.9x. We drop the 6.2x takeout as a control-premium deal. Brisane's growth trails the middle comp, which supports 3.2x–3.6x on the kept band (24.9 × 3.2 = 79.7; 24.9 × 3.6 = 89.6). We would re-engage at or below $89.6M, with recertification as a condition precedent and an escrow sized to the certified revenue at risk. The inputs do not say how much revenue depends on the certification, and the seller's carrier contracts would show it.
