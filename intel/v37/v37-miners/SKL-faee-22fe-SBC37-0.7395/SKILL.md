# The committee memo as a question ladder

A committee memo answers five questions, and the order matters because each answer closes doors. Once a blocking finding is open, no valuation can rescue approval at signing. Once the true base is fixed, the seller's headline multiple stops mattering. Once the comp set is fixed, the range becomes arithmetic, not opinion. Work the ladder privately, then write the memo. Output only the finished memo: no working notes, no restated procedure, no question labels.

## 1. Is any top-tier finding really still open?

A status column is the upstream analyst's claim, and the narrative is the evidence, so read each row against the narrative. A top-tier row marked "resolved" is still open if the narrative shows a live process:
- a re-bid still under way
- a regulatory review that was deferred, not closed
- counsel calling a matter "substantially" done with no closure document
- a change-of-control consent that is required but not yet obtained
- a certification that has lapsed

A blank status counts as open, and so does an "under way" status. Severity and closure are separate axes. A top-tier item closed with documents does not block, and a mid-tier item can still move price.

Then test the tiering itself. A finding that would strike the thesis directly belongs in the deal-breaker band whatever its label says. Examples are an injunction against a core product, the loss of the funding channel that carries most revenue, or the loss of the licence or sponsor the business runs on. An item that only costs money is manageable. Upstream tables use different words for the tiers, so map them onto three bands of your own: blocks the deal unless cleared, manageable through price or structure, and monitor. When you disagree with a label, keep the upstream label visible next to your effective tier and status and give the reason. Never re-tier silently.

What this forecloses: if a blocker is open, approving at signing is gone. What remains is whether the blocker can be cured before signing (a consent, a closure letter, a recertification, a documented contract award), which points to conditional approval with the cure as a gate. If it is structural instead (the revenue, licence or sponsor cannot legally pass to this buyer), the choice is to decline or to re-scope the deal around it.

## 2. What base is actually being bought?

Value only the economics that survive closing:
- Remove one-time project revenue.
- Remove revenue that cannot transfer to this kind of acquirer.
- Take recently cancelled customers out of the run-rate.
- For processors and marketplaces, use revenue after pass-through costs, never gross volume.
- Treat an unaudited upward restatement as unproven.

Test each add-back on its merits. A one-off departure package may truly be non-recurring, but "strategic" or growth spending is ordinary operating cost. If EBITDA is negative, or turns positive only through contested add-backs, an EBITDA multiple cannot be used, so switch to a revenue multiple and say why. Show the seller's base next to yours.

What this forecloses: from here on, judge the ask as an implied multiple on your base, not on the seller's.

## 3. Which comps belong, and where does the target sit?

Name every comparable and say whether it is in or out, and why. Take these out of a trading set, or show them only as a ceiling:
- control-premium or sponsor-premium acquisitions
- outliers whose growth describes a different business
- any multiple marked NM

Place the target against the comps you kept on growth, margin, scale, retention and revenue mix. Growth or margin below the median prices the target below the median. A shrinking installed base (retention under 100%) or heavy customer concentration pushes it toward the low end. If the comps' mix of hardware and software is unknown, say that the margin comparison is weaker.

Compute each point as multiple × base, to one decimal, and recheck every product before writing it down. A wrong product costs more than a missing one. Where both multiples exist, cross-check one against the other and explain any gap. If step 1 found a structural blocker, still give the range, because the committee needs to know what the asset is worth if the issue clears, but label the range contingent.

## 4. Which option does the evidence support?

Set the realistic options side by side: approve at the ask, approve with conditions at a revised price or structure, defer until named gates clear, or decline. For each, state what it assumes and which finding breaks it. Then choose one. In the same sentence as the verdict, name the blocking finding and the gap between the seller's base and yours. The primary risk named in the thesis must appear in the risk table. State confidence honestly: say what the input proves, and what it leaves open.

## 5. What would change the answer?

Each condition must cure a named finding and state three things:
- the evidence that closes it: an executed consent, a regulator's closure letter, an auditor's opinion, a renewed agreement
- the deadline, using only timing the input provides, such as a response window, an expiry or a re-bid
- the consequence if it fails: walk away, re-price, or an escrow or specific indemnity sized from an input figure

A generic item such as "complete confirmatory diligence" is not a condition. If the input lacks a date, owner or amount, say it must be obtained. Never supply one yourself.

## Writing the memo

**Opening.** Start with two sentences giving the verdict, the value range against the ask, and the finding that drives the call.

**Section order.** Write five sections, in this order:
1. **Thesis**, 120–200 words: why the asset is attractive, using input figures, plus the primary risk.
2. **Risk table** with columns for finding, upstream tier and status, effective tier and status, and why it matters. Put deal-breakers first, then add a short paragraph on every label you changed and every gap or contradiction in the inputs.
3. **Valuation**, 180–300 words: base reconciliation, comps in and out, adjustments, and the arithmetic written inline.
4. **Recommendation**: a compact option table, then a prose verdict.
5. **Conditions**: three to six, numbered, each naming its finding.

**Length.** Aim for about 3,500–6,000 characters in total.

**Style.** Outside the two tables, write varied, connected prose, the way a senior corporate development lead would. Every figure must come from the input or be computed from it.

## Miniature (invented)

A cold-chain monitoring software firm reports revenue of 26.0m (all figures in millions), including a one-off 3.0m sensor rollout. The risk table marks a firmware export licence "resolved", but the narrative says only that a renewal was filed. The comps are four trading peers at 3.0x–4.4x revenue plus one strategic take-private at 6.5x. The ask is 110m.

Working the ladder: the licence is open despite its label, so approval at signing is foreclosed, but a grant is a document, so the blocker is curable. The base is 23.0m of recurring revenue. The take-private comes out as a control premium. The target grows 12% against a peer median of 16%, with margins in line, which supports 3.0x/3.4x/3.8x, giving 69.0 / 78.2 / 87.4. The ask works out to about 4.8x the recurring base, above every peer kept.

The memo then reads like this: "We recommend conditional approval at no more than 87m, gated on the export licence grant. The risk table calls it resolved, yet the narrative shows only a filed application, and the seller's 110m prices the one-off rollout as if it recurs."
