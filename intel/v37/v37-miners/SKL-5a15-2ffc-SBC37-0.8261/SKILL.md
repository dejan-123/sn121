# Investment committee memo: the decision menu

The committee is not voting on whether the company is good. It is choosing among a few actions. A strong memo shows which actions the evidence has already removed and why the remaining one is best.

**Core rules**
1. The menu is: proceed; proceed only once named conditions are met before signing; proceed at a lower price; defer until evidence arrives; decline. Compare the live options and choose one.
2. The most severe finding that is not proven closed sets the limit on the menu. A label that says resolved is only a claim. A finding is closed by a document: a closure letter, a signed consent, an executed renewal, an auditor's opinion or a regulator's decision.
3. The defended price is a multiple applied to the base the buyer keeps, not the base the seller quotes.
4. Comps are chosen, not inherited. Name who stays and who goes, then show base × low and base × high.
5. Each condition cures a named finding and states the evidence that proves the cure.

## Tier and status are separate questions
Tier says how bad a finding is if it is true. Status says whether anyone has proven it is no longer true. Sometimes the narrative, an analyst note or the finding's own wording contradicts the status column. Examples: a re-bid is still live, a review was postponed rather than ended, counsel says "substantially done" with no closure letter, or a needed consent was never obtained. In those cases, say so in the risk table and carry the item as open. Keep the analyst's tier visible and explain your treatment instead of silently re-tiering. A finding marked closed whose text describes a structural fact is mitigated at best; ask what was actually fixed. Examples are month-to-month key customers, a partner who can cancel at will, or a competing patent filing. If a tier or status is missing, say it is missing and treat the item conservatively. You may raise a lower-tier item when the numbers show it bites, such as retention below 100% or a ratio already past a regulatory limit, but say that you are doing so.

## Gates versus priceable risks
Sort findings by what they do to the asset.

A **gate** is a risk that removes what you are paying for. Examples: revenue that cannot pass to a new owner, a licence or sponsor that ends on change of control, an approval the revenue depends on, or a key patent lapsing into rivals' filings. While a gate is open it blocks proceed. If it cannot close within the deal timeline, it argues for defer or decline.

A **priceable risk** has a quantifiable cost. Examples: fines, deferred capex, a tax reassessment, or a litigation range. It belongs in price, through a deduction, an escrow or a specific indemnity.

Key-person risks are usually handled with retention terms. Order the risk table gates first. The thesis must name the top gate as its primary risk.

## The surviving base
Rebuild the base before applying any multiple:
- Remove one-off projects.
- Remove revenue that will not survive the buyer, such as non-transferable funding or departed customers.
- Where the sector values revenue after pass-through costs, use that basis.
- Test add-backs. Severance from a real one-off event may stay out. Recurring "strategic" spend goes back in.
- Treat revenue lifted by an unaudited accounting change as unproven.
- If EBITDA is negative, an earnings multiple is meaningless. Say so and value on revenue.

Always match the basis the comps use.

## Comp discipline
Exclude a comp only for a stated reason: a control or strategic premium inside a past transaction, growth or margin far outside the target's band, or a different model or revenue mix. Then place the target within the remaining cluster on growth, margin profile, retention and scale. A target below the peer median sits at the low end, and you say why. If the comps' mix or basis is unknown, state the limitation and discount for it. Write the math as base × multiple = value for both ends. Cross-check with EBITDA where it is meaningful. Express the ask as a percentage above or below your range. Recompute every product and percentage once before writing, because one wrong figure discredits the memo.

## Options side by side, then the call
In the recommendation, set out the realistic options, usually three, in a compact table or tight sentences. For each, state what it requires, what it risks, and why it wins or fails on this evidence. Unconditional proceed never appears while a gate is open.
- Gates are cured by conditions satisfied before signing.
- Priceable items are handled through price mechanisms.
- If the ask is above your range, give a counter-price and a walk-away point drawn from the range.

Close with the decision in one plain sentence.

## Writing it
Output only the finished memo. Open with the decision line, with no preamble, plan or restated instructions. Use five sections in order: thesis, risk table, valuation, recommendation, conditions. Keep them proportionate and obey any length limits in the request. The memo usually runs 2,500–5,000 characters.

Write connected prose that uses this vocabulary naturally: gate, priceable, surviving base, labelled closed versus closed. Use tables only for the risk register and the comp or option comparison. Let each deal's facts decide where the emphasis falls, so no two memos read alike.

Use only names, figures and timings found in the input. Where evidence is absent, write that it is not established rather than assuming it in either direction. Unknown deadlines become "before signing" or "before close", never invented dates.

## Miniature (invented)
**Facts.** Hollowmere Kiln Services reports LTM revenue of $26.5M, including a $3.5M one-off rebuild contract. The top-tier finding is an emissions-permit renewal labelled resolved, while the narrative says the hearing was only rescheduled. A lower-tier item is $1.3M of deferred refractory capex. Comps trade at 1.9x, 2.1x and 2.3x, plus one strategic buyout at 3.4x. The ask is $61M.

**Memo excerpt.** "Proceed only after the permit is renewed in writing, at no more than $51.6M. The permit is the primary risk. A rescheduled hearing is still an open hearing, so we carry it as an open gate despite its label. The surviving base is $23.0M once the rebuild contract is removed. We exclude the 3.4x buyout because it embeds a strategic premium. The 1.9x–2.3x cluster gives $43.7M to $52.9M, and the ask sits about 15% above the top. Paying the ask buys one-off revenue and an open permit, so we reject it. Deferral is the fallback if the hearing slips again. Our preferred option is a conditional proceed at the top of the range less the $1.3M capex. Conditions: an executed permit renewal before signing, and the capex deducted from price or escrowed."
