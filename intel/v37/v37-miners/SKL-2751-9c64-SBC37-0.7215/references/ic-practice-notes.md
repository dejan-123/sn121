# Practice notes for investment committee work

Background a corporate development lead brings to a memo. None of it describes a particular deal; use it to choose the right protection, read a figure correctly, or explain a judgment in one sentence.

## Turning a finding into a protection

A finding is rarely a plain yes or no. The committee wants to know which tool converts it from an unknown into a bounded, allocated risk.

- **Condition precedent.** Signing or closing waits on an event: a regulator's clearance, a counterparty's consent to a change of control, a licence assignment, a renewal signed. Use it when the risk is binary and the answer will exist before closing.
- **Specific indemnity with escrow or holdback.** The seller keeps paying for a known, quantifiable exposure such as a tax reassessment, a pending claim or a remediation estimate. Size the escrow from the artifact's own figure and release it on a final ruling.
- **Price adjustment.** When a finding permanently reduces what the buyer owns (revenue that cannot transfer, a customer already lost), the remedy is a lower price, not a warranty, because nothing will be breached.
- **Earn-out.** When value depends on something the seller believes and the buyer doubts (pipeline conversion, a contract re-bid, a product approval), pay part of the price only if it happens.
- **Representations, warranties and insurance.** They cover the unknown, not the known. A disclosed problem is excluded from cover, so a known issue needs its own protection.
- **Retention and transition.** Key people with undocumented knowledge, sole relationships or visa dependence are handled with retention agreements, documented handover and, where relevant, transfer filings started before signing.
- **Consents and novations.** Licences, channel agreements and sponsor relationships that terminate or need approval on a change of control must be consented in writing before signing if the business depends on them.

## Reading the financial narrative

- **Quality of earnings.** One-time costs genuinely gone after the deal (a departed executive's severance, a completed relocation) may be added back. Costs a business keeps incurring under a new name ("strategic investment", annual launches, recurring recruiting) may not. Revenue that will not recur (an implementation project, a single licence sale) is taken out of a recurring base.
- **Restatements and policy changes.** A switch in revenue recognition that lifts reported revenue without auditor sign-off is a caveat on every multiple built on that revenue until the auditors opine.
- **Gross versus net.** Businesses that pass third-party money through (payments, marketplaces, staffing) are valued on net revenue, the part they keep. A multiple quoted on gross volume is not comparable to peers priced on net.
- **Grant and subsidy income.** Public grants often cannot follow a for-profit acquirer, and they frequently subsidise fixed costs, so the profitable-looking blended picture can hide a loss-making commercial business.
- **Retention and concentration.** Net revenue retention below 100% means the installed base is shrinking before new sales; above 110% it is expanding on its own. A few customers holding most revenue on short contracts make the revenue line less certain than the growth rate suggests.
- **Working capital.** Days sales outstanding well above peers ties up cash and can signal billing or collection problems; it rarely changes the multiple but matters to the price mechanism.
- **Revenue mix.** Hardware and software inside one company earn very different margins; a blended margin can look average while each half sits far from its peers.

## Building and defending the comparable set

- **Trading versus transaction multiples.** A listed peer's multiple prices a minority stake. A completed acquisition includes a control premium, commonly a fifth to two-fifths above trading levels, and a private-equity exit can include a financing premium. Mixing them inflates the median.
- **Outliers.** A peer growing at twice the rate of the rest, or with a fundamentally different model, pulls the median toward a business the target is not. Remove it with a stated reason, keep everything that resembles the target, and never prune to reach the ask.
- **Not meaningful multiples.** A negative or tiny EBITDA produces a meaningless EV/EBITDA; such a peer simply drops out of that metric.
- **Adjusting from the median.** Faster growth and higher margins justify a premium; slower growth, lower margins, customer concentration, contracting retention and smaller scale justify a discount. State each direction with the two figures behind it, then move the median by a stated amount.
- **Scale.** Smaller private targets usually trade below larger listed peers because of liquidity, concentration and key-person risk; where the artifacts give no scale for the peers, the direction is an assumption and should be said as one.
- **Cross-checks.** When a second multiple gives a very different answer, the gap usually has a reason worth one line: margins below the peers' implied margin, an unusual mix, or a base distorted by one-off items.

## How committees read the verdict

- **Proceed** means the committee can sign once the stated conditions precedent are met; it is never paired with an open must-address finding, because a signed deal cannot be undone if that finding goes the wrong way.
- **Request more data** means the deal may be sound but the record is not yet good enough to sign; each request should name the document, who holds it, and what answer would change the verdict.
- **Pass** means the record already shows the outcome that defeats the thesis; the useful pass memo says what would have to change for the committee to look again.

## Writing for the committee

Members read the thesis and the recommendation first, then check the risk table and the arithmetic. Short sentences, figures with their source, and one idea per bullet serve them better than adjectives. A memo that leaves its conditions vague hands the work back to the committee.
