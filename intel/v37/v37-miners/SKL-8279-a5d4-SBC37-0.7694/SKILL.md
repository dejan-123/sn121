# Five questions, in the order the room asks them

A committee does not read a memo front to back. It asks five questions, roughly in this order, and votes on the answers. Answer them before drafting, without writing the answers out as notes: the reply is the memo alone, and the answers show in what it says. Every input has been through other hands — an analyst's status column, the seller's revenue line, a banker's peers — so test each answer against the material.

## Question one: is anything at the top of the table still live?

**Find the gating tier by meaning, not by label.** Analysts name tiers differently — must-address, critical, red, tier one, high — and the top band is whichever of these names means the deal cannot close with it outstanding. When the worst entry in a table is called manageable, moderate, low or informational, the table has no gating issue, and the call can be to proceed.

**Test every status against the narrative.** A status is a claim made by whoever logged it. "Resolved" holds only when the narrative shows the thing actually closed. Treat these as live whatever the column says:
- a required third-party consent not yet given, such as a licence that lapses on change of control;
- a contract under re-bid, or a renewal assured verbally but unsigned;
- a review deferred, a deadline extended, a proceeding stayed — delay is not a ruling;
- counsel's view that a matter is "substantially resolved" when no closure letter, consent decree or determination exists;
- a self-certification standing in for the independent audit that is normal for that obligation;
- a rival filing with an earlier priority date;
- a partner or customer that can walk away on short notice;
- revenue restated upward that the auditor has not yet opined on;
- two experts who reach opposite answers — the point stays live until one authoritative answer arrives: a definitive opinion, a court's ruling, a regulator's word.

**Report contradictions; do not re-tier.** For each one, set out the table's version, the narrative's evidence, the memo's reading, and the single document that would close it. Leave the tier as logged; the analyst's tier stands beside your reading. An input sometimes carries a note pointing at a misfiled status; act on it. Most say nothing, and catching those is the point. If every status survives, say so in one line and do not manufacture a doubt. Where the inputs are silent on something the call needs — whether a renewal happened, what a contract permits, how revenue splits — say so, do not fill the hole with a guess, and list it as a request.

**Read across rows.** A manageable item can be the mechanism of a gating one — a metric drifting below the standard a regulator or accreditor enforces threatens the licence it guards; one employee holding every key relationship threatens the contract base. Name the link.

## Question two: what exactly would we own?

Price only what transfers. Value on the narrowest defensible base and print the headline beside it.
- **One-off revenue** — a project, an implementation, a settlement, a one-season programme. Remove it and recompute margin and EBITDA; a rich project often hides a loss-making core.
- **Revenue that cannot move to this buyer** — a grant limited to non-profit or academic holders, funding tied to the current owner, a contract void on change of control.
- **Pass-through and gross billing** — carries no margin; where the trade is valued on net revenue, the gross line is not a base, whatever the banker applies to it. Use the peers' basis.
- **Customers already gone** — strip them from the run rate.
- **Unaudited restatements** — carry the caveat and the prior figure.
- **Forecasts resting on deals not yet won** — upside, never in the price.

Treat earnings the same way. Accept an add-back only for a cost that will not recur: a departed executive's severance may pass; spending renamed "strategic" does not; a "one-off" that appears every year is simply a cost. Show your adjusted EBITDA beside the seller's. Negative EBITDA means no earnings multiple applies. Where cash and debt figures are supplied, show what the shares are worth: the enterprise figure, minus borrowings, plus cash.

## Question three: which businesses is it really like?

Name every peer kept and every peer dropped, with the reason for each. Drop any completed acquisition from a trading set: its multiple includes the premium a buyer paid for control, so treat it as an upper bound, never as a peer. Drop not-meaningful multiples. Drop peers whose growth or margin sits far outside the target's, since those are the dimensions the multiple prices. When a mix or accounting difference matters and the data to correct for it is absent, do not correct on an assumption: drop the single peer it affects, or, when it affects the whole set, keep the set and state that the margin comparison is not like-for-like. "No exclusions flagged" upstream describes someone else's work, not the peer set. Choose the multiple the trade actually prices on — earnings for low-margin manufacturers and asset-heavy operators, revenue where margins are thin or negative across the set.

Then put a number on the adjustment instead of an adjective. Pick the kept peer whose growth rate is closest to the target's — on a tie, the cheaper one; with no growth figures, the middle of the set — and say why it is the anchor. If the target's margin trails that peer's, or it grows slower than every peer, knock off a round 5% or 10% you name. Do each step as its own multiplication, written out, and never stack them into one formula: compound arithmetic is where mistakes get in. Receivables collected far slower than peers, net retention under one hundred percent, or a handful of customers carrying the book each justify a further named cut. Where an earnings multiple is usable too, run it as a second opinion and say whether the two agree.

## Question four: what is it worth, and where does the ask fall?

Write the arithmetic out — multiple × base = value — for the cheapest and dearest kept peer and for your adjusted figure. State the ask as a multiple of the corrected base and against the range in money and percent — an ask that looks ordinary on the headline is often far outside the range on what transfers, and saying so is often the memo's most useful line. Where a live issue threatens a named share of revenue or earnings, rerun the value without that share as a stress case. When the ask is above value, name your own number and how far apart the two are. If the seller names no price, do not make one up: give the range and the figure at which you would sign. Where two bases are both defensible — headline and recurring, gross and net, with and without a grant — price both, say which one the peers are built on, and recommend on that one.

## Question five: what must happen before we sign?

Make the call from question one. A gating issue is live if unresolved, in progress, or resolved in the column but contradicted by the narrative.
- **Proceed** — nothing gating is live. A manageable item still moving, such as an approval in train, can ride along as a completion condition; the title still says proceed.
- **Proceed subject to conditions** — each live gating issue can be cleared by paper the other side is able to hand over before you sign: a written consent, a countersigned renewal, a closing letter, a re-inspection certificate. Tie each condition to its issue and name who chases it and the step it must clear, signing or completion; where the other side sets the pace, add a drop-dead date without inventing its length.
- **Defer** — clearing it means waiting on something outside the deal's control with an uncertain result: an agency still reviewing, a tender not yet awarded, a lawsuit, a hearing on a later date. An outsider's pending decision is never a condition to signing.
- **Decline** — a live item breaks the thesis, or the ask, on the corrected base, is richer than every kept peer, so nothing near it holds up: decline at the ask, even if an outside wait is also live, and give the number that would reopen the vote.

Never pair a live gating issue with a plain proceed. Never invent a gating issue to justify hedging a clean table. Waiting or walking away still answers question five: list what would reopen the vote, issue by issue, and who gets it.

## Check it against itself

Before you finish, reread the draft: the numbers in the call are the numbers in the valuation; each product is correct; the thesis's risk shows up as a row; every live gating issue is either a condition or the reason you are waiting or walking away; no peer goes unmentioned; and the call is what question one left live.

## Writing it down

The memo has exactly five numbered sections, in this order, each within the length the reference sets: deal thesis, risk table, valuation range, recommendation, conditions. The title carries the call; contradicted statuses go under the risk table; the base and peers go in the valuation. Read `references/worked-memos.md` first — it holds that order and three complete memos in unrelated trades — and open with the title line, nothing before it.

Write full sentences; the table is for risks, not for thinking. Use no figure, name, date, multiple or obligation the inputs did not provide, and no tier word the table did not use.
