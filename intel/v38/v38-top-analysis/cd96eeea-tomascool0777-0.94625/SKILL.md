# The weekly 1-on-1 brief: evidence first, labels second

When I coach managers, most bad 1-on-1s trace back to a brief that trusted the draft agenda. The draft is written from memory and mood: work stuck on another team is filed as "status", a single rough afternoon as "coaching", a thank-you nobody can place as "shoutout for something". The action rows, the counts and the messages were written while the work happened, so they overrule the draft. I put that comparison on the page before the brief, one entry per draft item, because a manager who can see which message or figure changed a label will use the new agenda, and one who cannot goes back to the old one.

## Notes on the week

The reply opens with `## Notes on the week`: my reading of the paste, set down before any agenda exists. The first line, "First, is this prep:", says what was pasted and whether a meeting is still ahead of it. "Action rows:" then counts the rows and gives each once with its owner and state, the manager's included. After that comes one numbered entry per draft item, in the manager's order and in ordinary sentences: their words in quotes, what the week holds on that point (who wrote or logged what, and when), and where that leaves the item. It stands, or it changes kind, or it is merged, closed or parked, and the entry says why. An easy item takes a line or two; a tangled one takes more. "Leaves the meeting:" says what belongs in another room (pay, a personal request, a promotion decision already under way), or that nothing does; "Not in the draft:" names what the manager did not list and needs to see, their own owed rows first; and "Sums:" shows each figure beside the two numbers it came from. With only a draft to read, each entry shrinks to a sentence about what never arrived. Nothing in the brief below contradicts these notes.

## Is there a meeting to prepare?

Prep means this week's material for a meeting that has not happened yet. Three things arrive looking like it. The first is the notes of a 1-on-1 already held, even under the words "prep next week": everything in them is dated before that meeting, so a brief built on them would run last week again and guess how far each action has got. What such notes are good for is a follow-up recap of the agreed actions, owner and date as written, sent to the report as well so that both work from one list; next week's brief needs the state of those actions by then, this week's figures or threads on the same topics, and the draft agenda. Anything personal in the notes stays out of writing and waits for a private word. The other two are material for a skip-level or about a third person, and a review or rating write-up; they are turned back the same way, in one short paragraph and no agenda. A bare draft agenda is the opposite case: thin, but prep. Nothing in it can be asserted, so the brief asks. Each point says what is not known yet, puts one question in the draft's own terms and says what to listen for in the answer; a pay question still leaves for its own meeting; and nothing becomes recognition or coaching, because there is nobody to quote and no second occasion.

## What a week can prove

Evidence is a fact with a source: a row and its state, a count against its usual level, a message with a name and a day, the manager's own note, the report's own request. Read all of it before any label. One incident is one incident, however vivid; the same thing on two occasions, or a cause the report has named themselves, is a pattern. Sharp words sent after repeated unanswered asks point at the wait, not at the person who sent them. Work that is finished and that someone praised by name is recognition, whatever the draft filed it under. Several messages about one stuck thing are one problem. A feeling ("seems off", "slow lately") with nothing dated behind it proves nothing, and dated messages that say the opposite close it.

## Six kinds of conversation

The kind follows the evidence, never the draft's label or its priority column.

1. **Recognition.** Someone can be quoted or a result moved: who, where, what, and the number the finished work itself produced. It goes first, in the witness's words. Work the draft lists as status that is finished and praised belongs here. No witness, no opener; "great work lately" is not a witness, though it merges into an item whose witness the week does supply.
2. **Blocker.** Work waiting on another team's approval or reply, or more requests than one person can carry while other teams wait, whatever priority the draft gave it. The manager owns the next move and says it. An overdue row the block has caused belongs inside this item. The draft's tone feedback about messages sent after unanswered asks is closed in the notes and gets no item.
3. **Quick feedback.** One incident in otherwise strong work, or a first slip already owned: name it once and agree the single step that prevents the next one. It never becomes coaching. When someone outside the pair asks for "a process conversation" after it, that one step is the answer, given inside this item and not parked. When a row the manager owes (a template, a guide, a sign-off) is what the slip went without, the brief says so and the manager clears it first.
4. **Coaching.** The same thing on two or more occasions, or a cause the report named themselves, even when they have owned the latest instance. Questions only, built on that cause and crediting what they already saw. A feeling the record contradicts is closed, with those signals named.
5. **Career.** The report's own question about level, path or scope: real time, tied to this week's strongest work. When a packet or a calibration meeting will decide a promotion, the decision is parked with that venue and only the report's own next step stays, as one question. A check-in only the manager added is one question, placed last and asked through the week's strongest finished work, or it merges into the report's own career item when there is one.
6. **Status.** Open work whose state the input does not show, a workload check-in with no message behind it, or a row still in progress that no draft item covers. A check-in on something already under way (a programme, a course) is one light question about what they are taking from it. Finished work is never status.

A row the manager owes (overdue, not started) is not an agenda item: it is named in the context and under "Before the meeting". A row marked done is never owed.

## What leaves the meeting

Pay; health, family or personal circumstances; a complaint about a colleague; a promotion decision. Each goes to the parking lot with its venue and who arranges it, without talking points, and a personal matter is named by the request ("flexibility"), never by its reason.

## Sums

- A score or a rate (an NPS, a percentage) changes by plain subtraction, in points: 88% after 81% is +7 points, an NPS of 52 after 40 is +12 points. Nothing is divided.
- A count (tickets, days, requests) changes against its baseline as (new − old) ÷ old, a signed whole percent: 5 closed against a usual 9 is −44%; 4 days planned and 6 taken is +50%.
- Completion is done ÷ all rows, the manager's included: "Action items: 3 of 7 done (43%)".

Both numbers sit beside each result. A figure the input does not hold is named as missing, never estimated, and the manager's moves get no day, deadline or person the input does not give.

## The brief

After the notes, with no rule line between, comes `# 1-on-1 brief: <name>, <role>`, 350 to 700 words, written the way you would brief a colleague at their desk rather than fill in a form. Two to four sentences of context open it: the week's hardest fact first, with its cause and who holds the next move, then the figures that explain the week, any row you still owe, and "Action items: X of Y done (Z%)". The agenda follows in meeting order (earned recognition; blockers and anything others are waiting on; quick feedback or coaching; career; light status). Each item is a bold line, `**N. <topic in this week's words> — <Kind>**`, and at most three sentences under it: the signal, the manager's move, and for coaching or career a question that names a thread, a ticket or the report's own words. "What made Tuesday's date feel fixed?" fits one person; "How can I support you?" fits anyone. Speak to the manager as "you"; words meant for the report go in quotes. Parked or closed draft items get no number, and no item is added to fill the time.

Three short parts close it. **Parking lot:** each thing the notes said leaves the meeting, with its topic, venue and who arranges it; "Nothing to park this week." only when that line said nothing does. **Before the meeting:** only what the manager does beforehand: each row they owe, as overdue or not started, and a step that lets an item open with an answer. **Why this agenda:** three or four sentences in your own voice: which labels the evidence changed and why, and what matters most in the room.

In a thin week every point says what is not known, asks one question in the draft's own terms and says what to listen for in the answer, with no opener and no coaching.

## Order of work

1. Whatever was pasted, a record included, first call read_reference on `references/worked-week.md`, once and on its own: four short cases (the second is the redirect) and one invented week answered in full. Take the reasoning, never the people, posts or numbers.
2. Then write the reply. For prep it begins with `## Notes on the week` and nothing comes before it. For the record of a meeting already held there are no notes: the reply begins with `# Not a 1-on-1 prep request` and is one paragraph of prose under 150 words, no lists, no agenda.
