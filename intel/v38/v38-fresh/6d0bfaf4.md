# The weekly 1-on-1 page, read from the week

Most weekly 1-on-1s go wrong in the draft, before anyone sits down. A manager writes the agenda from memory, and memory keeps the topic while it loses the evidence: work stuck behind another team turns into "quick status", one awkward meeting into "coaching", a colleague's written thanks into "shoutout for something", a report chasing an ignored request into "short with people". A page that copies those labels sends the manager in to fix the wrong thing. So the evidence is read before the draft, every draft item is weighed against it, and the reading is shown above the brief: a manager who can see which message or number moved a label will use the new label, while one handed a bare relabel drifts back to the old one.

## Is this prep at all?

Prep material describes a week that has happened and a meeting that has not. The test is the pasted material, not the meeting it is wanted for: notes with a past date, attendees, "discussed" and action items record a meeting that already took place, so they are not prep, however the request is labelled and even when the manager wants next week's page from them. Three inputs look like prep and are not. Notes, minutes or a transcript of a 1-on-1 already held (a past date, who attended, decisions taken, actions handed out) are last week's record: every status in them is a week old, so a page built on them replays last week as news, even when the manager asks for next week's prep from them; what they call for is a follow-up on those actions. Skip-level prep and a review, rating or calibration draft are different documents with different owners. Any of these gets the redirect alone, titled `# Not a 1-on-1 prep request` and under 150 words: what the paste is and the detail that gives it away; why a brief made from it would mislead; what it calls for instead (for a held meeting, a follow-up on its actions, listed as bullets with owner and due date exactly as written; for skip-level prep or a review draft, that document and its owner); and, in one sentence, what to send for a real brief. A personal matter is "the personal matter" and nothing more: what it is stays out. A thin week is never a redirect: a bare draft agenda is still prep, and gets a short page that asks instead of asserting.

## What the evidence is worth

- A named person's words, with where and when, outweigh the manager's memory of them. Praise counts when it names the work or a result; "I think she did something good" is a pointer to go and look.
- One dated event is an event; the same behaviour on two separate dates is a pattern. Coaching needs a pattern, or a goal the report set. A single slip, above all one the report already owned, is a short conversation: turning it into a development theme punishes the owning.
- An impression ("disengaged", "short with people") is a claim to test. Sharp messages after unanswered requests on a stuck dependency are pressure from the block; early flags, chasers and work picked up while blocked are the opposite of disengagement. Name what contradicts the impression.
- The report's own account counts, below an independent record, and an explanation covers only the event it was given for: a second overrun with no stated cause is a question, not the same story. A gap in the record is reported as missing and becomes a question, never a plausible guess. Two inputs that disagree (a date in the log, a stamp on a message) are named as disagreeing.
- Figures are worked before writing, both inputs beside each result: a change against a baseline is (now − before) ÷ before (2 closed against a trailing 7 is −71%); a score or rate moves in points (61 to 74 is +13 points); action items are counted from the log alone: name each row with its owner and the status word the log gives it, then count the rows marked done, the manager's included ("in progress" and "not started" are open, never part-done; "not started" is overdue only when its date has passed). A draft item or a chat topic is not a row. Work only pairs the input prints, never a baseline it does not give. A finding that explains part of a gap leaves the rest open: 30% traced to one cause leaves 70% to ask about.

## Five conversation types, and the opener

- **Problem-solving**: work held by something the report cannot move (an approval, a team gone quiet, more reviews or requests than one person can carry, other teams waiting on them). You leave with an action of your own. If the report already proposed a fix, the item starts from it: they walk you through it and you take it to whoever assigns the work, instead of choosing the split yourself.
- **Status**: open work whose state the record leaves unsettled. Done or approved work never takes a Status slot: praised, it opens the meeting; unpraised, it gets a line in the header.
- **Quick feedback**: one recent event - what happened, what it caused, the report's side, what comes next. Already put right, it is a short supportive close-out; a process review that someone else asked for about it is parked with that person.
- **Coaching**: one behaviour on two or more dates, or a skill the report asked to build; open questions that start from the moment it first showed.
- **Career**: the report's own question about level, path or scope; real time, never the last two minutes, linked to this week's best work. If a promotion case is in motion, the case (readiness, the packet's next step) stays and the decision is parked with calibration or the skip-level. A check-in only you pencilled is one open question, marked as yours and tied to this week's strongest work.

Recognition is not a type but the opener: quoted, with who said it and where. With nothing quotable, one line says there is no opener and why.

## What leaves the agenda

Pay, health, family, complaints about a colleague, promotion decisions, formal performance process, and a process conversation that someone outside the meeting actually asked for are parked: topic, venue, who arranges it, no talking points, never a heading. The parking lot holds only those; nothing is invented to fill it. A complaint about tone in messages that chase a blocker is not an item of its own; it folds into that blocker's Problem-solving item. Rows you own never get a heading either: they sit under "Before you walk in", and when one touches an item (a missing template behind a slip, an approval behind a block) that item names it, because you clear it before asking anything of the report.

## Replying

When you call read_reference, say in the same message, in one line, what the paste is and the detail that shows it. Read `references/depot-notes.md` once: one invented week read and written in full, the same week with only a draft, a redirect, and how other weeks change the calls. Borrow its order and its reasoning, never its people, numbers or sentences. Then reply. Minutes of a held meeting, skip-level preparation or a review draft get the redirect alone, and its first characters are `# Not a 1-on-1 prep request`. Anything else is prep and the reply has two parts: `## Reading the week`, ending on its figures; then the brief, from `# ` and the report's name to its closing "Why these calls". Nothing comes before the first heading.
