# Preparing a weekly 1-on-1 brief

A manager's draft agenda is written from memory at the end of a busy week; the signals are what happened. I read the signals first and the draft second, because a brief earns its keep on the items that move when the two meet: a "status check" that is really a block only the manager can clear, "coaching" resting on one bad afternoon, a shoutout half remembered while the colleague's thank-you sits in the pasted log, finished work still listed as status. Notes of a meeting already held, a skip-level request or a review draft get a short redirect, not a brief.

## What the week actually shows

Before touching the agenda I read every signal and settle the one or two findings that matter most. The lead is usually the adverse one (a stall, a block, an overdue item, a figure off its baseline), told with its cause and who holds the next move: often not the report, sometimes the manager, whose own open items go in the header because the report has noticed them. Good news gets its own line so it neither softens nor hides the problem.

Every figure is read against its baseline, both numbers in the sentence:
- change = (this period - baseline) / baseline: "14 late against 8 last week, up 75%".
- completion = done / total: "2 of 6 action items done (33%)". Only items marked done count; in progress, overdue and not started do not, whoever owns them.
- overrun = (actual - estimate) / estimate: "4 days estimated, 7 taken, 75% over".
Whole percents, rounded half up at the end. Someone's estimate is quoted as theirs; a figure the input lacks is not computed or guessed. When the input is thin, the header names what is missing and what to pull first; an item with nothing to anchor it says so and asks the report to bring their own example, and the brief gets shorter rather than filled.

## Testing each draft item against the signals

Each item gets exactly one of five types, from the evidence, not from the manager's label or priority. Where they differ, the brief says so in one plain clause naming the signal that moved it.

- **Recognition** needs something to name: who said what and where, what shipped, the figure it moved. A manager's "great work" or half memory is not it, though what impressed them often sits in the input unrecalled, so look. When nothing in the input praises a result, there is no opener and one line says so; praise with nothing behind it is heard as filler.
- **Status** is work still in flight. Finished work is not a status conversation: recognition when praised, a header line when not. A blocked item stays Status and its talking point is the manager's move to clear it; asking someone to explain a stall another team owns tells them the thread went unread. A load the report flagged (too many reviews or requests) is the manager's Status decision, not feedback, ranked by how many people it holds up.
- **Quick feedback** is one incident, especially one already owned: name it, agree the fix, move on. Turning a first, owned mistake into a development theme punishes the owning. Several messages in one thread about one stall are one episode; when they react to a real block, the block comes first.
- **Coaching** needs a pattern (the same behaviour twice or more, in separate situations) or a development signal (the report's own reflection, a goal they set). A manager's impression alone is neither. When the signals contradict it (the person flagged the block early, chased it, kept working meanwhile), the item is reclassified with the reason stated, the impression gets no talking point, and what read as distance folds into the Status item behind it. When coaching holds, aim it at the cause the signals show, which may not be the manager's word for it.
- **Career** is the report's own question about path, level or a programme, asked with this week's work as evidence. It gets a real slot even when the draft squeezes it to the end. The work toward a promotion is discussed here; the decision itself is routed.

## Topics that leave the meeting

Some topics never get a talking point: pay, health or family matters, a promotion decision that runs through calibration, a dispute that needs the other person in the room. Each goes to the parking lot with the venue that owns it and who takes it there: a pay conversation with HR, the calibration or skip-level slot, the other party's own meeting, an async thread. Private detail is not repeated; "a personal matter she raised" is enough. With nothing to park, one line says so.

Three inputs only look like prep: notes or a transcript of a meeting already held, prep for a skip-level, a performance-review draft. Notes with their own date, attendees and actions already assigned are a meeting already held, even when the request says to prep next week from them. Each gets a short redirect instead of a brief, 60 to 180 words: what the material is, where it belongs, the open actions it records with owner and due date, any personal matter sent to a private conversation, and what to send for next week.

## Writing it

Run the items in the order the meeting should take: recognition when earned; then what the report needs the manager to unblock; then the report's own topic; then feedback or coaching; then quick status. The order itself shows the reasoning, so the brief does not announce it. Talking points run at most three sentences per item, each pointing at a named signal. Coaching and Career points are open questions naming the thread, ticket or pattern they come from; a question that fits any report at any company has no place here. Let each point argue its own facts instead of copying the last one's sentence shape. 350 to 700 words, a thin week fewer; the reply is the brief itself, from its title line.

## Order of work

1. In the same message where you call read_reference, write short working notes: whether the input is prep at all; each draft item with its label quoted as the manager wrote it, the signal that decides it, its type and where it lands; every derived figure worked out, with the action log counted row by row by its status word; people keep the role the input gives them, and a stated plan stays a plan.
2. Call read_reference on `references/worked-weeks.md` once. It holds complete briefs for two invented weeks, a thin week and a redirect. Take their shape and their checks, never their facts or sentences.
3. Every draft item appears in the brief once: as its own agenda item, as the opener, in the parking lot, or in one line saying why it was dropped; an item never disappears into another. A changed type's italic line quotes the draft's own words; when the draft's label already fits, there is no italic line, and the brief never describes a label the draft did not use. A check-in the manager added asks one question built on the report's strongest named signal this week. Every date, name and figure in the brief is the input's.
4. When it returns, send the brief and nothing else. Its first characters are `# ` and the title; the notes are not repeated, summarised or introduced. A brief closes with a short **Why these calls** paragraph in plain words: the two or three calls that changed and what in the week changed them, written for the manager, not as a list of rules.
