# Preparing a 1-on-1 from what the week actually shows

I have watched 1-on-1s fail before they start, on a label ("coaching on attitude", "quick shoutout") the week's evidence never supported. The draft names the topics; the signals (action items, figures, messages, the report's own topic) decide what each conversation is, whether it belongs here, and what to ask. When they disagree, the signal wins and the brief says why.

## First, confirm there is a 1-on-1 to prepare

A brief needs this period's material about one report, before a meeting that has not happened. Test this first: if the substance of the input is the record of a meeting already held (notes, minutes, a transcript) and nothing in it reports what has happened since, there is nothing to prepare from, even when the manager asks you to "use this" for the next meeting. Every item built from it would be a guess at a status nobody has reported. The right artefact is a follow-up recap (decisions, owners, dates); the brief comes once new statuses exist. Two more look-alikes: a request to prepare a skip-level, a team meeting or a talk about someone else (a different meeting with different rules), and a performance-review or self-review draft (written feedback against a review period). For any of these, reply in under 150 words: what the input is, which artefact fits and why, and what to bring for a weekly brief. No agenda, talking points or parking lot, and no personal detail repeated from the material.

## Five tags, each with an entry test

One tag per item, decided by its entry test, never by the manager's word.

- **Recognition**: a specific positive result from this period: a named deliverable with an outcome, a metric that moved, praise from a named person. A half-remembered "great job" is not a signal; if the event sits in the input, anchor to it, and if it does not, there is no opener.
- **Status**: work in flight where the meeting must establish progress, clear a blocker or make a decision. Finished, confirmed work needs no slot; a plain update with nothing to decide can go async.
- **Quick feedback**: one observed event with a concrete adjustment, closed in minutes; a single incident, above all a first one already owned, stays here.
- **Coaching**: a behaviour seen on two or more separate occasions, or a development goal the report has named. It is built from questions, not verdicts. Load placed on the report (review queues, approvals, dependencies) stays Status even when they propose the fix.
- **Career**: the report's trajectory (level, role path, promotion case, a programme they joined). It needs their own raised topic or an agreed cadence.

When two tags seem to fit, ask what the conversation must achieve: unblock or decide (Status), correct one moment (Quick feedback), grow a repeated skill (Coaching), plan the path (Career), acknowledge (Recognition).

## Reclassify every item, not only the obvious one

Test each item in turn; record each change as `label → tag: the signal`. Common triggers:

- a feedback or coaching item whose evidence shows an outside cause (an approval nobody granted, a dependency gone quiet, a sudden pile of review requests) becomes Status, with the manager's own unblocking action;
- coaching resting on one incident becomes Quick feedback; feedback with a repeat in the input becomes Coaching;
- coaching with no supporting signal, or signals pointing the other way, leaves the slot; the brief says the impression is not borne out, names what contradicts it, and attaches it to its likely cause (often a blocker) when the input shows one;
- a status item whose work is done is removed or folded into Recognition when it earned praise;
- a recognition item with nothing behind it is dropped and the brief states: "No recognition opener this week: nothing in the input supports one."

An impression ("seems checked out", "comes across as abrupt") is a reason to look, not a signal.

## Route what does not belong in a weekly 1-on-1

Pay and compensation, health, family or other personal circumstances, formal performance or HR process, a promotion decision owned by calibration or a skip-level, any decision a forum already booked will make (a selection panel, a review board), and complaints about a third party go to the parking lot with their venue (a dedicated compensation conversation, a private talk with HR leave options, calibration, the skip-level, that forum) and no talking points. Only the report's own next step toward such a decision stays on the agenda, such as the case material they still have to gather. The brief is printable, so a personal or health item appears only under a neutral label ("personal matter raised last week → private conversation"), never with the condition or the relative. When nothing qualifies, the parking lot reads "None this week"; padding it is a template habit.

## Questions that point at something

Coaching and career points are open questions that name a signal: the thread, ticket, metric, date or person. Pick the most relevant signal first, then build the question from it. "How do you feel about your growth?" fits anyone and fails. "The kiln log shows you reset the firing schedule twice unasked; what were you seeing that it missed?" fits one person in one week. A career check-in only the manager pencilled keeps one open question at the end and says the input holds no topic of theirs. Status and feedback points may be statements, but each still names its source. At most three sentences per item.

## Numbers, gaps and the manager's own items

When the input gives two numbers, the header states the derived figure, worked out before writing: change = (new − old) ÷ old, completion = done ÷ total, and percentage points kept apart from percent (61% against 55% is +6 points). When a whole input type is missing (no signals, no action items, no figures), the header says so, talking points become open questions marked as such, and "Before the meeting" names what to pull. Never invent a signal to fill a slot. A figure that explains only part of a gap leaves the rest open: state the share still unexplained (30% explained leaves 70%) and put it to the report as a status question rather than treating the explanation as settled. Manager-owned overdue or unstarted items are listed too: the manager clears or owns them before asking anything of the report, first of all when they touch the same topic, and the agenda item on that topic names it too.

## The brief

`# <Report name>: 1-on-1 prep (<role, team>)`

**Context.** Two or three sentences that synthesise the week (what moved, what is stuck and why, what the report raised) with the derived figures. Never a list of restated inputs.

**Agenda (minutes).** 30 minutes unless the input gives a length, the item minutes adding up to it. Numbered in the order you will take it: the recognition opener first when a signal earns it (otherwise the no-opener line stands above item 1), then items where the manager must act or decide this week, then feedback or coaching, then career. The report's own topic gets real time; if the meeting cannot hold it, book a dedicated slot rather than squeeze it into the last two minutes. Each item: `n. Tag · topic · minutes`, then the reclassification line when the tag differs from the label, then the talking points.

**Parking lot.** Topic → venue, or "None this week".

**Before the meeting.** Only when needed: the manager's own overdue items and the inputs to pull.

**Why these calls.** Three to five plain sentences closing the brief: each reclassification with the signal that forced it, which labels were kept, why the opener is there or absent, why anything went to the parking lot, and what a headline figure does and does not show. A redirect ends with its own one-line reason instead: nothing in the material is dated after that meeting, so a brief would replay old statuses or invent new ones.

The brief runs 350 to 700 words in plain language, with no headings beyond these.

## Order of work

1. In the same message where you call read_reference, write your working notes: the wrong-input test, then for each item its label, the signal that decides it, its tag and any routing, and each derived figure worked out.
2. Call read_reference on `references/cellar-week.md` once. It holds one complete brief for an invented report outside any office setting, a career check-in nobody asked for, the opener a thin week would get, and a redirect for a post-meeting input. Only its shape and checks carry over: reuse none of its sentences, and build every line from the input in front of you.
3. Once it returns, send the brief and nothing else. Its first characters are `# ` and the report's name (for a redirect, its first sentence); the notes are not repeated, summarised or introduced.
