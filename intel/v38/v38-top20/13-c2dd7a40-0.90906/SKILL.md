# The five-test draft audit for weekly 1-on-1 prep

A weekly 1-on-1 is the report's meeting. The draft agenda a manager jots down is a list of hunches, and hunches miss in the same few directions: frustration with someone who is blocked comes out as attitude, one rough moment comes out as a pattern, finished and praised work gets filed as a status check, an overload the report flagged gets filed as feedback. The week's evidence decides. A good page says what the week showed, audits each draft item, names where the draft missed, and leaves one question per item. The audit is written on the page, in the "your draft, checked" paragraph, so the reply opens on its title.

**First decision: page or redirect?** Settle it before the first word; the title commits you. Prep looks ahead from what changed since the last meeting. A record of a meeting that already happened (minutes or notes listing who came, what was discussed and which actions were agreed, or a transcript) is not prep material, even if the manager asks for next week's prep built from it: it captures last week's conversation and calls for a recap of decisions and owners. Skip-level prep serves another audience; a review draft or rating belongs to the review cycle. Each gets the redirect title, no page. A forward-looking request with little signal is still prep.

## The five tests

**Who caused it?** Feedback concerns something the report did or made. If the cause lies elsewhere (an approval still pending, another team gone silent, something the manager owes, scope that grew, work handed to them), the item is Status: the meeting deals with the unblock, not the person. A sharp message written while chasing a block is part of the block: Status, and the page leaves the tone out.

**Count the instances.** A single observable event, however visible, is Feedback. Coaching needs a pattern the input records (the same behaviour on two dated occasions, or a trend in the figures) or a skill the person is deliberately building. An impression with no instance under it is neither; when the signals contradict it (work delivered, blocks chased, praise), the item takes the tag they support. A repeat with an outside cause is coached only on what the report controls.

**Whose question is it?** Growth, level, path, scope and any programme the report chose (a mentoring scheme, a course) make an item Career. When the report brought the topic, it gets set time. Calibration decides promotion, so the item weighs evidence and gaps without promising.

**Can you name the win?** Recognition rests on a named person's praise or on a figure that went up. A manager who remembers being impressed but not by what has usually missed a signal in the input; find it. If a named senior person praised a finished piece, it opens the meeting even when the draft listed it as a status check. With no win you can name there is no opener, and a single line explains why.

**Who else belongs in the room?** Pay, health, family, formal HR or legal matters, a conflict needing both parties, and promotion decisions have their own venues. A printed page sits on desks, so each becomes one pointer line with a venue and no talking points. Workload and capacity stay on the agenda as Status. The manager's own overdue promise is never parked; it is Status, near the top.

## Your draft, checked

Take the draft items in the manager's order and give each one plain sentence: what the deciding test turned up and where that leaves it. "Your shout-out stands; the win has a name: the councillor's Tuesday email." "On the feedback about his tone, asking who caused it leads to the kiosk repair waiting on you, so it is Status." "On the coaching about late openings, counting instances finds two, the 4th and the 11th, so Coaching stands." Report what the test found, not that an item passed or failed. Every draft item gets a sentence, so none disappears; a parked one says so. Two draft items about one thing share an agenda item and a sentence. When the draft is all the input there is, the paragraph is two plain sentences: nothing this week can test the items, so they keep the manager's wording, and anything sensitive moves to its own room.

## Order of the meeting

An earned opener first; then what the manager owes; then blocks and overdue work, with anything that could be a message kept to one line and closed items dropped unless they carry the win; then feedback and coaching. The report's own topic closes the list with its time slot stated.

## The page

The page starts with the title `## <name>, <role> · weekly 1-on-1` and has nothing above it: no plan, no run-through of the tests, no list of signals.

1. **The week.** Two to four sentences of synthesis: the main thread, what the manager owes, which inputs are missing, and any derived figure with its working shown so it can be checked: "9 orders against a usual 12, down 25% (3 of 12)"; "from 40 to 46, up 6 points". Describe each action item by its state in words (order submitted, training at 1 of 2, your approval overdue) rather than adding them into a count. When the input prints figures (a table row, a current figure beside an average or a prior one), the week's first sentence carries the biggest change in digits with its working: a row reading "this period 6, average 8" becomes "6 against an average of 8, down 25% (2 of 8)". Keep states, owners and dates exactly as the log gives them ("due on the 3rd", "not started"); never turn them into days or weeks overdue. An item the manager owes that is overdue or not started gets its own Status item on the agenda, never the parking lot.
2. **Your draft, checked.** The paragraph above.
3. `### Agenda`, then each item under its own heading in the order above, `#### 1 · <item> (<Tag>)`, followed by at most three sentences tied to signals the input names (who, what, which day or figure). Coaching and career items end on an open question about one specific thread; feedback says what happened, what it cost, and asks one question; status names the decision or unblock needed. With no nameable win, the agenda opens with `No recognition opener: <reason>.` One draft item becomes one agenda item; its evidence and its open question sit under the same heading.
4. `### Parking lot`: one line per routed topic with its venue, or "None this week".
5. `**Walk in with:**` one closing sentence naming the decision or commitment the manager must bring into the room this week, in this week's terms.

Aim for 420 to 560 words; below 350 or above 700 is out of bounds. Thin input still gets a full page and is the one most likely to run short: the week names the missing signals, the draft check is two plain sentences, nothing is tagged Coaching, a career topic keeps its agenda slot, each question is put to the report and collects what is missing, and a short "Before you go in" list names the inputs to pull. Never add a person, day, quote, figure or duration the input lacks, and never state that something happened when the input only says it might.

The redirect is shaped differently: a `## Redirect: <what the input is>` heading, then two or three paragraphs totalling no more than 150 words that say why this is not prep, which artefact fits and who owns it, and what the manager should send instead. Any personal matter is mentioned only as private, and none of the page's sections appear.
