# Walking a manager into a 1-on-1 with the week's evidence

I have watched 1-on-1s go wrong before a word was said: a "feedback" item that was really a blocker the manager owed, praise neither person could point to, a career question the report had rehearsed for weeks squeezed into the last two minutes. Each came from trusting the draft agenda's labels. A good brief reads the week first and lets the evidence decide what each item is. I make the calls below in this order.

## 1. Is this a prep job at all? (decide before opening anything else)
I ask what the material is, not how much of it there is. A draft agenda or this week's signals for a meeting still to come is prep input, however thin: I write the brief. The record of a meeting that already happened - its notes, minutes, transcript or action list - is not prep input even when the request says "prep" or "use this for next week": it needs a follow-up (a recap with owners and dates), and a brief built on it would only replay last week. Preparation for a skip-level and a performance-review draft are other jobs too. For any of these I open no reference and write no brief, only a redirect: a title `# Not a prep brief: <what the material is>`, then three to five sentences on what the material is, why a brief is the wrong artefact, the right next step, and what this week's prep would need.

## 2. Does each topic belong in this room?
Pay and compensation, health and family matters, legal or HR questions, the content of a formal review, and conversations that need other people present are acknowledged, not worked. They go to the parking lot with the venue that owns them, get no talking points, and never appear as numbered agenda items. A salary question is never a career item, even when the report raised it.

## 3. The evidence names the conversation; the label is a first guess
Every remaining item gets exactly one of five types:
- **Recognition**: a specific, attributable positive signal from this week - named praise, a shipped result with its outcome, work someone else relied on.
- **Status**: where a piece of work stands and what it needs - a decision, an unblock, a date. Done work has nothing left to check; it moves to the header or into recognition. A check-in with no signal behind it (workload, "how is it going") is status too, and so is a manager's impression of the report's attitude that no signal supports: the item becomes a status question about the work, headed Status.
- **Feedback**: one observed event of the report's own behaviour, recent and concrete, with an impact to name and a fix to agree. A single incident stays feedback however vivid it was.
- **Coaching**: a pattern (the same behaviour seen at least twice in the input) or a development goal the report has named. An impression with nothing behind it in the signals does not earn coaching.
- **Career**: the report's own question about growth, level, promotion or path, or a development programme they have joined (mentoring, a course), even when the manager calls it a check-in. A promotion is decided elsewhere, but preparing its evidence is career work.
A problem in the work around the report (an overload they flagged, a stuck approval, a broken process) is status, whatever the label says. I test every item, not only the ones that look wrong, and never give one item two types. When a label falls, the item takes the type of what the signals do show, or merges into the item that already covers that signal, and the change is written beside it.

## 4. Recognition is evidence, not courtesy
The brief opens with recognition only when the input holds a specific positive signal: who said or saw what, about which piece of work. If the manager cannot recall what the praise is for, I look for the signal in the input and use it when it is there; generic praise is replaced by that signal or dropped. With no signal there is no opener, and the agenda's first line says so. A manufactured win tells the report the manager was not paying attention.

## 5. Every point points at something
Each talking point names its anchor: the ticket, thread, message, figure or action item it comes from. Coaching and career points are open questions about that anchor, never statements, and never a question that could be put to anyone at any company. The header synthesises rather than restates: what moved this week, what is blocked and on whom, what the manager still owes (an action item assigned to the manager and not done; nothing else counts as owed), and every figure the input lets me derive - each this-period number beside a baseline (a trailing average, last period, a target) becomes a change, (new - old) / old, and a count of parts becomes a completion rate, done / total - computed once and checked. When the draft agenda gets a call wrong - a label the evidence overturns, a topic that leaves the room - the header's last sentence says so plainly. When the input is thin, the header names the missing inputs and what to gather, and the questions stay open; an invented signal is the worst error a brief can make.

## 6. Order on purpose
Recognition opens when it is earned. What the report asked to raise and anything blocking their work come next, while there is time to act; feedback and coaching follow once the report has been heard, because a blocked report hears critique of their tone as blame. A career topic the report raised gets real time, not the last minutes.

## Writing the brief
- `# 1-on-1 brief: <report's name>`, then one line with role, team and meeting length if the input gives them.
- **Context**: three to five sentences, as in step 5.
- **Agenda**: one line giving the reason for the order (or `No recognition opener: <why>.` followed by that reason), then numbered items - the manager's items and the report's own topics, plus a blocker only when it needs a decision this week - headed `**<n>. <topic> - <type>**`, where the type is the one the evidence earns. Only when it differs from the manager's label, the next line reads `Was: <label>. <the signal that decides it>.` The heading must agree with its reason: one event means Feedback, a pattern means Coaching. Then two or three bullets led by Signal, Say, Ask or Decide, one sentence each.
- **Parking lot**: each parked topic with its venue and who arranges it, or one line saying nothing is parked.
- 350 to 700 words in all, ideally 450 to 600. A thin week still reaches 350: the header names each missing input and what to gather, and each item adds what the manager should listen for. No closing summary, nothing after the parking lot.

For a prep job, open `references/worked-brief.md` once with read_reference before writing. It turns one invented week into a brief, then shows how the brief changes when the evidence changes. Take its method, never its names, figures or sentences.
