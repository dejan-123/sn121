# The weekly 1-on-1 brief: signals first, labels second

A manager's draft agenda is written from memory and mood ("she seems disengaged", "quick status check", "shoutout for something last week"). The action-item log, the ticket figures and the Slack threads were written while the work happened. When I coach managers, most bad 1-on-1s trace back to a brief that trusted the draft over the record. So the brief reads every draft item against the signals, lets the signals set its class and its place, and says in one plain line when it overrules the draft.

## Eight readings that change the agenda

1. **Done is not status.** A "status" item whose work the log marks DONE has nothing to check. If someone praised it, it becomes recognition; otherwise it drops.
2. **Friction usually has a blocker under it.** When a report's sharp messages were chasing an approval or a reply that never came, the item is the blocker, and removing it is the manager's job. The draft's feedback on tone drops, because escalating a stalled request was the reasonable move; keep a tone item only if the signals show the same behaviour in unrelated threads.
3. **One stumble is not a pattern.** Coaching needs a repeat (two sprints over estimate) or the report's own account of a recurring cause. A single rough moment in otherwise strong work gets, at most, one question about the follow-up it left open. A first slip the report has already owned is a short supportive item, never a coaching item, and any process conversation someone else asked for goes to the parking lot.
4. **A feeling needs evidence.** Test "disengaged", "slow" or "short with people" against the log. If the log shows the opposite (blockers flagged early, other work picked up while waiting), say so and drop the label.
5. **Blast radius sets priority, not the label.** An item tagged low that is blocking other teams goes near the top.
6. **Praise needs a witness.** Recognition opens the meeting only when a named signal earns it (who said what, where). If the manager half-remembers something, find it in the signals and name it; if nothing earns it, there is no recognition item. Last week's figures, already discussed, are not new praise.
7. **The manager's debts come first.** An overdue deck, feedback not started, an escalation promised: the manager reports on these before asking the report for anything.
8. **Live work the draft forgot still gets a slot** when it has a dated next step (two of four sessions done, the third rescheduled), and the report's own topic keeps its place and its wording.

## What this meeting is not for

| Topic | Where it goes |
|---|---|
| Pay, bonus or salary | a dedicated compensation conversation on HR's cycle |
| Health, family or personal circumstances | a private conversation, with leave or flexibility policy if needed |
| A process dispute that involves someone else | a separate conversation with that person |
| A formal performance concern | the performance process, with HR |
| A career question that needs more than ten minutes | a dedicated career session; keep a short slot here |

Routed topics go to the parking lot with their destination and no talking points. A mistake the report already owns (a first slip, already being fixed) is one short, supportive item; if someone else asked for a process conversation about it, that conversation is routed. Do not pad the parking lot: if nothing belongs there, write "Nothing this week."

## Figures in the context header

The header synthesises; it does not restate the input. It names which draft items are already done, the week's main theme, and every figure that explains the week (a throughput drop together with its cause). Compute a derived figure only when the input gives both numbers, show both, and keep the arithmetic to one step: change in points (91% vs 85% is +6 points), percent change ((3 − 8) ÷ 8 = −63%), completion (4 of 6 interviews). Round to a whole percent. Never compute from a figure the input does not give.

## When the input is thin or old

If signals, action items or figures are missing, say which ones in the header, write every talking point as an open question, and add a one-line "Before the meeting" note telling the manager what to pull. Last week's notes are last week's: each of their action items becomes a follow-up whose status is unknown and is asked about, not assumed. No invented ticket, date, figure, praise or concern anywhere in the brief.

## The brief

Read `references/worked-brief.md` once, on its own, before writing; take its shape and checks, never its names or sentences. Then reply with the brief alone, in this shape:

```
# 1-on-1 brief: <name>, <role>
## Context
## Agenda
### 1. <Topic> (<Class>)
## Parking lot
```

- **Context**: three to five sentences synthesising the week, with derived figures stated correctly, the main theme, what is already done, and any missing input.
- **Agenda**: items in the order the signals justify. Under each item heading, one plain sentence says which signal drives it and why it sits in that class, naming the source (person, channel, ticket, figure); then one or two talking points, at most three sentences each. Coaching and career points are questions that point at a specific thread, ticket or pattern, never statements or generic questions.
- **Parking lot**: each routed topic with where it goes and why, no talking points; or "Nothing this week."

Keep the whole brief between 350 and 700 words; in a thin week, reach 350 with sharper open questions and the "Before the meeting" list, never with invented content. A plain brief that makes the right calls beats a longer one that makes the same calls. The reply is the brief alone: its first characters are `# 1-on-1 brief:`.
