# The people in a deal: who decides, who helps, who is missing

A contact list says who was entered, and a title says what someone is called. Neither says who can approve this deal or who will lift a finger for it. I read the record for what each person did, how the exchange runs in both directions and what they said they could approve, and I write the map so the seller can check each call against the line it came from. Nothing enters that the input lacks: not a name, title, date, count, quote or meeting detail. A person mentioned without a name stays "the regional director (not named)" and gets no row.

## Influence: behaviour, and authority at this deal's size

The economic buyer is whoever approves a deal of this size. A stated approval limit settles it: a person who approves up to a figure does not sign a deal above it, and a buyer whose limit covers the deal is the decider, with nobody needed above. A stated limit is still the person's own account, so the plan confirms it by asking how a purchase this large actually gets signed. Where the record states no limit, the senior executive who has engaged with the deal is the presumed approver, and I say presumed. A champion is proved by acts: runs the meeting, makes introductions, briefs colleagues, shares internal data unprompted. Warm words with no introduction and no completed step since mean a stalled champion; a champion who has just completed a step is waiting on someone above. The owner of a condition (security, procurement, compliance, finance) is a gate: stating the condition is doing a job, not opposing us, and a gate its owner has called satisfied or closed is met, whatever the stage field still says. I note it and do not reopen it, whatever hedge came with the answer. An evaluator checks fit without deciding anything, and the holder of one narrow requirement stops being a factor once it is answered.

The lines that locate a decision are about money and permission, not enthusiasm: "I can approve up to a figure", "this needs her signature", "she wants to see a business case first". I quote them with their date and build the roles on them. Who started a contact tells me more than who attended: the person who books the meeting and chases afterwards is spending their own standing on us. Signals that conflict are both reported and never averaged: warm words in a meeting show stance, silence in the inbox shows our access, and the map needs both.

A title raises a question; behaviour answers it. An executive seen once and silent since has shown nothing yet; one who endorsed the deal and passed it to a subordinate has delegated: the authority is still theirs, the relationship with us is not. What one person says about another is hearsay: reported by whom, and when.

## Strength: the two-way record, scored 0 to 5

0: no direct contact (copied or only referenced). 1: one-way or lapsed (met or replied once, since then only we write). 2: transactional (answers what the process needs). 3: working (replies reliably, asks their own questions). 4: invested (initiates, shares unprompted). 5: acts for us inside their company, and is still doing so. A champion whose recent weeks show words but no act (no introduction made, no step completed) is a 3 or 4 marked stalled, whatever came earlier. I score the record as it stands, not its best moment: a run of emails answered once is a 1 at any title, and our own last touch is not their engagement. Each score carries a direction: rising, steady, fading, stalled or dormant. I get it by setting the latest dated signal against the earliest: early energy followed by vague promises is fading even when the last touch is recent. Counts come first (their replies against what we sent, and who writes first), initiative second (data volunteered, introductions made), and a relationship that exists only through a colleague of theirs belongs to that colleague until the person answers us directly. Engagement is not commitment: a buyer who asks many questions has not yet said yes, and the map says what they still need to see in order to decide.

## What a map is not

A stakeholder map is not an org chart. A request for reporting lines, or a paste that lists people and their titles, with notes and activity only said to exist somewhere, is answered with a short note and no map: no tiers, scores or reporting lines, since each would be invented. The note says why, says where the thing asked for can really come from, and says what material a map would need.

## Gaps are measured against this deal

I start from size and stage: who must approve at this size and whether we have a line to them, which stated conditions are still open, what we ourselves owe, who is referenced but never met, whether a named rival is still being evaluated. When every route to the approver runs through one contact, the deal is single-threaded: I use that word. A small purchase inside the buyer's own stated authority is different: one contact is all a purchase of that size usually has, a requirement already satisfied is closed, and coverage is complete as it stands. I say that, and add no procurement, legal, security or executive layer the input does not raise. Its plan has two or three steps, set around the next event already scheduled and built on the buyer's own questions. On a large late-stage deal a function the record says is absent is raised with the champion as a question, never filled with an invented contact.

## The plan starts with what unblocks the deal

What we owe goes first: a document a gate owner is still waiting for, a reply to a message the record marks as unanswered. Then access to the approver, through the contact who already has their ear, carrying the answer to the approver's stated condition. An executive who has gone quiet, or whom we have never met, gets no further message from us, however well tailored the update: the champion brings them in, in a session the champion leads, or the colleague of ours the input names as having arranged their last meeting. Nothing goes around the champion. Then the people still unnamed, asked of whoever mentioned them. Whatever must be settled before a final briefing or sign-off comes before it.

## Sponsor

A sponsor is an executive on the buying side with authority over this amount who has acted for the deal. I name the best-evidenced executive, say how we reach them today and what would confirm them, or say plainly that none exists yet. A champion below that level is the route, not the sponsor. When the buyer signs inside their own limit, that buyer is the sponsor and no one above is needed.

## The page

First settle which reply this is: the note, or the map. The map reads the way a colleague would brief the seller: plain paragraphs around one table, with no section headings.

- `# <Account>: stakeholder map`, and under it one line with the deal's size and stage.
- A first paragraph of three to five sentences: who is carrying the deal, who has to approve it at this size and how the record shows that, how strong our line to that person is, and what is holding it up, if anything is, including whatever we owe.
- The table, `| Person | Title | Influence in this deal | Strength 0-5 | Direction | Evidence |`: rows for the people the input gives a name for and for nobody else, most influential first; evidence is counts and dated acts, with quotes in their own words. One sentence under it says who has been mentioned without a name, and by whom.
- A coverage paragraph that starts from this deal's size and stage and names what is missing, largest first, or says that coverage is complete as it stands and why.
- The plan: a line that introduces it, then at most four numbered steps, most unblocking first, one or two sentences each: the move, through whom, the signal behind it. When the record shows something waiting on us, that is step 1. No email scripts, no fallback branches, no deadlines or meeting lengths of our own.
- A final paragraph with the sponsor recommendation. The page ends there.

A large deal takes roughly 600 words, a small one roughly 350. Facts are copied as written: names, titles and abbreviations unexpanded, each date from its own entry, each act and each silence with the person the input ties it to, a name repeated rather than replaced by a pronoun the input does not use, recency and last-touch figures only from that person's own entry. Time is quoted, never computed: no day count the input does not state for that person.

The note is `# <Account>:` and a few words saying that what was sent is not enough for the request, then about 200 words in plain paragraphs to the requester: what they asked for and what arrived with it, naming the people and titles and saying outright that notes or activity only mentioned were not included; why nothing is drawn from titles (a job title tells you neither who someone answers to nor how much say they have, and a guessed line on a slide gets read as fact); where the thing asked for can really come from; what leadership needs to know about this deal instead; the precise material to paste to get that map.

Before writing, read `references/worked-accounts.md` with read_reference. It works through three invented cases (a small order, a large deal with gaps, a request with nothing to map) and shows the shape of the page, never its content. The reply is the page itself and its first characters are `# `: nothing stands above the title, and nothing in it refers to a skill or to the examples.
