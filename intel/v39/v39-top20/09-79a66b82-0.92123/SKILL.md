# Stakeholder mapping from behaviour

A title tells you which seat a person occupies. It does not tell you whether that person will move this deal. Sellers who have lost a deal to "the VP loved the demo" learn to read influence from what people have done: who asked for the first meeting, who forwarded the material, who made an introduction, who answered the same day, and who went quiet. This skill teaches that reading and how to turn it into a map a seller can act on this week.

## Reading influence

Behaviour outranks title. Real influence shows in acts that cost the person something: starting the contact, running the agenda, sharing internal data nobody asked for, forwarding our material upward, introducing colleagues, writing an internal briefing or business case. Polite attendance, one warm meeting and "this looks right for us" are sentiment, not influence. A director who does all of the first list matters more to the deal than a chief officer who did one call.

Authority is a number, not a rank. When someone states a spending limit, compare it with the deal value. If the deal is above the limit, that person cannot be the economic buyer however keen they are; the buyer is whoever signs above the line, and the map has to say who that is and whether we have any relationship with them at all.

Delegation points at the delegate. An executive who tells someone to "take it from here" has made that person the route to them, and has also told you she will not be driving it herself.

Praise is not yet championship. A supporter whose enthusiasm has produced no introduction, no confirmed hand-off and no decision is untested; "leave it with me" with nothing after it is a stall, not a step.

Gates close in writing. A security, compliance or legal reviewer who has confirmed in writing that the requirement is met is a closed gate, even when a stage label written earlier still says the review is pending. Record it as done; it is not a gap. The same holds for a narrow check on a small deal that the checker has called satisfied: it is complete, and it earns at most one light courtesy step, never a demand for written sign-off.

## Scoring relationship strength

Strength is a 0–5 judgment backed by counts and dates from the input:

- 5: starts contact, replies the same day, and has already introduced us to someone or written internal material for us (a business case, a briefing). Forwarding our deck is not enough, and waiting on someone else's sign-off does not lower a champion's own score.
- 4: engaged and responsive now, but untested: support has not yet produced an introduction or a decision. A supporter whose promise led to nothing sits here, with trajectory stalling, not rising.
- 3: a working relationship inside their own part of the evaluation.
- 2: thin or transactional: one positive meeting, a standard form, a brief acknowledgment, a narrow check already satisfied, or earlier warmth that has gone quiet (one reply to several emails, weeks of silence), whatever the title.
- 1: cold: copied on an email, attended without engaging, or leaves our emails unanswered.
- 0: never engaged.

Strength measures the relationship now, not the warmth of one meeting. Someone who has gone quiet for weeks, or answers fewer than half of what we send, scores 2 at most, however positive that meeting was; the sentiment goes in the evidence cell. Read the ratio, not the volume. Six emails sent and one short acknowledgment back is a weak relationship carried by our effort; eleven sent and nine answered is a strong one. That gap between what we send and what comes back is the asymmetry to name. Then read the direction (rising, stable, cooling, dormant) from when the person last did something, not when we last wrote, using the dates or day counts the input gives.

Cite only figures the input gives. If it gives no email counts for someone, cite what they did and when, never a made-up "N sent / M replies". The input does not say what today is, so never write "N days ago" or "in the N days since" for a date; use the date itself, or a day count the input states.

## Gaps come from the deal's shape

Ask what this deal needs to close at its size and stage, with the people already involved, not which personas an enterprise roster lists. A small purchase inside one buyer's own authority, with no procurement or legal step raised, needs no hunt for an economic buyer, no legal contact, no executive above the buyer and no second thread into the team that will use the product: say so in one line, note once that resting on one relationship is normal at that size, and do not ask the buyer to name finance, IT or legal people. When the record itself says no procurement step or legal review has come up, that is the answer, not a gap to confirm. A large deal above the champion's limit needs the person who signs. Someone who is mentioned but never introduced (an unnamed chief executive, "my head of engineering", people at a demo who were never introduced) is a gap to fill through the person who mentioned them; never give them a name or a row in the table. When one person holds all of our access on a deal that needs more than that person, call it single-threaded in those words, in the opening paragraph and first among what is missing.

## Sequencing: what unblocks the deal first

Order actions by what removes the next blocker, not by seniority:

1. Anything we owe that the buyer has made a precondition, or any message of theirs we have not answered.
2. The condition the decision-maker has stated in her own words, or the blocker a contact has named ("Finance has to accept the cost model first").
3. The introduction to whoever signs, brokered by the person who has access.
4. Widening the thread (ask the champion for the names and roles of people who sat in a meeting but were never introduced), and the steps a closed or nearly closed gate still needs.

A reply we owe should carry something the deal needs, such as material that answers the decision-maker's open questions and an offer of a short session the champion hosts, never just a request for an update. Do not spend the first action confirming someone's authority when nothing in the input puts it in doubt; go to the named blocker. A silent executive or a dormant buyer is reached through the champion, with a reason tied to that executive's own question, never by cold email: going around the champion can cost you the champion. A gatekeeper who has gone silent is reached through the engaged colleague who works next to them (the engineer beside the silent security head), not by a third unanswered email. A cooling executive gets a step of her own, built on the question she herself asked. When the record names a competitor in the evaluation, say what waiting costs. Every action names a contact, the signal that justifies it, and what it depends on.

## Executive sponsor

The sponsor is the person whose authority covers the deal and who has said or done something for it. When that person is silent, unintroduced or unnamed, say plainly that there is no active sponsor, and still name the candidate and the route to them. On a small deal the buyer is the sponsor and nobody above them is needed.

## When the request is not a stakeholder map

Some requests ask for a different artefact: a reporting-line org chart of who sits under whom, an email, a forecast. Others name people but include no activity to read. Do not build a map or guess a hierarchy from titles. Write a short redirect instead (under 170 words): what was asked, why it cannot come from this input, where that artefact comes from, and exactly what to paste for a stakeholder map. Do not rank anyone, and name no one as the likely champion or as the person to ask. Its first line is the bold "**Redirect — …**" line, with nothing before it: no "---" line, no note on which kind of request this is.

## How to work

Open references/worked-account.md once, on its own, before writing: it holds a full worked map and three short cases. Then write the map as your reply. Do your reading of the account silently: the message you send after opening the reference is the memo and nothing else, and its first characters are "**Stakeholder Map —" (or "**Redirect —"). No analysis section, no notes and no "---" line come before it.

Write it as a short memo to the seller, in the second person ("your champion", "you have no sponsor today"), the way a senior colleague would brief them, not as a form. The first line is the bold title "**Stakeholder Map — <Account> | <deal value>, <stage>**". After the title there are no bold section labels. In this order:

- An opening paragraph: who really moves the deal, what blocks it, the fact that changes the obvious reading, and what waiting costs. Its last sentence leads into the table.
- The stakeholder table: Contact | Title | Influence tier | Strength (0–5) | Trajectory | Evidence, with a row only for people the input names (a decision-maker known only by role, such as "the CEO", is not a row); the evidence cell cites counts, dates or their words.
- One sentence on roles mentioned without a name, saying they have no rows.
- A paragraph on what is missing for a deal of this size and stage: the coverage gaps, in plain sentences.
- The engagement plan: a line saying the order matters, then numbered steps written as full sentences. Each step names the contact and the signal behind it, and says what it waits on ("once she has it", "in the same conversation").
- A closing paragraph on the executive sponsor.

Stop after the sponsor paragraph.
