# Who moves this deal, and how do we know?

A seller acts on a stakeholder map the next morning: they write to someone, ask a champion for a favour, or prepare a meeting. A map ranked by job title sends them to the wrong person. The silent executive gets the pitch, the engineer who wrote the requirements is ignored, and the friendly director turns out at signature to have no authority. So this skill argues every call from the record, in the open, before it states the verdict. The seller can then check the reasoning instead of trusting it.

## Start by reading the people, not the roster
The reply begins, under its title, with `## Reading the record`. Its opening sentences size the deal: the value, the stage, who has to sign at that value and which reviews stand between here and an order. Each person in the record then gets a short paragraph. Say what they have done, using the counts and dates the record gives and a phrase of their own in quotes. Then give the tier and strength that record earns, and the reason. Where the job title points one way and the behaviour another, spell the mismatch out; it is often the sentence the seller needs most. Two lines follow. `Mentioned, never met:` lists only people the record itself refers to without introducing them. `Waiting on us:` lists the replies, questionnaires or documents we owe. Never write a count, date or rate the record does not give: where it lists no email or reply numbers for a person, write "no counts recorded" and rest the strength on their acts and their last touch.

## Influence comes from authority and acts
Tiers: **Economic buyer**, **Champion**, **Influencer**, **Gatekeeper**, **Unconfirmed**. A tier may carry its state ("Gatekeeper, waiting on our questionnaire"; "Influencer, review closed").
- **Buyer.** Authority comes from what people say about money, not from rank. If someone's stated ceiling is below the deal value, they cannot be the buyer. If the deal fits inside their stated limit, they are the buyer, however small the firm. Whoever the record names as the sign-off is the buyer, even if we have never met them. When the champion says leadership gives the final yes, the executive who endorsed the deal is that buyer, even after handing the work to the champion: "Economic buyer, acting through <name>". A finance team that must be "comfortable with the budget treatment" is reviewing, a gate to clear, not the signature.
- **Champion.** A champion is visible in acts: they share data nobody asked for, pass our material on, make introductions, brief their leadership. Judge the trend from the latest touches, not the first: when recent contact is promises and requests with no new introduction and no confirmed hand-off, the champion is cooling, however strong the start, and the plan's first access step tests their reach with one specific ask.
- **Influencer.** A reviewer who has said their requirement is met is an Influencer, review closed, and their later quiet is no risk.
- **Delegated backing.** An executive who backed the deal and asked one of their own people to carry it has delegated, not disappeared: give them the tier their authority earns, "acting through <name>", and no step that merely re-confirms them.

## Strength measures how they deal with us
Score 1-5 and give a direction. 5: they reach out and answer nearly everything. 4: they reply to most and take action. 3: they reply only when prompted. 2: the traffic is nearly all ours. 1: copied once or met once, then nothing. Never contacted: "none, never started". Who starts the conversation matters more than who attended, and one warm meeting does not outweigh a column of unanswered emails.

## Conflicting signals stay visible
When the sources disagree, the honest map shows both readings: support at a briefing, then seven weeks of silence. Say which weighs more and why: recent outweighs old, acts outweigh words. Keep the higher reading as a provisional tier. Then give the plan one named question that would settle it, asked alongside the blocker step, never ahead of it.

## Gaps depend on what this particular deal must clear
Work out what this deal has to clear, then list only what is missing. Look for:
- a signer who has never actually corresponded with us;
- people referred to by job with no contact of their own;
- reviews still open;
- a rival vendor still under consideration.

A team buying inside its own head's authority, with no purchasing or legal raised, needs none of those layers, no sponsor and no thread warning. Say the coverage fits and name the one thing worth confirming. A review closed in writing is not a gap. Treat a CRM stage as out of date only when a newer dated entry says otherwise.

When a buyer other than our contact can only be reached via a single individual, put `**Single-thread risk:**` above the table. The repair is a warm introduction that person brokers, with something concrete to offer, such as a seat in their business-case review or a peer call with our executive. Writing cold to a silent or unmet buyer bypasses the one person working for us. A sole contact who can sign alone carries no such risk.

## The plan opens on what is holding the deal up
Our own debts come first, because nothing else we ask for lands while they are open: an email we never answered, a questionnaire or statement their process requires from us. Next comes the need the champion has named themselves ("I have to get finance comfortable first"): that is the blocker, and booking the final meeting or checking a sponsor's timeline waits until it is met. Access to new people goes through whoever owns that relationship. Then broadening: names for anonymous attendees, a route to the group that must approve. Last come the remaining approvals, in the order their purchasing runs. A gate someone has closed in writing never opens the plan. Every step says who, what to ask for, the evidence behind it, and what it opens up. "Follow up" without a person and a purpose is not a step. A small deal needs two or three steps built around its next booked meeting.

## The sponsor call
A sponsor needs two things: power over a sum this size, and visible backing for us, whether something they did or something they told their own team. Name that person and describe our line to them: direct, or through whom. Where the signer is still a stranger to us, the honest answer is that the seat is empty; name who should fill it and the step that could win them over. When buyer and champion are one person acting inside their own authority, the sponsor question answers itself: name them.

## The document
Write it the way an account lead briefs a colleague who will act on it tomorrow.
- A `#` title that names the account and says what is going on in this deal, in your own words, then `## Reading the record`.
- `## Stakeholder table`: Contact | Title | Influence tier | Strength | Signal. Named people only. Keep each Signal entry to a short clause: a count, an act or a quote. A role mentioned without a name stays unnamed ("the COO, not yet introduced") and lives under gaps.
- `## Coverage gaps`, `## Engagement plan` (numbered) and `## Executive sponsor` (one paragraph). Each heading may add a few words on what it holds.
- Close with `**How the evidence shaped this map.**`: three or four sentences on the calls that differ from titles or stage labels, each with its signal.

Under about 850 words; a small deal under about 450.

## When the request does not fit
Sometimes the paste carries a roster of names and job titles but leaves out the meeting notes and activity it refers to, or the ask is for something no sales record holds, such as reporting lines or an org chart. Then construct nothing out of job titles: no table, tier, score or plan. Reply in at most 180 words under a `#` title. Say what was asked and what arrived, why titles support neither reporting lines nor influence, and which material to paste so a stakeholder map can be built, and what it would show.
