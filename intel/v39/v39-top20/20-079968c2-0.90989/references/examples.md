# Worked examples: stakeholder maps for an aircraft-maintenance software vendor

Five invented cases. Case 3 shows a single thread to an unintroduced executive; Case 4 a request the map does not draw, answered with a short note and no table; Case 5 an executive who delegated and a stage label the record has overtaken; Case 1 a conflict about one person and a single thread to a dormant approver; Case 2 a small deal kept small. The seller offers aircraft-maintenance tracking software (task cards, component tracking, airworthiness-directive compliance) to regional airlines. Each case shows the pasted input, then the full document. The names, airlines and figures are made up; compare shapes, not facts.

How the parts divide the work. The person-by-person reading carries the reasoning: two plain sentences per person, the first saying what the record shows them doing, the second the tier and strength that earns and why, the way an account lead would explain the call to a colleague ("she has sent us two internal decks unprompted, so she is spending her own standing on this"). The table then only points at the signal in one short clause, except that a conflict is always written out in the person's cell. The strength cell always carries grade, score and trend. The closing paragraph names the labels the record overruled. The plan steps read like the reading: who, what we will ask for, why now, and what it waits on.

## Engagement plan

3. **Barnaby Quarmby:** ask how capital-plan items are approved and whether procurement or IT security reviews come next. Signal: none recorded. After step 1 lands.

## Executive sponsor recommendation

No one on record can sponsor this deal yet. The Director of Maintenance is the likely sponsor because she controls the capital plan, but we know her only through Barnaby's two emails. Barnaby is the path: the benchmark and the joint walk-through he hosts (step 1) are how we learn her name, her criteria and whether she will back it.

**What the record overruled.** Barnaby's title reads as an engineering influencer, but he initiated, ran the demo and is carrying our case to the capital-plan owner, so he is the champion. A CRM with one contact makes this look like a one-person deal; the emails show the money sits with a director we have never met. The "mid-evaluation" label hides that the decision has not reached anyone who decides, which is why the plan opens with the reply we owe Barnaby and the introduction it carries.

## Case 4: a chart request with no activity pasted

**Input.** Thornwick Aviation. Deal: $1.6M ARR, procurement stage. Request: "Can you sketch who reports to whom in their engineering division so I can show my director?" Background: three contacts, Ottoline Rennick (Head of Maintenance Strategy), Rufus Tamberlin (Engineering IT Lead) and Clementine Solvey (Procurement Officer), "plus our call notes". No notes, counts or reporting lines were pasted.

**Reply.**

# Stakeholder map: Thornwick Aviation - what this record can show

You asked for a sketch of who reports to whom in Thornwick's engineering division, to show your director. The paste holds three names with titles (Ottoline Rennick, Head of Maintenance Strategy; Rufus Tamberlin, Engineering IT Lead; Clementine Solvey, Procurement Officer) and mentions "our call notes", but no notes, tallies or reporting lines came with it.

I have not drawn a chart. A job title says nothing about who reports to whom, and nothing about who is moving a $1.6M order at procurement stage; a guessed hierarchy shown to your director would turn into the account plan.

To build the map, paste:
- the call notes, with dates;
- for each of the three, our sends, their replies and the last two-way contact;
- anything said about who signs at $1.6M;
- what procurement still needs before the order.

From that the map shows who approves, who is carrying the deal for us, how warm each line is, which seats are still missing and the first move. That answers the question behind the sketch better than boxes would: your director learns who will actually decide, not only where people sit.

## Case 2: small, early deal, right-sized buying group

### Input (as of 2026-11-18)

Account: Skerrow Island Air (island commuter airline, 46 staff, 5 aircraft)
Deal: $27K per year, task-card and defect-logging module for 11 maintenance technicians. Stage: early, one discovery call and one demo done.

Contacts:
- Casimir Kestrin, Maintenance Controller: came in through our pricing page on 2026-10-26, booked the discovery call, sent four emails with questions about offline mode on the hangar floor and defect logging. Said he can approve software under $35K from his maintenance budget.
- Elspeth Haverick, Technical Records Clerk: joined the 2026-11-09 demo for the records section, asked whether we import their component history spreadsheets, said "fine if it reads our exports", left early. We sent one follow-up; she answered with a sample file the same day.

Notes:
- 2026-10-29, discovery with Casimir: technicians log defects on paper task cards; two overdue component inspections this year. Engaged throughout.
- 2026-11-09, demo with Casimir and Elspeth: Casimir asked for a three-week trial for four technicians. No procurement or legal raised.

### Document

# Stakeholder map: Skerrow Island Air - $27K task-card module

Casimir Kestrin is the sponsor: he found us, owns the budget at this size and is driving the evaluation. No more senior sponsor is needed for a $27K purchase, and adding one would slow a deal he can sign himself.

**What the record overruled.** A controller title might suggest someone who needs sign-off above, but Casimir's stated $35K limit covers the deal, so he decides. A clerk title would usually be peripheral, yet Elspeth holds the one technical condition. The "early" label does not call for procurement, legal or finance steps the record never raises, which is why the plan opens with her file and the trial.
