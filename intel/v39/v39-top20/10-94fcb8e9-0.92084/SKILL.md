# Says who? A stakeholder map built from the record

Sooner or later someone senior points at a row of the map and asks how we know. The only answer that survives is a line from the record: the meeting this person arranged, the replies they sent or never sent, the promise they made and what became of it. So I build the map from conduct and treat the title as a hypothesis to test. Three questions go to every name, always in this order: what has this person done in the deal, what power do they have over it, and what comes back when we write to them. No name, title, meeting, reporting line, date or count goes into the map unless the record holds it, and the map admits it when the record is thin or argues with itself, because a confident call resting on nothing is the mistake a reader cannot catch.

## Conduct, strongest first

What someone did outweighs what they said, and both outweigh having been in the room. At the top: arranging a meeting with a colleague, building something internal for us (a business case, a briefing note), handing over data unasked, carrying our material to their own leadership and reporting back. In the middle: pointed questions, quick answers, a requirement stated or confirmed. At the bottom: attending, being copied, one kind remark. A single warm meeting followed by silence is weak evidence however senior the person. An offer to act ("leave it with me", "I'll raise it upstairs") becomes evidence when it produces a name, a date or a meeting; until then it is an open offer.

## Power over the deal

- **Economic buyer**: releases money at this deal's size. The record must show it: a spending limit they gave, a sign-off others say the deal is waiting for, or a business case they requested before deciding. When a contact's stated limit sits below the deal, the buyer is whoever signs above that line, whether or not we have met them. If nobody qualifies, write *buyer not established* and make finding out a plan step through the champion. A finance team that has to accept how the cost will be booked is reviewing, not signing, unless the record says it signs, and a remark that senior management will want a last look identifies no one.
- **Champion**: pushes the deal in rooms we are not in, and the record shows the pushing. Warm words alone make a supporter. When the champion's offers to take it higher have not yet put anyone new in front of us, keep the label and add *reach unproven*; the plan then asks for the specific meeting.
- **Executive sponsor**: a senior figure who blessed the project and passed it to someone below them ("you run with it"). That is delegation: we reach them through their delegate, and their silence afterwards is normal. A senior person who was warm once, handed nothing on, and has since let our messages go unanswered is a sponsor *gone quiet*, judged on the silence.
- **Evaluator**: tests fit in one area such as technical, security, data, compliance or cost treatment; their test is open or passed, in their own words. A test its owner passed in writing stays passed even if an older CRM stage still calls it pending: name the stage as out of date and ask that person for nothing further.
- **Gatekeeper**: runs a procedural step (procurement, vendor risk, contracts), can halt an order but not choose a vendor. State what the step requires and whether we have supplied it.
- **Blocker**: only on an act against us, such as backing a rival or objecting. Someone who sat silent and has left follow-ups unanswered is *quiet, position unknown*: a question to answer, not an opponent.
- **Named, never met**: anyone who appears in the record only as a name or a role, with no exchange with us. Use the record's wording for them, assign no role from the title, and treat what others say about them as second-hand.

A person may hold two of these.

## The line back to us

Judge each relationship by what returns, not by what we sent. **None**: no exchange. **Cold**: once engaged, now unanswered. **Thin**: one-way or a single exchange, an acknowledgment at most. **Working**: answers within their own remit, nothing beyond it. **Strong**: engages and acts for us unprompted, noting when they have also opened a door to the decision-maker. Quote counts and recency exactly as the record words them ("9 of 11 answered", "last touch 8 days ago"); never compute a gap between two dates or any figure the record does not state. Name a direction when the record shows one: rising, holding, stalled (an offer of theirs with nothing after it), fading. If we owe them a reply, the delay is ours, not a sign they are cooling.

## A record that disagrees with itself

When two recorded signals about one person or item point opposite ways, that is a conflict: a supportive email beside a silent inbox, an offer that a later note shows went nowhere, a CRM stage that a dated, first-hand message contradicts. Cautious wording ("fine for now") is not a conflict. Never settle one quietly: the evidence cell gives both signals and says which counts for more (a deed over a word, the newer record over the older, a count over an impression), the call carries the word provisional, and one plan step exists to settle it.

## What this deal must pass

Begin the coverage section with one sentence on what a deal of this size and stage has to clear, each item anchored in the record: the signature the amount needs, the reviews and gates someone named, the groups a contact said will weigh in. A gap is an item that no one we talk to covers. A role mentioned without a name stays nameless and is approached through whoever mentioned it. A small purchase inside its champion's own limit, with no procurement or legal step raised, is covered: one sentence says so and that a single relationship is normal at that size, and nobody else is sought. On a large deal, steps the record has not reached yet are questions for the champion, not contacts.

**Single-threading** is structural risk, not an ordinary gap: one person holds every route to the buyer, or is the only champion we have. Say it in the opening, give it a bold line before the gap list, and make its repair the first step. If that one person is also the buyer, the risk does not apply, and one sentence explains why.

## Plan order

1. A single thread: widen it through the person holding it (a joint meeting with the buyer, names for the people not yet introduced), and include anything owed to that person in the same message.
2. Whatever we owe (an unanswered email, a questionnaire, a form) goes back to whoever is waiting, since it is wholly in our hands.
3. A silent or never-met senior figure is reached through someone they already know, our champion or someone from our leadership who has met them, bringing what they asked for; a silent reviewer is reached through a colleague of theirs. No cold note over the champion's head.
4. A champion with reach unproven is asked for the specific meeting: who, and when.
5. A stated condition is answered exactly, in words the champion can repeat upstairs; then the gates, then the step that settles each conflict.

Every step names its person or gap, the signal that makes it the move now, what it depends on, and how we will know it worked. "Follow up" is not a step, a passed test is not reopened, and a small deal gets the two or three steps that close it, led from its next booked event.

## Sponsor recommendation

Judge both sides. Theirs: who backs the deal from above right now and on what signal (active, working through a delegate, gone quiet, or no one). Ours: propose someone from our leadership only when the record gives them a counterpart to meet and a way in, preferring anyone of ours who has already met that counterpart; otherwise state frankly what is missing.

## Thin records and requests for something else

A record with little but real activity keeps the full layout at a smaller size and makes fewer claims, not vaguer ones: an untestable call reads "unclassified: nothing recorded", and asking for the missing notes becomes a plan step. A paste of names and titles with nothing they did, which usually arrives with a request for an org chart, gets no map at all: a note of under 200 words says what was asked for, why nothing is drawn, and what to paste instead.

## The reply

`# <Account>: stakeholder map`, then `## The record, person by person`: what this deal must clear, then two or three sentences per person in plain words, most powerful first, saying what they did (with the record's own numbers), what that makes them, where title and conduct disagree and any conflict; close it with the people named but never met, what we owe, and whether one person holds every route upward. Then `## Stakeholder table` (contact, title, influence tier, relationship strength with counts and direction, evidence), `## Coverage gaps`, `## Engagement plan`, `## Executive sponsor`, and two final sentences on the calls that overrule a title or a CRM label. Read `references/maps.md`, then `references/before-you-send.md`, then write the reply, with nothing before its first characters, `# `.
