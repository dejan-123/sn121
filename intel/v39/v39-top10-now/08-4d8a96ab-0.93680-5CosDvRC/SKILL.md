# Stakeholder mapping from account evidence

A title tells you where someone could sit in a purchase; their behaviour tells you where they actually sit in this one. On most enterprise deals the person moving things is a director who books the meetings and forwards our material, while the executive with the big title answered once and went quiet. So I read what each contact *did* before I look at what they are called, and every call I make points at a line in the input. Where the record is silent - whether we answered an email, which pronoun someone uses - I leave it silent rather than fill it in.

## Costly signals decide influence

A signal counts toward influence when it cost the contact something: time, internal data, or standing with colleagues. Costly: setting up or running a meeting, volunteering data nobody asked for, forwarding our material internally, making an introduction, stating a budget limit or approval path, closing a requirement in writing. Cheap: attending, a polite reply, enthusiasm with no next step, a cc line, the title.

Each named person gets one tier:
- **Economic buyer** - controls the money or the final yes at this price. A stated sign-off limit that covers the price settles it: that person is the buyer, and nobody above them needs mapping. A stated threshold the price exceeds, or a contact's word that their leadership or a committee signs, points to whoever sits above, called what the record calls them; a finance lead who must be comfortable with the cost does not sign unless a line says so. If no line says who holds the budget, the buyer is unidentified; I do not promote the most senior name or supply a CFO or board the record never mentions. Someone whose yes covers one condition (a workflow, a security review) approves that condition, not the spend.
- **Champion** - sells for us inside the account through costly actions.
- **Evaluator** - shapes one part of the decision: technical fit, security, compliance, user fit.
- **Gatekeeper** - owns a process step such as procurement or contract review; can delay, rarely decides.
- **Unengaged** - in the record, with nothing to read now: listed but never reached, reached and silent, or a senior who backed us early and went quiet, scored on what came after.

A gate closed in writing stays closed even when an older stage line calls it pending; newest first-hand evidence wins. A form or questionnaire their process needs from us keeps that gate open until we send it; sending it is our step.

## Strength is two-sided and has a direction

Strength measures the relationship, not the person's importance, on 1-5: 5 they act for us and have already opened a door above them (a senior brought into a meeting); 4 they act for us, but no door upward has opened yet; 3 responsive but transactional; 2 we chase and get occasional answers; 1 one-way or none. When the input gives reply counts, they set the ceiling: replies to most of our emails plus initiative of their own can reach 5; about half, 3; one reply to four or more of our emails, 2 at most, however senior the person; never reached, 1. A warm meeting or a thank-you shapes the reasoning, never the number. Where no count is printed, conduct decides and none is written. Their message that we have not answered is ours to answer; it never lowers their score or makes them cooling.

The direction reads the newest lines: rising only when the newest act outdoes the earlier ones - a new person brought in, a dated commitment; steady while the pattern holds, including a champion still working as the decision waits above them; cooling when acts have thinned to words - a promise to raise it internally, material taken upward with no word of where it went - with nothing after; dormant when someone once active has stopped.

## When signals disagree, show it and design a test

I weigh actions over words, first-hand over reported, recent over old, written over spoken. Then I make a provisional call: the tier follows the acts, and the reasoning column names both signals, says which weighs more and points to one ask that would settle it: something only a real champion can grant, and what a yes or a silence would tell us.

## Gaps come from the deal's shape

First I work out who this deal needs: who must approve at this price, who pays, who has to live with the result, who runs each system the product connects to, and which gates the input shows. A gate - security, procurement, legal, vendor risk - becomes a gap only when the input raises it or notes that nobody covers it on a deal big enough to need it. A gate nobody mentioned is one question to the champion inside the plan and never a line in the gaps. A small purchase inside the champion's own sign-off authority needs almost nobody else, and I say so instead of listing executives it will never meet; its plan is the next booked meeting and the signature after it. Someone mentioned but never introduced is a gap: I record the role, who mentioned them and what was said, and never supply a name the input did not give.

## A single thread is a structural risk

When one contact is our only route to the economic buyer, or our only champion, the deal goes dark if they stall, leave or lose an internal argument. I flag it above the plan, and step one repairs it: ask that contact for a warm introduction, folded together with anything we owe them, and make it easy - what we would bring, which of the buyer's questions it answers. That ask waits on nothing else. When the one contact is the buyer, inside their own authority, no thread can break: one sentence on continuity, no flag. Cold outreach to a dormant or never-introduced buyer goes around our champion and spends their trust; if the champion stalls, widen through people already in the deal.

## Ordering the plan

A named competitor, a stated approval rule or an outstanding document is used in the plan or set aside in a sentence.

Something waits on us only where the record says so - "no reply from us yet", a questionnaire still outstanding - and then answering it opens the plan; a question someone sent is not proof that we left it unanswered. Unanswered emails from us are that person's silence, never their neighbour's. After the single-thread repair come our other debts, then the move that clears the current blocker to signature, then the work that depends on it. Parallel work is written as parallel; a step waits only on what it cannot start without. Each step names the contact or role, the signal it rests on, what it waits for and how we will know it is done. "Follow up" is not a step.

## Executive sponsor

The sponsor has authority where this decision is made and has shown support; I cite the signal. When the champion's own authority covers the deal, the champion is the sponsor. Otherwise, if nobody qualifies, there is no sponsor yet: I name the strongest candidate at that level and the route to them. A senior title without support is not a sponsor, and neither is a supporter below the approval threshold.

## When the record cannot carry a map

Some asks are for what a sales record never holds - reporting lines, an org chart, a power ranking - or the notes and activity are mentioned but not pasted. Titles do not show who reports to whom or who matters, and a guess shown to leadership becomes the account plan, so I build nothing from them: no table, tier, score or plan. The note is the whole reply, and its title line says plainly what this record cannot give. Its first paragraph says what was asked and what actually arrived - notes only mentioned did not; the second, why titles cannot answer it, where the true answer lives (the account's own chart, or one direct question to a named contact) and what we can build instead (an influence map read from behaviour); the third, exactly what to paste. At most 220 words.

## How the reply reads

First decide what the record can carry: activity per person gets the map; names and titles alone, or an ask no record answers, get only the note. The map begins at its title line, which names the account. Then three parts.

**How I read the account.** One paragraph per named contact and one per person referenced but never introduced: the evidence first (counts, dates, what they did), then the call, in a few plain sentences. Close with one paragraph on the rest of the input (competitors, stated rules, outstanding documents, overtaken stage lines) and what each changes.

**The map.** A stakeholder table (Name | Title | Tier | Strength and direction | Evidence and reasoning) with a row for every person the input names - a listed contact never reached included, since that empty row is the warning - and only for them. Under it, one sentence names who the record mentions but nobody has met (an officer known by role, unnamed attendees, a team); they are gaps, not rows. Then the coverage gaps, each with the reason this deal needs that persona; the engagement plan as a numbered list under the single-thread flag when there is one; and one executive-sponsor paragraph.

**Why the plan starts where it does.** Two or three sentences.

A small deal needs a few hundred words; a large one stays within about 1,000. Every count, date and silence sits beside the name it belongs to in the record, and an undated remark gets no date.

First read references/worked-records.md: a full map, a small deal and a short note, all invented. Take their reasoning and form, never their facts. Then send your reply as the whole message - the map, or the note when the record cannot carry one. Its first characters are `# ` and the account name; nothing sits above the title.
