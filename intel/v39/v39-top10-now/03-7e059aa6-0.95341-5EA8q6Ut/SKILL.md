# The stakeholder map: rank people by what they did, not by what their title says

A CRM export lists titles, and a title is a claim about influence, not evidence of it. A chief officer who answered once and went quiet is not a sponsor; a director who is on every call, hands over internal data and gets us into rooms is carrying the deal. A seller who plans from titles writes to the wrong person first and learns at signature that the friendly contact could not sign. So I read the record before I rank anyone: what each person did, how often they answer, what they said they can approve. I show that reading first, person by person, so the seller can see why each call was made; the map is then written from it. Nothing is supplied that the record lacks: no name for a person mentioned only by role, no reporting line, no meeting, date or count.

## 1. The reading

Under the title the reply opens with `## What the record shows`. Its first sentences size the deal: the amount, the stage, and what a deal of that shape has to pass (whose signature the amount needs, which reviews the record says stand before an order). Then one short entry per person in the record, two or three plain sentences: name and title, what the notes and counts show them doing (who started the contact, replies against emails sent, time since the last one, their own words in quotes), then the tier and the strength that behaviour earns, with the reason. Where title and behaviour disagree, say so outright; where they agree, rest the call on the signal, not the title. `Named but never met:` then takes everyone the record mentions without introducing them, in its own words: a person known only by role, unnamed attendees of a meeting, a team or leadership group someone says must agree. `Ours to close:` lists what waits on us, and the record often says it only in passing: a latest message noted as still unanswered by us, a document a reviewer asked for and has not received. An answered question is not listed. Where the record gives no reply counts for a person, the entry says "no counts recorded" and rests on their acts and last touch; a count, rate or date the record lacks is never written. A stage label is called stale only when a later dated note from the reviewer contradicts it (a review the label calls pending that the reviewer closed in writing).

## 2. Tier: what a person can do to this deal

- **Decides.** Approval that covers THIS amount: a stated limit, a sign-off others say they are waiting for, the final yes. A stated limit below the deal size moves the decision to whoever signs above it, however senior and keen its holder; a limit above the deal size makes a team head the decider, and nobody higher is needed.
- **Champion.** Works for the deal when we are not in the room, and it shows in acts: shares internal data unprompted, forwards our material, briefs their leadership, makes introductions. Warm words with no introduction or confirmed hand-off behind them are support whose reach is untested; say so, and let the plan test it with one specific ask.
- **Evaluator.** Checks fit: technical, security, compliance. Give the state of the check in their words: open, or cleared. A cleared check is no blocker, and a narrow check that was satisfied does not make its owner a power in the deal.
- **Gate.** Procurement, vendor risk, legal: can hold an order, cannot choose us. Say what the gate asked for and whether we delivered it.

One person can hold two. An executive who endorsed the deal once and left their own person to carry it is a sponsor candidate, not an active sponsor: the tier reads "Executive, endorsed once", the line to them runs through that person, and their strength with us stays low until they answer us themselves. A senior person the record shows doing none of this is an executive by position only, and the map says that.

## 3. Strength: two-way, recent, and which way it is moving

Strength is the relationship with us, on a scale of 1 to 5 with the counts beside it and a direction (warming, steady, stalling, cooling, dormant). 5: they initiate, answer nearly everything, volunteer what we did not ask for, and have got us in front of the person who decides (or sign themselves). 4: the same energy, but we have not yet been in front of the decider. 3: they answer when asked, business only. 2: mostly us talking, one reply or meeting and little since. 1: copied in or met once, then silence. Promises with no act behind them cap the score at 4, stalling. A person we have never contacted gets no score: write "none, never started". Who initiates counts for more than who attended; sitting silent in a meeting and leaving follow-ups unanswered is a 1 or 2.

## 4. Gaps come from the deal's shape

Ask what this deal has to pass, and list only what is missing from that:

- the signature the amount needs, when we have no two-way line to its holder;
- each person named by role with no contact, each team the champion says must have a say;
- each review the record leaves open, and a named competitor still in evaluation;
- one thread: when every live line to a decision made by someone else runs through one person, the first gap reads `Single-threaded on <name>` and says what stops if that person stalls. One contact who is also the decider is not this gap.

Scale decides the rest. An amount inside the buyer's own authority, one team, no procurement or legal in the record: add no finance, legal, procurement, sponsor or single-thread gap and no extra sessions; say coverage fits the deal and name the one thing to confirm. Where the record says the amount needs executive or board approval, an absent finance or legal line is a gap of one sentence, put as a question for the champion, never as an invented contact.

## 5. The plan: most unblocking move first

Each step gives the action, the person (or through whom), the signal that prompts it, what it unlocks and what it depends on.

1. What waits on us goes first: the unanswered message, the requested questionnaire.
2. Then access, through whoever holds the relationship. A decider who is dormant or has never met us is not written to cold: the note is easy to ignore, and it tells the champion we went round them. Ask the champion for one named step and hand them the vehicle: a joint session, a one-page answer to the question the executive raised, a slot in a briefing they already plan. Until that introduction is made nothing goes to the executive from us, not even a note ahead of the meeting. Where our own executive made the first introduction, that executive is the way back in.
3. Then widening: names and roles for unnamed attendees, who that leadership is, the introduction to a team that must have a say.
4. Then the gates, in the order the buyer's process needs them.

A small deal gets two or three steps around its next scheduled event.

## 6. The sponsor call

A sponsor has authority over this amount and has backed the deal by an act, or in words to their own people. Name the candidate on that signal, say how direct our line to them is and through whom it runs. An executive who endorsed once and went quiet is the candidate, not yet the sponsor: say there is no active sponsor, and which briefing of the champion's would bring them back into the room. When the person who must approve has never met us, say there is no sponsor yet, who it has to be, and the step that would earn it; do not promote the champion to fill the space. When decider and champion are one person acting inside their own authority, say no separate sponsor is needed.

## 7. The document

- `# <Account>: <where the deal stands, in a few words from the signals>`, then the reading.
- `## Stakeholders`: a table of Person, Tier, Strength (score and direction), Signal. One row per person in the record; a single person known only by role is a row under that role, marked not in CRM, strength "none, never started"; unnamed attendees and teams get no row: they are a gap and a question for the champion. The Signal cell is one clause: the counts or the quote.
- `## Coverage gaps`, `## Engagement plan` (numbered), `## Executive sponsor` (one paragraph).
- `**Why the map reads this way.**` Three or four sentences: the calls that differ from the titles or the stage label, each with its signal, and what was left out on purpose.

Under 850 words; under 400 for a small deal.

## 8. A request the record cannot support

Sometimes the paste holds names and titles while the notes and activity are only mentioned, not included (say so plainly: mentioned is not pasted), or the request is for something a sales record does not contain: reporting lines, a chart of who reports to whom, a ranking. Build nothing from titles: no table, no tier, no score, no plan. The whole reply is a note of at most 180 words that starts at its title line `# <Account>: ...`, then three short paragraphs: what was asked and what was supplied; why nothing is built (a title gives neither a reporting line nor influence, and a guess shown to leadership becomes the plan); and exactly what to paste so a stakeholder map can be built, with what that map would show. Case four of the reference shows it; read it first.

Then send the reply as the whole message. Do not announce it, mention the skill or the examples, or put a rule line above it: its first characters are `# ` and the account name.
