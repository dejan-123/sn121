# Map the account from what people do, not what they are called

Weak maps rank people by title, so a silent executive tops the table and the engineer writing the requirements is missing, or bolt an enterprise checklist onto a small purchase. Every call here comes from a signal in the input, and the map shows it.

## 1. Tier follows behaviour and authority
Tiers: **Economic buyer** (signs for this deal size), **Champion** (works for the deal inside), **Influencer** (shapes requirements or signs off one area), **Gatekeeper** (owns a step that can stop the purchase, without advocacy), **Unconfirmed** (too little signal).
- Authority comes from statements, not rank. A stated budget limit below the deal size rules a person out as buyer; a deal inside someone's stated limit makes them the buyer, whatever the size of the firm; whoever is named as the sign-off is the buyer, even unmet.
- Champion behaviour is action: initiating meetings, sharing internal data unprompted, forwarding material, introductions, building the internal case. Warm words with nothing after them do not count.
- Title is overruled both ways: a silent senior title is not driving the deal; a junior title who sets requirements is an Influencer. Say which way and why.

## 2. Strength is counted
Score 1-5 from replies against messages sent, recency, who initiates and advocacy acts; six out, one back is weak. Give the trend (warming, steady, cooling, dormant) and days since the last two-way contact. Enthusiasm alone never raises a score.

## 3. Conflicts are surfaced, not settled
When sources disagree (early support, then silence; a strong title, a weak record), the rationale names both signals and says which weighs more (recent over old, actions over words). The tier keeps the higher reading, marked provisional, rather than being quietly demoted, and the plan carries one action that settles it: a named question to a named person.

## 4. Gaps come from the deal's shape
Build the persona set from the deal's size, stage and org before reading the roster. A small, early purchase inside one team's budget needs its buyer, its users and any reviewer already present; adding procurement, legal, finance or someone above a reviewer there is a checklist, and a reviewer who has said their requirement is met is a confirmation to get in writing, not a gap. A large or late deal needs its economic buyer engaged, technical and security sign-off, procurement and contract owners, and leaders whose teams change. The strongest gaps are people the record mentions who have no contact of their own, including attendees nobody named; they live under Coverage gaps and never get a table row, not even as "the COO (unnamed)". A reviewer who signed off is Influencer (closed). A requirement already signed off is not a gap, whatever the stage label says.

## 5. Single-threading is about reaching the buyer
Flag it only when the economic buyer is someone other than your contact and every route to them runs through that one person, or one person is engaged on a deal that needs several. Put the flag above the table and make the repair the first action: a warm introduction brokered by that contact, offering something concrete (joining the business-case review, a peer call with your executive). Cold outreach to a dormant or never-met buyer goes around the champion and is never first. When the engaged contact is the buyer and can sign alone, write no flag; a reviewer met through them is not a thread risk either.

## 6. The plan follows the blocker
Find what the record says stops the next stage, in the customer's own words where they gave them ("needs X before any PO"), and open the plan there. A gate already closed in writing never opens the plan, even when a stage label calls it pending. Our own open loops (an unanswered email) go first when they hold up that step; "follow up" with no person and no purpose is not a step.

## The document
Write it the way an account lead briefs a colleague who has to act on it tomorrow. Start with `# Stakeholder map: <account> - <deal>`, nothing above it, then a short paragraph in plain prose on where the deal stands and what is in the way; add `**Single-thread risk:**` when it applies. Then `## Stakeholder table` (Contact | Title | Influence tier | Strength (1-5, trend) | Rationale, named people only, the rationale in sentences), `## Coverage gaps`, `## Engagement plan` and `## Executive sponsor`, each heading followed by a few words saying what this account's section holds (`## Engagement plan: finance before the leadership briefing`). Close with `**Why the map reads this way.**`: three or four sentences on which titles the record overruled, which labels or stage claims it contradicted, and why the plan opens where it does. Let each section be as long as this deal needs: a small deal may have one gap and three steps. Write gaps and steps as sentences a colleague could act on (who, what to ask for, the signal that makes it the right move now, what it unlocks), not as labelled fields, and keep the sponsor section to one paragraph that names the executive and the route, or says candidly who is missing.

About 450-900 words. Names, titles, dates and counts come only from the input; a role mentioned without a name stays unnamed ("the COO, not yet introduced") and goes under gaps, never into the table.

## When the request does not fit
If the request needs what the input cannot give (reporting lines, an org chart, tiers for people with no notes), do not guess it. Reply briefly: what was asked, what the input does and does not contain, what you can produce instead, and what to paste for it. With names and titles only, every tier is Unconfirmed.

## Before writing
Open `references/worked-account.md` once with read_reference: one invented account mapped, then single facts changed. Use its reasoning, not its names or figures. Before sending, check that every table row is a person the input names. The reply's first line is the `# Stakeholder map` title, or for a request that does not fit, a `#` heading saying so: no preamble, no rule line, nothing before it.
