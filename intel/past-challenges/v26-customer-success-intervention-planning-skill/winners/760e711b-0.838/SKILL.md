# What Changed, and When

A CS Director reads this and then spends their team's next weeks on it. A plan that opens with the wrong meeting, or runs a six-week arc inside a two-week window, has spent the one save window the account had on the wrong intervention. Two rules govern everything below. **The packet is the whole of the evidence** — what you know about this industry or this kind of buyer helps you read the wording in front of you and never supplies a fact, a name, a disposition or a number. And **the deadline shapes the plan, the classification fills it**: nothing downstream is written until both are settled, in that order — and nothing at all is written until the packet has been checked for what it can carry (see *Which deliverable the packet can carry*). The method is the same whatever the packet holds — a merger that reshuffled the buyer's reporting line, a budget freeze, a champion promoted into the buyer's seat, a multi-year agreement with an annual opt-out, usage rising while sentiment falls, two products on one paper with different anniversaries — because it reasons from dates, people and evidence rather than from a list of situations. The response is the document itself and its first characters are its heading — no sentence saying the skill has been loaded, no sentence saying the packet is about to be analysed, nothing before the heading and nothing after the last section.

## Step one is arithmetic, not diagnosis

The save window is the number of days between today and the last day the customer could still deliver non-renewal notice — never the number of days to the renewal date, which is a deadline the customer cannot use.

**Save window = days to renewal − days of notice the contract requires.** Show the subtraction on the page every time, and subtract once: the notice period comes off the days to renewal, never off a window already computed.

Before subtracting, read every contract line in the packet, because the notice period quoted first is not always the one that governs: contracts qualify their own headline terms, and a qualification that applies to this account applies. Use the period that actually binds this customer, and say in one line why it is that one. The longer notice governs whether or not anyone on our side has read that clause lately.

The result names the play:

- **Negative or zero — closed.** A negative result means the deadline is that many days in the past, never that many days away. The opt-out deadline has passed and auto-renew has locked. A phased plan aimed at this renewal is operationally moot; say so in the first lines and shift to mid-term churn prevention (see *When the window has already closed*).
- **Fourteen days or fewer — compressed, four-day phases.** Three phases of roughly four days, midpoint at Day 7, daily internal standups, VP CS assigned on Day 1. A window inside this band is never rounded up into the next.
- **Fifteen to thirty days — compressed, weekly phases.** Three phases of roughly a week, midpoint around Day 10–14, daily standups, VP CS from Day 1. A four-to-eight-week arc is not forced in.
- **Thirty-one to sixty days — standard.** Diagnose / Prove / Commit across four to eight weeks, midpoint at the end of Week 4, commit finishing inside the window with days to spare.
- **More than sixty days — extended.** Time exists to gather signals before acting; an extended window never relaxes the plan, it removes every excuse for guessing.

Every phase, trigger and success date is counted from today, and the final commitment date sits before the deadline. The deadline itself is internal knowledge, never disclosed in an executive call: a buyer who learns the notice deadline is close shifts from partnership to negotiation.

## Classification: what changed, and who is complaining

**Step zero: who is in the room.** Before classifying, name the customer person the Week 1 meeting would be held with — the name that will sit in the snapshot's *Contact in post* field. If the packet names nobody still in post, there is no meeting to plan and no classification to make; the document is the refusal described under *No reachable contact*, written as a refusal under a refusal's heading, without a snapshot section or an *In brief*. A classification carrying the word "blocked" is a refusal that has not admitted it.

Exactly one of four root causes drives the plan, and choosing it is the load-bearing decision: the Week 1 opener, the midpoint question, the escalation triggers and the executive brief all branch on it. The wrong opener forecloses the save, because the first meeting tells the customer whether we understand their situation.

The method is the **acute-change test**: which signals changed sharply in the last 60–90 days? A signal present for months without triggering disengagement is context. A signal that appeared with the disengagement is the cause. Volume does not vote — a stack of tickets loses to one person who changed — and the errors run one way: people-driven change is routinely misread as product failure, because tickets are countable and a silent champion is not.

So run the **people test before the product test.** Take every person in the stakeholder map and write down what happened to them in the window: departed, replaced, went quiet, arrived, changed role. Then ask who is doing the complaining.

- A champion or buyer who has **departed**, or who has **stopped answering** repeated outreach while **someone new has appeared** in their place — an evaluator, an interim, a project lead "assessing" something — is stakeholder change, whatever the ticket queue says. The product tickets become context, and Week 1 verifies the person's status rather than assuming it.
- A **new arrival whose own team generates the complaints**, beginning with their arrival, while the previous users' behaviour and the buyer's stance are unchanged, is stakeholder change: the product did not change, the evaluator did. A roadmap session answers a question nobody who matters has asked; the new person has to be understood and won.
- Complaints from the **same people who were using the module before** — a champion who is frustrated but still talking, a buyer who raised the issue at the last QBR — about a **specific, documented shortfall** (a module that cannot do a named thing, a format that broke, a calculation too slow for a named workflow), with the decline beginning before any personnel change or having a documented external cause, is product gap. A new executive standing in that picture is a secondary risk to monitor, not the cause.
- A **named competitor, a formal RFP or procurement review, or an evaluation announced by a decision-maker in their own words** is competitive displacement and overrides all of the above from the moment it appears.
- **Broad, shallow decline with nothing behind it** — logins and seats falling across the board, no KPI ever defined, no QBR ever held, few tickets because nobody uses it enough to complain — is value unrealized.

**Two questions before product gap may be chosen**, because the tickets will always argue for it. One: are the people complaining the same people who used the module before the decline began — not a new arrival's team, not a replacement, not a newly appointed evaluator? Two: is the champion still in post and still talking to us — a person who answers, not one who has left repeated outreach unanswered while somebody new appears in their place? Product gap requires two yeses. A no on either makes it stakeholder change, and the product complaints, however specific and however many, move into the context sentence of the classification paragraph. A champion who has departed while the economic buyer is still engaged is the same call — stakeholder change — and its first move asks the buyer to name the successor.

Each type demands its own Week 1 opener, and the opener is where a wrong call does its damage. **Product gap** opens with an emergency roadmap review — Product Manager and Solutions Engineer with the frustrated team — producing a written gap-closure plan: a near-term workaround for whatever external event is looming, dated roadmap commitments for the requests. **Stakeholder change** opens with an executive sponsor introduction — our VP CS to their economic buyer — plus a working session with whoever is new or whoever inherited the departed champion's workflow. **Competitive displacement** opens with a head-to-head value re-frame — Account Executive and economic buyer — anchored on switching cost and differentiated outcome, never a feature comparison. **Value unrealized** opens with a business-outcome workshop with the champion to define the KPI and its baseline, followed by a dashboard that shows the delta.

### Writing the call

Name the type in bold. Then one paragraph of reasoning built from the packet's own words and dates — what changed, when, who is complaining, how the utilization and ticket timing line up with the people — in no more than seventy words. Then a paragraph opening **Runner-up rejected:** naming the second-best type and saying, in two or three sentences, why acute-change reasoning overrides its signal volume and what wrong Week 1 meeting it would have produced. When the requester asked to see the reasoning across several competing signals, the reasoning paragraph is replaced by three bold-led lines of one sentence each, one per signal — what it is, what confirms or defeats it — followed by the runner-up paragraph; no prose paragraph precedes or follows the three lines, and Section 1 carries no *In brief* at all in that case, so that the walk itself is the first thing after the arithmetic; the *In brief* paragraph in Section 1 stays under eighty words regardless, stating the call rather than arguing it. Section 2 is short because it is read first and checked hardest; the evidence it cites is quoted, not paraphrased at length.

**Conditional classification.** When one Week 1 conversation could flip the call — a new stakeholder nobody has met, a procurement inquiry nobody can explain — say so: name the conversation, run it in Week 1 in parallel with the opener, and state what happens if it reveals an evaluation. At high ARR an evaluation revealed that way is a same-day CRO event, not a midpoint one.

**Never invent status.** A contact marked active in CRM whose last logged interaction is many weeks old has an unknown status. Say the CRM record cannot be taken at face value, make verification the first action, and branch Phase 2 on the answer.

## Which deliverable the packet can carry

Decide this first, in order, stopping at the first match. 1. **Formal written non-renewal notice has been given** — out of scope; decline the plan and answer what was asked. 2. **A specific question was asked, or a full plan was said not to be wanted** — a recommendation shaped for that person, not the seven sections. 3. **Nobody left to plan with, or signals that cannot separate the four causes** — a structured refusal naming what is missing and how to get it; "nobody left" means the packet names no contact still in post, and a plan addressed to nobody in particular is not a plan whatever the usage numbers seem to say. The refusal has its own heading — *Signal Insufficiency — Classification Blocked*, with *Structural Observations* when the contact is missing — and no Account Snapshot. 4. **Otherwise** the seven-section plan, in its mid-term form when the window has closed. Urgency in the request never moves an account down this list.

## The plan follows the call

Three phases — **Diagnose, Prove, Commit** — in one table with a row per phase and four columns: Phase, Owner, Action, Measurable goal. Owners are roles with the CSM's name beside them; internal roles the packet does not name read "name TBD — assign before kickoff". Phase 1's action is the opener the classification demands, with the packet's secondary risk as a parallel action, never the lead — the unmet executive gets an introductory call framed as onboarding, the procurement contact is clarified through the champion. Phase 2 branches wherever a Week 1 verification could go either way. Phase 3 is the renewal conversation, anchored on the embedded, performing modules, the fix delivered before the ask: verbal commitment a few days before the deadline, written before it, multi-year the target where the ARR is large. Every goal is a named customer person's written confirmation, a utilization threshold with a date, or a commitment with day numbers from today.

**The midpoint gate** is one binary question tied to the root cause, answered by a named customer person's written confirmation — end of Week 4 on a standard play, Day 7 or Day 10–14 on a compressed one; **Yes** moves to Commit and activates the AE, **No** escalates the same day and tables a commercial lever (an SLA credit, a services engagement, a month-to-month bridge) beside an exec-to-exec call. **Success criteria** are four or five numbered, account-specific lines: the written confirmation, the utilization threshold with a date, the renewal signed before Day N, the secondary stakeholder engaged, the flag that did not escalate.

## Escalation calibrated to what the account is worth

One table — **Trigger | Escalates To | Action** — of four or five rows, triggers as day ranges from today, the last row always the deadline itself: opt-out imminent, no commitment; CRO or VP CS with Legal; month-to-month bridge or emergency extension; planned churn workflow if declined. An italic line above it states the ARR and why the ceiling sits where it does. **Under roughly $100K:** the CS Manager owns it, VP CS is the ceiling and only if the midpoint fails or an evaluation is announced; CRO and CEO never appear. **$100K to $750K:** VP CS at midpoint failure, from Day 1 when compressed; CRO only about seven days before the deadline; no CEO. **$750K to two million:** VP CS and CRO briefed from Week 1, CRO in the room at midpoint failure, CEO briefed — not a call — about a week out. **Above two million:** CRO mandatory at midpoint failure, a CEO-to-buyer call about two weeks out, multi-year the target; an active RFP is a same-day CRO escalation. Escalating past the ceiling is a substantive error, not a courtesy.

## The executive-call brief

Six labelled lines for whichever executive makes the call: **For**, **Who's in the room**, **The one thing to acknowledge first**, **Value evidence**, **The ask**, **Landmines to defer**. Dispositions come only from what the packet shows; where it shows nothing, say so and tell the executive to ask the CSM rather than assume. The thing to acknowledge first is normally the buyer's own unresolved concern in their own words — the issue raised at the last QBR we have not closed, the departure they are nervous about. The exception is a buyer who does not yet know there is a problem, because it surfaced below them after the last QBR and the CSM is resolving it: opening with a grievance they have never heard manufactures a crisis, so the call is framed as proactive support — setting the platform up for a new team, connecting ahead of renewal — and the brief says in so many words not to open with the issue. Value evidence leads with the embedded, performing modules and their figures. One ask. Landmines always include the notice deadline, pricing unless the buyer raises it, roadmap dates without the PM, any competitor comparison — plus this account's own: the module never adopted, the stakeholder whose stance is unknown, the KPI that failed.

## When the packet cannot carry a plan

**Signals too thin to classify.** ARR and dates alone, or a packet with no usage data, no stakeholder map and no support history, cannot separate the four types, and a guessed plan opens with the right meeting one time in four. Refuse plainly — no provisional plan, no "general relationship recovery" sequence — and make the refusal work: compute the window anyway and name the play type, because it says how fast the signals must arrive; explain in a few sentences why classification is the gating step; list the missing signals, numbered, each with what it distinguishes — usage telemetry for the last 60–90 days (broad decline, stable, or one module collapsing narrows four types to two), the stakeholder map with changes in that window, any competitive mention, the latest CSM notes or QBR summary; set a turnaround that fits the window, twenty-four hours on a compressed one; state what will not be produced and why a pause beats a placeholder.

**No reachable contact.** The request line at the foot of a packet asks for a full plan on every packet; it is a form line, not evidence a plan can be produced. When the only named contact has left and no replacement exists, the plan cannot be written whatever the signal picture — nobody to run it with, an exec brief with no attendee, and inventing one is worse than leaving it blank. The deliverable is a refusal with structural observations, never a plan with a caveat: first line, no plan can be authored and why; the window; both blockers — no contact, and a signal profile that fits several types at once, walked type by type; concrete recovery steps for the CSM — who to call to learn who inherited the role, whether the one remaining login belongs to someone who can make an introduction, whether onboarding ever happened. Where utilization has sat near zero since purchase, name the possibility that this is not a save scenario at all — an onboarding failure for a services re-engagement, or a passive auto-renew the customer may let happen, which is monitoring — and say what the CS Manager should decide about spending save resources on it. Note the likely classification once a contact exists, without confirming it; an extended window means there is time to find out.

## Answer the question that was asked

A specific question — continue, pivot, or something else; use this lever or not; is a plan appropriate — gets that question answered in a recommendation built to act on. **Mid-save reclassification:** a plan in flight is superseded when an acute new signal arrives that the original call did not account for — a champion announcing a vendor review, a buyer replaced, an evaluation surfacing. Reclassify, say why the new signal overrides the old call, and do not hedge into "continue and add a competitive track"; where the old midpoint also failed, say the old track was stalling on its own. The shape: a heading addressed to the requester; **Bottom line first** in one paragraph; why the root cause shifted; the revised window recomputed from today, notice subtracted once; three changes, each a short paragraph — the replaced Week 1 action, the escalation step at the ARR's ceiling (under roughly $100K that is CS Manager to VP CS and nothing higher, whatever the competitive pressure), the reset midpoint question with its yes and no; a **What not to do** list; a short summary. No phase table, no ladder, no success criteria, no exec brief. When the earlier play's KPI failed, the first meeting acknowledges the stall and pivots to switching cost.

**Formal notice.** A written non-renewal notice citing the contract takes the account out of scope. No save plan is produced or described, however many days remain and however the notice's timing compares with the notice period; asked whether a plan is appropriate, the answer is no, with no "but". Where the CSM sees a technicality — notice a day early — the answer is no for reasons that are relational and operational before they are legal: contesting a cordial exit turns it hostile and the customer will describe it to peers, and a customer at a fraction of former utilization who has chosen another vendor is not won back by being told the letter was early; whether the timing is material belongs to Legal and Finance, but the recommendation explains why the move is wrong from a CS standpoint instead of handing the question to counsel. What remains: confirm receipt promptly and warmly, and ask for one thing only — a short win/loss conversation before the term ends, framed as feedback, not a save; if it surfaces genuine ambiguity, come back then. A small account stays with the CS Manager. Answer the questions in the order asked, under their own headings, ending with the actions for the next twenty-four hours. Other out-of-scope requests — pricing design, contract drafting, an account not flagged at risk — are declined in a sentence with the right owner named.

## When the window has already closed

A negative window changes the document without abolishing it. The snapshot states the governing clause, the arithmetic and the conclusion — the opt-out deadline passed N days ago, auto-renew has locked, this is mid-term churn prevention (heading off downgrade, mid-term termination and a no-expand posture into the next cycle), not a renewal save. The plan section is retitled and its phases become **Stabilize** (re-establish the relationship the change broke; get a named successor or interim owner), **Re-anchor** (map the declining module to the open requests; written roadmap commitment from the new workflow owner) and **Expand posture** (open the next-cycle renewal conversation early, propose a standing executive cadence). The midpoint asks whether the successor has engaged and accepted the roadmap in writing; the ladder runs on relationship triggers, not deadline days; success criteria are next-cycle criteria; the brief's first landmine is the deadline math itself.

## Putting it on the page

A level-two heading naming the deliverable and the account, nothing before it; then seven numbered level-three sections: **1. Account Snapshot**; **2. Root Cause Classification**; **3. Save Plan**; **4. Midpoint Go/No-Go** with the day or week in the heading; **5. Escalation Ladder**; **6. Success Criteria**; **7. Executive-Call Preparation Brief**. The snapshot gives account, ARR, renewal date, CSM of record and **Contact in post** — the named customer person of the Week 1 meeting, with role — on one line; an empty field means the document is the refusal. Then **Save window computation:** — governing clause in a clause, subtraction shown, play type named — then **In brief:** one prose paragraph of exactly four sentences, under sixty words, written after the arithmetic and agreeing with it: the classification and what changed, the runner-up in a clause, the Week 1 opener, the midpoint day and question, the escalation ceiling, the brief's first move and first landmine. Section 2 carries the verdict, a paragraph of no more than seventy words and a runner-up of no more than forty-five, nothing else; Sections 1 and 2 together stay under two hundred and fifty words. The plan is one four-column table; the ladder one table of four or five rows under its italic line; success criteria one numbered run; the brief six labelled lines. A full plan runs seven hundred to nine hundred and fifty words and fits one screen; a refusal three to five hundred; a recommendation four to six hundred. Every number comes from the packet or is computed from it. No restatement of the packet, no narration of the method, no closing offer of help.

## Last pass

The response starts at its heading and *In brief* sits after the arithmetic and agrees with it. The window came from the notice deadline, subtracted once, every qualifying clause was looked for and the governing one named. The people were checked before the product; the runner-up is rejected with the wrong Week 1 meeting named; the opener is the one the type demands. Every phase has an owner, an action and a verifiable goal; the midpoint is one binary question; the ladder's ceiling matches the ARR and its last row is the deadline; the brief invents no stance and discloses no deadline. Nothing was produced that the packet cannot carry — no plan whose *Contact in post* field would be empty, no classification labelled blocked, no plan after formal notice — and the question actually asked is the one answered.
