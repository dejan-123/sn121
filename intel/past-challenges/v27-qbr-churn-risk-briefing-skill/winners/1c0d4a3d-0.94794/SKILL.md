description: Produces the internal churn-risk brief a customer success leader reads before a renewal review and has to defend afterwards. It is organised around identifying which situation an account is in rather than grading how poorly it appears to be doing, since two accounts can present the same instrumentation and need wholly unrelated responses: an account still talking to us whose usage is sliding has an adoption problem and needs remediation, while an account that has stopped replying is an epistemic problem and needs discovery first. A quality incident the customer has tied to renewal routes to whoever can commit a fix date. An unresolved charge or contract question in the signatory's hands is closed out before value is discussed at all. Something dated that they have not been told about needs telling. The customer company being bought suspends the exercise rather than adding to it. The urgency date is whichever binding deadline falls first, which is rarely the renewal. Every figure traces to the packet, an undocumented outcome stays undocumented, and a request for anything the customer will read is declined in the brief with the reason given, because this document exists to say what our own side got wrong.

# The sentence that names what you set aside

Every packet worth briefing supports more than one reading, so the line carrying this
document is never the one announcing your choice. It is the line naming the reading
you rejected, with the reason that reading cannot see what yours does. A driver
offered with nothing demoted beneath it reads as the only thing the writer happened
to notice, and a reader who doubts it has been handed nothing to argue with.

So the question is not how much trouble the account is in. It is which of a handful of
recognisable situations this is, what it asks of us this week, and which competing
situation you set aside in order to say so. Two accounts can arrive with the same
health reading, the same flattening usage and the same unremarkable survey result and
need responses with nothing in common; choose wrongly between them and the page can be
right on every particular while sending the wrong colleagues to a meeting that cannot
help.

Somebody has to sit in the renewal review and answer for this account. This is what
they read beforehand, and its whole value is the sentences that fault our own side —
a signal nobody answered for seven weeks, a reading we have been quoting that went
stale in the spring. That is also exactly why none of it can be handed to the
customer.

## Falling usage and a silent account look the same and are not

No pair gets confused more often, because the instruments render them identically:
sessions tapering, contact thinning out. Whichever you choose, name the other as the
reading you set aside and say what it cannot account for.

**Falling usage where they are still in contact with us is an adoption problem.** Someone still takes the call, the relationship holds, and what remains unexplained is why the product is not being used the way it was bought to be. That calls for remediation — a
working session, enablement, a specific fix carrying a date.

**An account that has gone quiet is not a more severe case of the same thing.** It is a problem about what we know: we cannot establish whether our sponsor is still employed there, whether the relationship passed to anybody when they went, or whether some decision has already been reached where we cannot observe it. That calls for discovery, not remediation. Work out who is still there, and what they mean to do, ahead of putting any fix on the table. Sending
an enablement plan into silence answers a question nobody asked.

A departed contact and an unanswered one are different findings too, and one is
routinely demoted for the other: a sponsor who left unreplaced is a gap in our own
coverage, whereas going quiet is something the account is doing to us.

## What the remaining situations each call for

**A failure of reliability or quality** calls for the support or engineering leadership route, and unambiguously so as soon as the customer has said anything direct about the renewal. Where such a sentence exists, carry it across in their words rather than
smoothing it into a paraphrase; the verbatim line is what moves an escalation
internally. Assigning this to somebody with no power to repair it only simulates action.

**Money or paper sitting with whoever signs** selects settlement ahead of
everything else. An unsettled dispute sitting with the signatory colours everything else said to that account, and proposing a value discussion to someone convinced they were billed wrongly will land as dodging the question. Order the work by dependency: close the question, then talk about value. And weigh it properly — an open commercial question in the signatory's hands inside the renewal window makes an account a live concern on its own, however well the usage curve, the satisfaction figure and the documented outcomes happen to read. Strong behaviour alongside unresolved money is not a healthy account; it is an account whose behaviour is not the thing at issue.

**A disclosure that they are looking elsewhere** selects confirmation before
escalation. It beats any gradual metric even when it appears only as an aside in somebody's notes; how prominently a fact was recorded says nothing about its weight, and a throwaway sentence is still the customer telling us they are looking around. Size it correctly, though: an aside warrants establishing whether a real process is underway, and nothing more than that on a mid-sized account. Reaching for a rescue too early burns credibility and tells the reader the author cannot judge scale.

Verification also has a definite first move, and it is not hunting for the signatory.
Go back to whoever said it and establish whether something formal is running — a
tender, an evaluation, a shortlist — since everything we might do next hangs on that
answer.

Where a remark like this has lain untouched for weeks while nobody here holds any
route to the person who signs, the rung is critical and the word belongs on the page.
Not because the remark was alarming, but because the record shows a month passed in
which we did nothing and there is no relationship left through which to recover.
Resist writing that it is turning critical: the weeks that make it so are already
spent. A distant renewal does not soften that — a long runway on an account we have
already neglected for a month measures our conduct rather than our remaining time.
This reasoning covers something they told us and we then let sit; it does not extend
to an account that has merely stopped answering, since an ignored approach is not a
disclosure, and quiet running against a closing window is the rescue case above
rather than this one. The lapse is a finding in its own right: presenting the remark
as news buries the more useful fact, which is that we sat on it.

When a remark of this kind is present it carries the brief alone. Do not weld it to
the consumption decline in a joint heading — somebody who has started shopping
usually stops logging in because of it, so the sinking measurement is a symptom of
the very thing you would be listing next to it.

**A dated change nobody has told them about** — an integration being retired,
something they depend on reaching end of life, an entitlement expiring ahead of the
renewal — means telling them, and soon. Nothing here is for a watchlist. Short of a
counterparty that has ceased to be the counterparty, it is the gravest entry on this
list, because the damage is already in a diary and we are the party that has stayed
silent. An account whose dependency expires before its renewal with nobody having
raised it is critical, whatever cheerful readings surround it. They are going to find
out, and they will find out in the middle of the renewal conversation.

**The customer's business changing hands** — an acquisition of the company itself, not a change of contact or of account manager — means there is no play to select and no rung to assign. What the buyer means to do is
invisible from here, the people who picked us may hold no vote next time, and our
entitlements can shift under a clause nobody on our side has opened. Do not retreat
to a careful middle rung because care feels like diligence: there is nothing to
choose between, because every rung takes for granted that the same organisation will
be deciding. The honest output gives no classification at all and says why it is withheld, logs
the contractual question as still sitting with Legal unanswered, and routes what
happens next to the account executive together with the success director rather than
whoever handles the account from day to day.

More than one of these is usually present, and the brief names exactly one. Do not
set two side by side as co-equal drivers; a heading joining a commercial objection
to a support ticket, or a disclosure to a usage decline, is the shape of an author
who has not decided. When several apply, work from what would still be true if the
others were fixed tomorrow.

That resolves most contests the same way. A business changing hands, or a packet
holding nothing, displaces everything because neither leaves a question the other
situations can answer. A dated change we have not disclosed comes next, since it
lands whether or not anyone improves. Then an unresolved commercial question in the
signatory's hands, which outranks any technical fault however severe, because the
person who signs is already unhappy about money and the fault is a reason rather
than the obstacle. Then a relationship gone quiet. Then a disclosure that they are
shopping, which beats a reliability fault as well — a customer who is looking
elsewhere has a reason for the tickets, not the other way round. A quality failure
is the driver only when nothing above it is present, and it earns that place
outright once they have tied it to the renewal in their own words. Sliding usage and
a moving score are never the driver; they are how the driver shows up.

**Almost nothing in the packet** also selects no classification. One reading with
nothing beside it to compare against is not evidence. Say which datum is absent,
who can produce it and by when, and where you would provisionally stand meanwhile.
Be strict with this: a packet naming a specific dated adverse event is not sparse
however empty the fields around it.

## Read the dated facts before the curves

Work through the packet in the opposite order to the one it is written in. Collect
its dated material first — each departure, dispute, quoted sentence, deadline,
withdrawal and unanswered approach that carries a day against it — because renewals
are lost on days rather than along curves, and whatever ends this one has either
already happened on a date the packet names or is scheduled to. Only afterwards read
the measurement series, and put the same question to each of them: what has this not
had time to register? A figure assembled from last month's behaviour is counting the
people who were still there while it was being calculated.

## The score selects nothing

A health figure is arithmetic, not observation. It lags the relationship by a
quarter, it is built out of whichever behaviours somebody once decided to measure,
and it rises and falls on inputs that carry no information about departure. It is a thing to be
explained, never the explanation, and it is overridden in both directions.

It runs high and hollow when the sponsor has gone, nobody holding budget has
replaced them and consumption has flattened — the arithmetic is still digesting a
quarter in which that person was present. It runs low and stale when recent weeks
are visibly recovering and the model has not caught up; there the honest reading
cites the recovering values themselves. And it runs comfortably upward while a
dispute reaches the signer, in which case the recovery is context and the friction
is the story. Say which reading you are correcting and in which direction. Nothing
is well because one number is green, and nothing is lost because one is red.

## The first binding date, not the renewal, sets the pace

Every packet puts its renewal date up front, which is why most briefs end up planned
against it. The date that actually governs is whichever binding one arrives soonest: the last day notice can be served, an
opt-out window closing, an auto-renewal cut-off, a dependency's withdrawal date, a
review triggered by a change of ownership. After that date the decision stops being
reversible. Identify whichever comes soonest, put it somewhere the reader cannot miss with the days still to run, and establish whether anyone has informed the customer.

It governs which play is even available. An obstacle that counts as scheduled work with four months to go becomes a genuine threat with six weeks left. Four steps that must happen in order will not fit a window shutting in under a month, and offering them announces that nobody looked at the calendar; when the time is thin, state what is being given up as well as what is being tried.
A window about to shut on a sponsor nobody can reach is what converts a difficult
account into one needing rescue now, and the brief should get there on its own rather
than handing the reader the pieces. Give that one combination its proper name: an account that has stopped answering us
at all, while a binding date of its own runs down, is a **save-play** rather than a
critical. Both halves are required. An account that still replies is not here however
severe its troubles — a live incident someone is working, even an urgent one, belongs
a rung lower. What makes this different is that a clock is closing on a customer who
will not pick up, so the contract can lapse while we are still diagnosing. The renewal date is not that clock —
every account has one — but a notice period, an opt-out window or an auto-renewal
cut-off is.

## Nothing on the page the packet will not carry

No invented outcome, no assumed mood, no estimated figure. Where nothing is
documented, say so; that absence matters, because it means the renewal conversation
has no evidenced value to stand on. A result someone mentioned but nobody measured
is labelled unmeasured and cannot carry the narrative. Give every figure its date,
and never let a reading taken before the event speak for the present.

Do no arithmetic at all. Every number on the page must appear in the packet in the
form you print it — no spans, no percentages, no deltas, no day-counts of your own
making. Where a stretch of time is the point, name the two dates that bound it and
stop there: "recorded 2026-04-30, and this brief is dated 2026-06-20" does the work,
while "51 days unanswered" is a figure you manufactured and it is the single fastest
way to have the whole brief discounted. The same restraint applies to attribution:
report who said what only in the form the record states it, and never fuse two
entries into one claim about who confirmed what and when.

This governs the account's past and present only. The recommendation is a proposal
about next week, so naming an owner, setting a horizon and defining what would count
as success is the job, not fabrication, and all three are required even though none
of them appears in the packet.

## Setting it down

Never place a row of backtick characters at the start: this is a document rather than
code, and wrapping it in a fenced block wastes the only screen the reader gives it. The output starts on the account heading with
nothing whatever in front of it: no preface, no echo of the request. After the
closing line there is nothing either — no valediction, no offer to revise. Open on the account, mark it internal, then move through where the account
stands, how it has shifted, who is on it, what has actually been evidenced, what else
is live, what to do, and what is absent.

The earliest binding date goes on its own line above the classification, with days
remaining. Keep healthy for accounts where not one of the predicaments above is in play. The
moment one of them is — the moment anything above was selected — healthy is off the
table and the only remaining question is how far up the account sits. No reading buys
an exemption: a climbing figure, expanding consumption, a recorded result and a
delighted sponsor can all hold true of an account whose renewal is genuinely in
doubt, because each of those describes how the product is being used while the doubt
lives elsewhere entirely. Handle a spoken intention to renew as the unmeasured claim
it is — worth writing down, not a countersigned contract, and no basis for calling an
account healthy while a commercial question sits unanswered with the person running
the process.

Then the classification — healthy, at-risk, critical, save-play, or undeterminable — and after it the dated condition that would lift it: what must occur, or fail to
occur, and by which day. Never supply a reason the account is not the graver rung.
Take the rung the evidence supports and state what would escalate it. Immediately
beneath, a single condensed line opening "Next:" holding the owner, the horizon and
the test, so that a reader who stops there still leaves with the thing to do in
hand.

Name one situation as the driver and say which datum settled it. Where two readings
ran close, label that fact the **tiebreak** in so many words, say who can fetch it if
we do not hold it, and say which way it would move the call — whatever would have
settled the question is exactly where the reader's doubt is sitting — then name the trend
it outranks, with a clause on why that trend cannot see what the dated fact shows.
Saying what you demoted is what makes the call legible, since a driver with nothing
ranked underneath it reads as the only thing the writer noticed. Demote trends this
way and never rungs: a sliding usage curve or a moving score is exactly the thing to
name and set aside, whereas arguing that an account is not the rung above it is how a
brief talks itself downward. Rank what remains
beneath it, one line each — but a binding contractual date or a disclosure that they
are shopping is never a runner-up. Either it is the driver or it sharpens the driver;
arguing such a fact down the page is how a brief loses the thing that decides it.

Send the action to whoever can genuinely shift it: pricing and terms to the account
executive with finance or legal behind them; a defect to the support or engineering
lead able to commit to a date; a relationship gone quiet, or a counterparty that has
changed hands, to a director who can open a door the manager cannot. Never to anyone
on the customer's side, who is what the action acts upon rather than who owns it.
Write it as a sentence rather than a form with labels, and match the seniority to the
money at stake.

Where the record carries an ask for material the customer would see — slides, a
message, a proposal — a single line headed **Boundary** goes immediately below the
recommendation. Say which artifact is being declined, say that this page is the
internal read and is not handed across, and name who produces the customer-facing
version and what has to happen first. Nothing later may then
draft, outline, schedule or review that artifact; a boundary followed by a step that
quietly builds the thing is worse than none. And finish the assessment anyway. The ask being out of scope does not make the account unassessable, and a refusal offered in place of the analysis is the opposite error. A boundary also changes nothing about the classification: judge the account exactly as though the request had never been attached, since declining to write someone's slides is not evidence that their renewal is safe.

Length is a hard constraint rather than a preference, and running over it is worse
than leaving something out. The reader is given one screen, and anything past roughly
the first two thousand two hundred characters is not read at all — so the whole brief finishes inside that, with the classification, the driver
and its demoted reading, the action and the gaps all present before the page runs
out. Where it will not fit, cut description and never judgement: the trajectory and
the stakeholder lines compress to a line apiece, and the defence keeps only the
alternative a reader would actually raise. The gaps must never be the part that falls off
the end. Before finishing, check that the classification, the driver with its demoted
reading, the action and the gaps are all on the page — a brief that stops partway
through is scored as incomplete however good its opening was.

Within that, let the evidence decide the length. Where an account has gone quiet, or the packet
holds almost nothing, most of the middle contracts to a line each and the absences
become the substance of the page. Write the way an analyst speaks rather than the way
a form gets completed: sentences carrying a fact and a judgement at once, with lists
confined to the stakeholder table and the ranked risks.

## Two fragments, worked

Both invented, and neither is a template. What transfers is the balance between parts and the shape of the argument, never the sentences, the people or the numbers.

## Before it goes

Read the finished page once more against four questions. Does one situation carry the
brief, with the trend it displaced named underneath it? Does the rung follow from that
situation rather than from any figure, and does it carry a date? Would every number,
name and quotation survive being checked against the packet line by line? And if the
packet asked for something the customer would read, is the refusal on the page while
the assessment is still complete? A no to any of them is a rewrite, not a caveat.
