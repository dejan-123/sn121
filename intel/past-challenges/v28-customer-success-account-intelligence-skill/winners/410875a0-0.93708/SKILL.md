# The Contract Reads the News

A CS practitioner opens this page before a QBR prep call with twenty minutes and a stack of headlines somebody pasted in. What they need from it is short: which of these events changes what happens at this account's renewal, through what, and how soon — and permission to stop reading the rest.

A news pool is ordered by what was loud. A renewal is decided by three things the pool never mentions directly: who owns the decision, what the contract actually counts, and the date the decision closes. The whole of the work is reading each headline against those three, and the page is the record of that reading.

## Why these three and not a longer list

Account teams are handed lists of risk factors — funding, leadership, layoffs, sentiment, news volume — and the lists fail the same way: every item on them is sometimes decisive and usually irrelevant, so an analyst working from a list escalates whatever was loudest this week. The three vectors are not a shorter list. They are the only mechanisms by which a renewal changes at all. A renewal is a decision a named person takes, about a counted thing, at a date. Move the person, change the count, or change the conditions around the date, and the renewal changes; touch none of those and it does not, whatever the headline.

That is also why each vector carries its own standard of evidence. Authority is about people, so it turns on who said what: a buyer's own post is proof and a trade rumour is a question. Scope is arithmetic, so it turns on the contract's unit: seats, sites, stages and modules can be counted, and the count is either changing before the gate or it is not. The environment is the loosest of the three, so it asks for the most specific statement: a named category, a named review, a named date. An owner's general promise of efficiency, a new chief executive's first speech and a sector downturn describe weather; a mandate that names software contracts describes this contract.

The gate matters for the same reason. The people who receive an Escalate drop other work to act on it, and the only work that can change a renewal is work done before the renewal is decided. An effect that lands after the decision is real and belongs to the next one; the page records it as Monitor so it is not forgotten, and keeps Escalate for what somebody can still act on.

## A headline has no class until it meets a contract

The same sentence — "the COO is retiring", "the company raised $60 million", "the plant in Ohio was sold" — is an escalation at one account and noise at the next. What decides it is not the event but the route by which it could reach this contract, and there are only three.

**The authority of the person who owns the renewal.** A renewal is signed by a named person, usually with an executive above them whose support they spend to get it through. An event that removes, weakens, replaces or strengthens either of them reaches the renewal directly, because the decision is a person's decision. This is vector 1: the champion, the economic buyer, and the reporting line they sit in.

**The product scope in use.** A contract counts something — seats, sites, vessels, modules, a named team's licences. An event that changes the number or the existence of the thing counted reaches the renewal as arithmetic: fewer planners means fewer seats to renew, a new plant on a per-seat model means more. This is vector 2, and it is the most concrete of the three, because the contract states its own unit. It also says which part of the company the unit lives in: hiring into a team the contract does not serve adds no seats, and new business the account wins that will run through the product it already licenses — more learners, more sites, more clients on that product — is growth in the scope in use.

**The renewal environment.** Budget pressure, a new owner, a vendor-rationalisation mandate, a strategic review of the unit that holds the contract. These do not touch the licences or the buyer, but they change the conditions under which the buyer will argue for them. This is vector 3, and it matters most when the statement is specific — a CEO who names software contracts as a cost target has said something a CEO who mentions "efficiency" has not.

An event that travels none of these routes is noise, whatever its size. A funding round, an award to the company, a profile of the company in a trade magazine, a peer company's fine: each is real news about the account or its sector, and none of it changes who decides, what is counted, or when. Recognition that lands on the renewal owner personally is different — an award, a keynote, a post defending the product in their own name — because it adds to the standing they will spend getting the renewal through, which is vector 1; it earns a Monitor row as an expansion signal.

## The proximity test

For every event that seems to matter, the page names the vector it travels and the element of this contract it arrives at — the 120 planner seats, the Director of Marine Operations who owns the renewal, the October co-term check-in. The test of a good rationale is that it would be wrong if pasted into another account's brief. "Leadership change may affect budget" is true of every account and therefore says nothing about this one; "the COO she reports to leaves three months before the co-term check-in" is only true here.

Name the element with the brief's own words and numbers. The reader acts on the row, and a row that says which licences, which person and which date can be acted on without reopening the brief.

## The gate, and the length of the fuse

Every contract has a decision gate: the renewal date, or — in a multi-year agreement — the co-term check-in, which is where the real decision is taken even though the paper runs longer. The tier an event earns is set by where its effect lands relative to that gate, not by how urgent the headline sounds.

**Escalate** is for an event that has already changed, or is confirmed to change before the gate, one of the things the contract rests on: the licensed scope, the person who owns the renewal, or an explicit decision about this vendor or its category. A buyer leaving in the month the renewal falls due, a divestiture that removes licensed scope before the renewal, a named replacement for the module in use, a chief executive naming software contracts as the cost to cut — each has a date and a contract element attached. A reduction in licensed scope that has already completed is Escalate however old the news, because the seats are gone today; that present-tense reading belongs to reductions of scope. Growth that has already completed is still Monitor, because seats gained are sold in the renewal conversation and nothing is lost by waiting for it. So does a competitor that is already inside: when the account buys a company whose people work in a rival product to the one we license, the rival is running in the customer's own operations from the day the deal closes, and the row is Escalate whatever the distance to the gate. A change of owner or of chief executive changes the environment, and its effect on this contract stays unknown until somebody names a review, a date or a category. A chief executive listing cost optimisation among next year's priorities has named a mood rather than a contract; one who names vendor consolidation or software renegotiation has named the category. A primary source saying the account itself may be sold, repositioned or put under strategic review is Escalate whatever the date on it, because who will own the contract is already in question; the same thing said in an unsigned column is Monitor.

The last quarter before the gate is different ground. By then the renewal is already in motion — the quote is being built, procurement is booking the review — so an event that directly threatens one of the three vectors is Escalate as soon as it appears, even before its effect lands: a buyer announcing a move, an acquirer that already runs a competing product across its own clients, an ownership change closing inside the window. Further out than that, Escalate waits for an effect that has landed or is confirmed to land before the gate.

**Monitor** is where every expansion signal sits, however concrete — new seats wait for the conversation that sells them, and nothing is lost by the wait — and it is for a real risk whose fuse runs past the gate, or has no date at all: an acquisition closing after the renewal, a new owner with no stated plans, a hiring wave the budget has not yet funded. A renewal ten months away with nothing compressing it is Monitor even for a private-equity acquisition that arrives with a new chief executive and a promise of efficiency, because the tier follows the evidence in the pool, not what owners of that kind are known to do; efficiency is what every new owner promises, and it names no contract.

Escalate is scarce because the people who receive it drop other work. That scarcity is a judgement, not a quota: when three events strike three different vectors inside the same short window, three Escalates are correct, and the note under the table says why they are three routes to one loss rather than one event counted three times. Several events on one vector are the opposite case — one pressure told several ways — and each earns the tier it would earn alone.

Recency and relevance are different questions. A divestiture five months old that took forty per cent of the licensed use is live; a funding round announced this morning with no link to the buyer's budget is noise. The fuse is measured from the event's effect to the gate, never from the headline's date to today.

## Who said it, and how many times

Sources differ in what they commit. A company's own release, an earnings or investor call, and the buyer's own quoted words are the company speaking; trade press reports what someone said; an anonymous blog or forum post may be right and commits nobody. A rumour that would strike a vector if true — the renewal owner leaving, the division that holds the contract being reorganised, a vendor change — keeps its row at Monitor, because the weakness of the source sets the tier and says nothing about whether the event is real; the row names the confirmation that would move it to Escalate the same day. The buyer's own words stay the strongest source wherever they appear — a trade publication quoting them, or their own post announcing a move: a buyer quoted naming the workflow our module serves as the thing being replaced has told us the decision, and a buyer posting that they are starting somewhere else has told us who will not be signing.

Two items about the same underlying event are one event. The page keeps the one from the stronger source, gives it one row, and counts the weaker one in the discard line as redundant; two rows for one story tell the reader something happened twice. Commentary about what a confirmed event will lead to belongs to that event as well — an analyst guessing that the acquirer will consolidate tools restates, at lower confidence, the risk the confirmed row already carries — so it is counted as redundant rather than given a row of its own. The same holds inside one announcement: the mandate a new owner states and the executive it appoints in the same release are that event's detail, carried in its row.

Two items that contradict each other in the same week both stay on the page, because the contradiction is itself information. They are resolved by who said them — a CEO on an earnings call binds the budget, job postings describe an intention the budget has not approved — and the row that loses the resolution says what outranked it.

## One event, two classes

Some events are a risk and an opportunity at once: an acquisition that brings a competing tool and new headcount, a reorganisation that removes one team and grows another. The row takes the class the account team has to act on first — usually the risk, because a lost renewal forecloses the expansion — and names the other as secondary in the same cell: *Renewal-risk (secondary: expansion-signal)*. Splitting it into two rows would make one event look like two.

## The discard line

Everything that fails the proximity test is counted, once, in a single bold line under the table: the total, then each category with a one-clause reason and its count. The reason is still account-specific — "affects that operator's contracts, not this one" — because a discard is a verdict too and the reader may want to check it. Discards never get rows: a table is where the reader looks for work, and a row of noise is work they will do for nothing.

A regulation earns a row only where it takes effect inside the decision window and reaches something the contract licenses — a rule that creates the work a licensed module does, landing before the gate, is an expansion signal for that module. A final rule that takes effect early in the term being renewed, and changes what a licensed module has to do, is decided at this gate: a customer renewing in the autumn for a term that must meet a January rule is choosing its compliance tooling now, so the rule earns a Monitor row. A proposed rule binds on the date its final form takes effect, not the date it opens for comment, so one whose final text is more than a year away is judged by that distant date. A rule that lands beyond the coming term, or governs something the contract does not cover, is sector news.

The usual contents: a competitor's or peer company's fine, funding or award; sector policy whose effect lands after the gate or touches no vector; company-level awards, profiles and rankings; funding with no traceable path to the buyer's budget; launches in a market the contract does not serve; the weaker copy of a story already in the table.

## When the brief cannot carry a verdict

The proximity test needs three things from the brief: the product scope in use, the champion or economic buyer and their role, and the contract shape — its unit, its value and its decision gate. Without them there is no contract for the headlines to meet, so there is no verdict to give, including on events that look like obvious noise: a charity sponsorship is noise unless the product includes the events module that runs it, and only the scope can say which.

So a brief missing those fields gets a page with no table and no discard line. It says in its first sentence what the brief does contain and what it lacks, then asks once for the three fields, each with the event in this pool whose verdict it would decide — so the person who pasted the pool knows exactly what to go and find. The ask is written from this account's events, which makes each one a different length and shape.

"Provisional" is a property of the context, not of the request. When somebody asks for a best guess but the brief does carry scope, buyer and contract shape, the verdicts are grounded and the page is a normal table; one sentence after it says the context was complete, so nothing on the page is provisional.

## The page

One heading, `## Triage Table — ` and the account's name. Under it one sentence, the read: the gate and how far off it is, the row that leads, and what the loudest item in the pool came to. Then the table, with five columns in this order: **Event** (the fact named in a few words, not the pasted item), **Vector → element** (the vector and the contract element it reaches, in the brief's words), **Relevance class** (Renewal-risk, Expansion-signal, or a dual class with its secondary named), **Proximity reasoning** (one sentence: the vector, the contract element in the brief's own words and numbers, and where the effect lands against the gate), **Action tier** (Escalate or Monitor). The rows are the events that passed the proximity test, strongest first.

Then the bold discard line: `**Discarded: N events. Reasons: [category — reason: count; ...].**` Then a rule, `---`, and one short note under a bold label naming what it is about: the action for the Escalate row or rows — who, what, before when — or, where nothing escalates, the fuse reasoning that says why. Where two items in the pool contradict each other, a second labelled note follows it: which source outranked which, the tier each keeps, and who can confirm which one holds. The page ends there.

The page is triage and stops at triage: no account narrative, no save plan, no churn probability, no comparison with other accounts. The person reading it is about to decide where the next hour goes, so every cell says one thing, in the order it matters, and the page returns alone, beginning at its heading.

The four pages below are the calibration. They are different lengths because their pools hold different amounts, and a thin pool earns a short page. A rich pool earns a denser page, not a longer one: the reasoning in each row carries more, and the page still fits in one screen.

## Worked — a brief that stops before the contract

*Six events; the brief gives the account's name, sector and headcount and nothing about the contract.*

> Four clinics bought in the west could be new seats or nothing at all, and Linden Vale Veterinary Partners' brief cannot say which: it names the group, its 1,900 staff and six events, and stops before what Linden Vale licenses, who signs, and when. None of the six can be placed until those are known — the charity dog show included, which is noise only if nothing we sell runs events.
>
> Three fields would settle it:
>
> 1. **Product scope in use** — the module and what its licences count: clinics, clinicians or seats. This decides whether the four clinics add seats or have no bearing.
> 2. **Champion / economic buyer** — the name, the role and who they report to. This decides whether the group CFO's departure removes the person who signs or happens elsewhere in the business.
> 3. **Contract shape** — term, renewal or co-term date, pricing model. This decides whether the pet-insurance partnership, starting in May, falls before the decision or after it.
>
> Paste those three and the full triage follows.

## Final check

Before it goes: the page begins at its heading, or — where the brief lacks scope, buyer or contract shape — at a first sentence saying what the brief holds and lacks, followed by the three-field ask and nothing else. Every row names its vector and a contract element in the brief's own words, and would be wrong in another account's brief. Every tier follows where the effect lands against the decision gate. Each story has one row, at its strongest source; contradictions stand side by side with their resolution. The discard line counts everything else once, each with its reason. Every figure and name on the page came from the brief or the pool, and any sum the page states is right. The closing note says who acts, on what, before when.
