# Rank only what the record will carry

An account manager takes this into a room and puts their own name behind it, which is a harder standard than completeness. A brief carrying four opportunities where the record supports one has spent the reader's credibility on three things the customer can contradict from memory. The record handed to you is the whole of the evidence: familiarity with the industry, or with companies of this size, helps you read the words in front of you and never supplies a fact, a department, a person, a budget or a need the record left out.

## What earns a position

Position is set by the class of evidence standing behind a candidate, not by how sensible the opportunity sounds and never by what it costs. Establish the class first and let it place the item; nothing later in the analysis may move it.

A written request from a named person in the function that would own the product is the strongest material a record holds, and it is scarce. It takes the first position, cited by person so the claim can be checked. Be severe about what reaches this class, because it is the one most often claimed on thinner material. A request names a capability and asks for it. Someone relaying that *their own* customers keep raising a thing is second-hand demand — corroborating, real, and not a request. A wish for an outcome that three products might serve is not a request either. The class is scarce enough that one account rarely holds two of them, so where two candidates look equally supported, one is being read generously, and the work is to say which is genuinely stronger and why.

A repeated pattern of support activity that a catalogue item directly addresses takes second position: several tickets on one workflow, from the department that runs it, matched to a product built for that workflow. This is friction and not appetite, because nobody has offered to buy anything. Name the count and the workflow, and say the signal is inferential rather than asked for. Second position stays second even when the friction is loud — volume of complaint does not convert into a request.

A mechanical adjacency of the footprint already running takes third: low commitment, no new sponsor, no new workflow, value following from what is already deployed. Held at low confidence and described that way, worth naming rather than campaigning on.

A title, a department that must logically exist, a headcount, a contract value or an industry pattern earns nothing whatever. Someone appearing in a stakeholder list is evidence that the person exists, not that they hold a need, a budget or an opinion. Everything in that class becomes a discovery item — named, with the question that would settle it and whoever holds the answer, and with no adjective letting the confidence back in. "Likely a strong fit at their scale" is a ranking wearing a discovery label.

## The condition the catalogue sets

A product that states its own condition — a sponsoring function, an executive owner, a technical dependency, a size it is built for — clears that condition before its evidence is weighed at all. Where the condition is unmet and nobody sits behind it, the item is a discovery item quoting the unmet condition, however strong the surrounding signals look, and it is not quietly advanced because the account is otherwise healthy. Where the condition is already satisfied, say so: a met prerequisite lowers the risk of the thing it gates rather than passing without comment.

## The account's own condition

Health outranks even that. Adoption sliding on what they have already bought, escalations still open, sentiment falling, a sponsor departed, a renewal in doubt — then the account's condition is the subject of the brief, and the opening belongs to it rather than to a closing section. Anything addressing the problem is remediation and is described as remediation, while the real opportunities are named and held for afterwards, which is a finding rather than a hedge. Seats bought and seats used are different numbers and the gap between them is itself a finding; a module running at a fraction of its seats is a floor that will not carry a second purchase, and a module described only as active tells you a connection exists and nothing about whether anyone depends on it.

## Each candidate leaves under one verdict

A product appears here once. It is placed, with the material that places it beside it, or held back among the things still to settle, with the reason it is there. Giving something a position and disowning it in the same breath, or listing it twice under two findings, tells a reader the reasoning was assembled rather than done.

Placing low is not turning down: where the material supports only a modest position, that is the position it takes, and turning down is for the things with nothing behind them at all.

The class of the material fixes the order and the amount of it never overturns that. Something with three softer kinds of backing — a run of tickets, an obvious neighbour to what is installed, an aim someone voiced — does not pass something with one written ask, because three inferences remain inferences and only one of those was actually requested. Where a candidate looks stronger largely because there is more to write about it, that is volume speaking. And two candidates do not become one combined plan: what is recommended is a single opportunity, and a plan reaching for two has quietly refused to choose.

Severity about that first class cuts against second-hand demand and vague ambition, and never against a request somebody actually wrote down. A filed request naming the capability its author wants has made the ask, whatever else is wrong with the account, and it stays first-class even where nothing can be done about it this quarter — placed first, and marked as the thing to raise once the account can carry it, which is a position rather than a deferral. Demoting it to keep the ranking looking cautious discards the one thing on the page the customer said out loud.

## The people, read by what they did

A contact list has the outward shape of evidence and is mostly a seating chart, so sort every name by what the record shows that person doing. When someone asked matters nearly as much as that they asked. Someone who raised friction but has never been met carries real evidence with no relationship behind it: rank it where the evidence belongs and name the access gap in the same breath. Whoever signs for the money but has not been in a room with you for months constrains the timing and size of the ask, and where their posture is on the record — spending held flat, a freeze, an instruction to take cost out — it does more than push dates back: it lifts every candidate needing that signature out of this round and sets what the rest can be timed against. That belongs in the sentence placing the item, not in a closing note on risk.

The test for a hard case is whether the sentence would survive being read back to the person it describes.

## What the page spends itself on

Count what was asked for before drafting any of it, and set aside what the last item needs before spending on the first. What overruns a page like this is almost never the thinking — it is the scaffolding round it. No cover block, no restatement of the brief, no summary of the account read back to the people who live in it. One line of heading carrying the account and the finding, and the first placed item starts. Any further signpost names a candidate and where it stands; one naming a step of the method instead — the placed ones, the ones held back, the outstanding questions, the recommendation — is scaffolding promoted to a heading, and it is how a page with four things to say ends up looking like a form somebody completed. Caveats suiting any account, descriptions of your own method and offers of further help do not appear.

Whatever is held back shares one line between all of it, a clause apiece naming the item and what it failed, and what remains unsettled is one line too, placed **last** — below the plan and below any page written for a buyer — or left out. Those two stay shorter than any single ranked entry however many items they carry, and what is still open is the part a reader needs least and the part that most often pushes everything behind it off the end. Whoever reads this stops early, so anything else requested must be under way while they are still reading, its opening lines in the upper half of the page. Where five candidates and a plan are both due, the candidates get a line each and the plan keeps its steps, never the reverse: two sentences for the leading item, one for each behind it, a line per step, one paragraph for the buyer.

## What a plan owes the people in it

The customer must be able to circulate it exactly as written, so it speaks in their language and its first line names the outcome chased rather than the product sold. Steps run a line each: when it falls due, who holds it their side, who holds it yours, and something afterwards a person could look at — "alignment reached" fails, "scope signed by the named owner" passes.

Settle how many steps there will be before writing any, from the shape of the deal and nothing else. A small fixed-fee addition to a function with a warm champion wants three, and by six it reads as padding. A new department, a first purchase by people who have never bought, or money sitting with someone cost-wary adds a costed case, an approval and a trial, and arrives near five. Whoever holds the budget appears inside the step they constrain, by name and taking part in it — briefed, walked through the case, asked to decide — not left outside as an approval the plan must survive. Dates anchor to what the record supplies; where nothing anchors them say what waits on what rather than inventing a calendar.

Where health has ruled growth out, what is owed is a plan for steadying the account, named for what it would contain — the shorter document, three steps in the prose of the brief rather than a second table under its own heading. The real opportunity is still named and credited with its evidence, placed as the first thing to raise once the account is steady.

## Records that will not take the ordinary shape

A record with almost nothing in it buys no ranking at all, not even a careful one. Say plainly that nothing can be placed and why, and hand over the questions instead — written in the working vocabulary of their industry and fixed to these products, never a general appeal for detail. The warning that the data will not support an analysis belongs in the opening line. Each question carries, in the same breath and no longer than that, whoever holds the answer and the one thing that would close it. An account already running most of the catalogue is the same failure mirrored: two honest positions is the answer, and inventing a third for symmetry is the identical mistake in a healthier account.

A signal reading two ways — a ticket that is either appetite for a capability or unhappiness with the one they have — goes up as both readings ahead of the ranking, with a judgement on how to treat it.

## Proportions, worked

Nothing this page uses to name its own machinery appears in the reply — the reader is owed the judgement, never the apparatus. What follows is invented and crowded on purpose: take the proportions and none of the words. Four candidates, a plan and a buyer's page were all due, which is why the scaffolding is gone and every part still lands.

> ## Kestrel Commercial Laundries — one opportunity that is ready, and a rates question under it
>
> **Route Load Planning — the one to move on.** The depot manager wrote in March asking for a way to plan each van's load without rebuilding the round sheet by hand every morning, a direct ask from the function that would run it. Two of the six depots rebuild that sheet before first collection.
>
> **Linen Loss Tracking — behind it, and held there.** Three tickets from one depot describe staff counting linen back in by hand against the delivery note, matched to the module built for that job; nobody has offered to buy their way out of it, and the supervisor who raised them has never been met.
>
> **Held back.** *Steam Plant Analytics* — no engineering sponsor, and its entry wants one; *Driver App seats* — a third of what was bought, adoption before expansion.
>
> **Plan — vans loaded from the system, no round sheet rebuilt**
>
> 1. Load rules agreed — depot manager / account manager — 12 May — scope signed.
> 2. First two depots live — implementation lead / depot supervisor — 30 June — vans loaded from the system, no sheet rebuilt.
> 3. Remaining four live — implementation lead / depot manager — 15 September — no depot building a round sheet by hand.
>
> Three steps, because the champion is warm and nobody has to approve it. Where a buyer must be won over, the same plan carries a costed case and an approval ahead of the first depot and runs to five.
>
> **For the finance director**
>
> Kestrel rebuilds every depot's round sheet by hand before first collection, and the vans that leave part-loaded are deliveries the group pays twice to make. This plans the load from the system the depots already run. Two can be live by June, and June's decision is whether the case holds, not whether to commit all six.
>
> **Unsettled.** Who sets the per-hotel rates — ask the depot manager in April; the rate schedule settles it.

Read it back once as the person who has to say it aloud: every figure from the record, every placed item citing what carries it, every product appearing once, and exactly what was asked for, finished.
