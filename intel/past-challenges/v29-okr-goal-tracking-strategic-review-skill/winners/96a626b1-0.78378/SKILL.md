# The Ledger, Not the Lamp

Leadership reads an OKR review on its way into the room: the first screen closely, nothing after it. So the whole page fits on that screen, its verdict and first decision sit in the opening sentences, and every line below is a call the tracker could not have made.

The tracker hands over a lamp per key result: a colour, a percentage, a date, a line of trend. The owner lit it on the day of the update, with whatever hope the owner had that day. It says nothing about whether the definition moved, whether a nearer date governs, whether an input failed, or whether anybody but the owner checked the figure. The review reads the ledger instead. Every key result committed four terms at the start of the quarter, and every failure a review must catch is one of them breaking while the lamp stays lit.

| Term | How it breaks | Who mends it |
|---|---|---|
| The result | narrowed, swapped, or delivered with conditions | leadership, which alone accepts a new definition |
| The date | an outside party or a downstream key result needs it earlier | the owner if pace allows; leadership if a team or budget must move |
| The inputs | another key result or an outside body has not delivered | whoever holds the input; leadership if one source holds an objective |
| The reading | old, hand-entered, or the owner's word alone | the owner, with a system reading |

## Pace against the clock that governs

Pace is the share of work done over the share of time used: progress ÷ (weeks elapsed ÷ 13). Use the export's own progress figure. At week 4, 30% done is 0.30 ÷ (4/13 = 0.308) = 0.97. At week 13 the time used is 1.0 and the ratio is the progress. Write every ratio with its inputs; one wrong division costs the page every right one.

About 0.9 or more is on-track. From 0.7 to 0.9 is at-risk: the owner can close it. Below 0.7 is off-track. At the close every result under 1.0 is not met, and the band says by how much. Early in a quarter low figures are normal: judge week 4 on pace, dates and dependencies, and say where the colour is more alarmed than the arithmetic.

The end of the quarter is only the default clock; it is not always the one in charge. A regulator's filing day, a queue that closes, a price that lapses, or a downstream key result that needs this one first sets an earlier date, and time runs to it: progress ÷ (weeks elapsed ÷ the week of that date). Work due at week 8 that is 20% done at week 4 reads 0.20 ÷ (4/8 = 0.50) = 0.40, not 0.20 ÷ 0.308 = 0.65. Put the governing ratio first. A comfortable ratio in front of a door about to shut is the most expensive mistake a review can make, so work backward from the date: what has to be true, and by which day.

## Settled, not paced

Filings, approvals, launches, certifications and inspections either happened or did not. A qualified outcome — a conditional rating, a provisional certificate, a pass with findings open — is not the result committed; the verdict is not met, the GREEN is overruled in those words, and the exposure is named. A threshold is met or missed on the last day: 97.1% against 98% is missed, however steeply it climbed; the trend is next quarter's starting point. A milestone that showed 0% right up to the week it landed gets full credit, plus one sentence noting that the updates never tracked the work.

## Judged as written, risk down the chain

Measure each key result against the words it was written in. A narrower delivery is a scope change, not a partial success, and accepting it is leadership's. Whatever was sized to the original inherits the miss. A key result needing another's output carries that one's risk whatever its own ratio. One outside body holding two key results is one blocker; one team wanted by two is one resourcing conflict. A key result planned to start after another finishes is sequencing, not failure: name the gate and its date. A figure untouched for five weeks, hand-entered, a trend word the owner typed rather than the system recorded, or "in final review" with nothing shared is a measurement gap, named beside the pace verdict.

Each owner, date, figure, currency and year is taken from the export. Where the export is silent, say so and name who can fill the gap.

## What only leadership can decide

The test for each candidate: is there a choice only leadership can make that changes the outcome? An outside party holding a single key result fails the test however low it reads and however long it runs — leadership has no lever on another body's queue, and offering to narrow, re-scope or re-plan around it is inventing an ask. Look for each of these in the export; each one present is a decision:

- a changed definition to accept or refuse;
- one team or budget wanted by two commitments, where the question is which yields;
- money, an order or a price above an owner's authority, above all one that lapses on a date;
- an answer to a regulator or counterparty that commits the company: what is disclosed, to whom, with what outside help;
- at the close, a commitment that finished below 0.7 on the company's own work — no outside body, no planned sequence behind it — or whose blocking input arrived only at the close: carry, narrow, resource or drop, and on what date;
- one outside body or single source holding two or more key results, or the whole of an objective, past the quarter or a fixed date: wait, add an alternative, or re-scope;
- a slipped date that breaks something already told to clients, prospects or a regulator: what they are told now, and by whom;
- a target the quarter proved was sized wrong, reset before the next plan.

Not decisions: refreshing a reading, an at-risk gap the owner can close, an outside queue holding one key result with nothing waiting behind it — even at the close, even if it runs into next quarter, the owner simply carries it — key results low because the plan starts them later, and a key result that inherits a miss, which is settled by the decision on its source and never gets one of its own. A key result partly delivered because an outside body still holds the rest — one of two sites open, the second awaiting its permit — is not a changed definition to accept or refuse: nothing was narrowed, the owner carries the remainder, and it gets no decision. These go to the narratives with a name and a date. Every option a decision offers comes from the export; an option the export does not name is invented. When none of the list is present, the section says so in one sentence with the reason in the export's terms; a padded list teaches the room to skim it.

Say whether each decision is urgent (this week) or a planning reset (before next quarter's plan).

## The page

Hard limit: the entire page stays below 2,400 characters, roughly 330 words, counting labels and keys. That is where the reader's screen stops, and a page broken off mid-line looks unfinished. Spend words on judgement, none on what the tracker already says: one sentence per at-risk key result, one or two per narrative, one line of updates. Order makes any cut safe: summary, every decision, at-risk entries, narratives, updates.

The request sets the form, followed to the character:

- Plain text: each label in capitals on its own line, a blank line, then sentences. No #, no asterisks, no bullets, no numbered lines, no tables.
- Markdown: ## headings, decisions as - bullets, the at-risk entries as a table, updates as bullets. No code fences.
- Capitals with a colon: each label in capitals followed by a colon, then a blank line, then paragraphs. No markdown, bullets, numbered lines or tables.
- JSON: the reply is the object itself, its first character the opening brace and its last the closing one, with no fence, language tag or wrapper of any kind, because the parser reads the first character. Exactly the keys the request names, and inside them exactly the sub-keys it names. Write it compact, objects on one line, every string one sentence, at most five rows in the table, so the object closes inside the limit.

Five sections, in order. Executive summary: posture and the single most important decision in the first two sentences, with figure and date, then a clause each for the other misses, overruled colours and gaps — five sentences at most. Leadership decisions: each what is decided, why only leadership, who brings options, by when; or the sentence that none are required, and why. Blocked and at-risk key results: only those not on track, each with owner, ratio and inputs or the settled result, status with any colour overruled, blocker type (internal dependency, external dependency, resourcing conflict, scope change, measurement gap, sequencing, execution), and what unblocks it. Objective narratives: a verdict per objective, then owner actions with names and dates. Informational updates: wins and on-track key results with figures.

The reply is the page alone, starting at its first label, heading or brace. The worked pages below are set off by > marks for display only, except the JSON page, shown bare exactly as it is returned; each is the length a real page should be.

## Clocks and evidence by sector

The deciding date is rarely printed as one. It hides in how each sector works, so read the export for these patterns before trusting the quarter's clock.

Medical devices and diagnostics: a regulatory submission is a milestone that settles on the day it is lodged, and suppliers, distributors and hospitals often refuse to commit until it is; anything they gate inherits the date. Ethics and review boards sit on a published cycle, so a missed sitting moves the result by a whole cycle, not by days. Validation runs, design-freeze reviews and notified-body audits are binary.

Energy and infrastructure: grid connection queues, planning committees and capacity auctions close on fixed days, and an application lodged one day late waits for the next window, often a year. Equipment prices are quoted with an expiry; the quote date is a clock in its own right. Lenders' credit committees meet on a calendar, and financial close usually needs a permit or connection reference first.

Consumer goods and retail: shelf space is won at range reviews held a few times a year; a listing missed at the review is lost until the next one. Promotions and price changes are booked weeks ahead with the retailer. Sell-through is the honest figure; shipments into a warehouse are not sales. A launch "live" for a waitlist only is not live.

What counts as a witness: a system extract, a signed contract, a regulator's letter, an audit report, a shipped order, a bank confirmation. What does not: an owner's percentage with no source, a trend word typed into a comment, "in final review" with nothing circulated, a count kept by hand, a figure older than the export by five weeks or more. When the only witness is the owner, the review says so beside the verdict and names the system that would settle it.

## Misreadings that sink a review

These are the errors a careful reader catches first, each paired with the reading that replaces it.

A green dot on a threshold that closed below its line is still a miss; "nearly there" is not a status. A deliverable that arrived narrower than promised is not "substantially complete"; the gap is named and leadership owns it. An item that shows steady progress but has not been touched in six weeks is not steady; nobody knows where it is. A percentage typed by the owner with no system behind it is an opinion, however reasonable it sounds. A late-quarter jump from nothing to done is real delivery and a broken reporting habit at once, and both get said.

On timing: a ratio above one means nothing if the work must be finished before an outside cut-off it cannot meet. A ratio below one means little in week three if the plan back-loads the work. An item scheduled to begin after another finishes is waiting, not failing. A supplier, regulator, committee or lender that holds two items is one problem with one conversation, not two problems.

On asks: leadership time is the scarcest thing on the page. A request that an owner could act on alone wastes it and teaches the room to skim. A request with options the export never mentions is fiction. A request that repeats another in different words is padding. An empty decisions section with a clear reason is a finding, not an omission.

On numbers: every figure traces to a line of the export, every division shows its parts, and every date sits in the export's own year. A rounded figure is rounded the same way everywhere it appears. Where two figures in the export disagree, both are reported and the disagreement is a measurement gap, not a choice for the reviewer to make silently.

On form: in JSON, the request's key list is the schema. Every sub-key it names appears in every object, even where the worked example used other keys, and nothing it does not name is added. A milestone that jumps from 0% to 100% in the last weeks is credited and, in the executive summary, named as reporting theatre: delivered, but invisible to leadership until it landed.
