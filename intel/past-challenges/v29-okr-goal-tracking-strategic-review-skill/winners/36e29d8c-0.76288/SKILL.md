# The Promise, Not the Paint

A leadership team reads an OKR review in the minutes before its meeting: the opening properly, the rest only where something catches. It needs to know which of the quarter's promises will not be kept, which of those only it can act on, and enough about the rest to leave it with the owners. So the page owes its reader the posture and the one decision that matters most in its opening lines, with the evidence underneath.

An export is organised around the tracker: a colour, a percentage, the date of the last update, a line of trend the owner typed. What decides whether a key result is healthy is something the export only implies — the promise it made. Every key result promised a defined thing, by a date, standing on the output of other work, and measured by somebody. The colour is the owner's own forecast of that promise on the day of the last update, so it carries the owner's hopes and the update's age and nothing about the other parts. The whole of the work is getting from the paint to the promise.

## Why the promise, and not a list of warning signs

The obvious alternative is a list of warning signs weighed together into an impression. It fails because the signs are symptoms of different failures needing different remedies, and weighing them hides which one is present. A low percentage on a key result meant to start late says the plan is working; the same percentage on one that should be finishing says it is not.

Every way a key result misleads a review is one part of its promise failing while the paint stays put. The definition has moved, so the thing delivered is not the thing promised. The date that governs is not the quarter's end, because an outside party set an earlier one or another key result is waiting on this one. The input it stands on has failed, so its own figure measures only what it could do alone. Or the reading is old or unverifiable, so nobody knows how the promise stands. Each part points at a different person to fix it, so naming the part that failed is half of the unblocking. And the same frame sorts the decisions: keeping a promise is the owner's work, and remaking one is the only thing the leadership team is needed for.

## Pace is progress over the promise's own share of time

A pace ratio is the share of the work done over the share of the time used: progress ÷ (weeks elapsed ÷ 13). At week 5 the quarter is 5/13 = 0.385 used, so 35% done is 0.35 ÷ 0.385 = 0.91. At the close the time used is 1.0 and the ratio is the progress. The ratio is written with its inputs, because one wrong figure costs the credibility of every correct one.

About 0.9 and above is on-track: the work is keeping up, and a few hundredths under 1.0 is the imprecision of an estimated percentage rather than a problem. From 0.7 to 0.9 is at-risk: behind by a gap an owner can close in the time left. Below 0.7 is off-track: more than ordinary effort recovers inside the quarter. The colour is reported beside the verdict and overridden in words where they disagree, because a YELLOW on work a third done at the close is a miss, and the one person who set it had a reason to hope.

The quarter's end is the default date and often the wrong one. Where an outside party sets a date — a regulator's filing day, an approval window, an offer that expires — or where the key result must finish early so another can begin, the time that counts runs to that date. Work due at week 6 that is 30% done at week 3 has used half its time: 0.30 ÷ (3/6) = 0.60, off-track, where the same figure against the quarter reads 0.30 ÷ (3/13 = 0.231) = 1.30. A good ratio in front of a door about to close is the most expensive misreading available, because it reads as the item nobody needs to watch, and a missed outside date is a lost window rather than a late delivery. So the page reasons backward from the date and says what has to be true by when.

## Some promises are kept or broken, not paced

A filing, an approval, a launch, a certification or an inspection is done or not done, whatever percentage the owner put on the preparation. A qualified result — a conditional approval, a temporary certificate, a pass with findings still open — is a different outcome from the one named, because whoever relies on the milestone relies on its unconditional form. The verdict is not met, the tracker's green is overridden in so many words, and what the qualification exposes the company to is said in plain words.

A threshold is met or missed on the last day. An improving trend is a fact about next quarter and the figure at the close is the fact about this one, so a result just short of the line is a miss with a good trajectory, and both halves are said.

A milestone reading 0% for most of the quarter and 100% at the end is credited in full, because the thing happened and scoring it off-track would punish a delivered result. The page adds one sentence about the updates: they were not tracking the work, so the tracker gave leadership no warning either way, and how it is measured next quarter is an owner action.

## A key result is judged as it was written

When the thing delivered is narrower than, or different from, what the key result named at the start of the quarter, it is judged against its original words. The narrower result may be good work and is still not the promise. Calling it a partial success lets a definition change pass without anybody accepting it, and accepting a changed scope is leadership's decision — so the page calls it a scope change, sets what was promised beside what was delivered, and puts the acceptance to leadership. Whatever downstream was sized to the original inherits the miss, because it was planned around something that did not arrive.

## Status travels along the dependency

A key result that needs another's output carries that one's risk whatever its own pace, because its own figure measures only what it could do alone. The page states the chain once, in the export's terms.

Common causes are counted once. One outside party holding two key results is one blocker — one conversation, one date to learn. One team needed by two key results is one resourcing conflict — splitting its time describes the team's calendar, and the real question is which promise yields. Counting either twice hides the one lever that moves both.

A key result scheduled to start after another finishes reads off-track by pace while it waits. That is the sequence working as planned, so the verdict names the sequence and the date that releases it. An outside body's approval is watched and dated the same way, since escalation cannot move it.

## A reading has a date and an author

A figure was true on the day it was typed, so several weeks without an update mean the colour and percentage describe an earlier part of the quarter. A figure the owner calculated with no system behind it, or a trend word typed by hand, is a claim about the promise rather than a reading of it. Both are measurement gaps, named beside the pace verdict when both apply: a key result can be off-track on what it last reported and unmeasured since, and the two go to different fixes — the owner refreshes the reading, the plan addresses the pace.

The shape of a series is evidence. Progress that slows is worth a sentence even where the verdict is set, because it says whether next quarter starts with momentum. A new line's revenue climbing month on month from a late launch is a ramp, and a target that assumed a full quarter of mature selling was sized wrong rather than missed, which calls for resetting the target rather than escalating.

Every owner, date, figure and trend on the page comes from the export; where one is not recorded, the page says so and who can supply it. The page becomes commitments once read, so an invented date is a deadline somebody misses and an invented ratio is a verdict nobody can check.

## A leadership decision remakes a promise

A decision belongs to leadership when only the leadership team can make it: moving a shared team from one promise to another, accepting or refusing a changed definition, answering a regulator or counterparty when the answer commits the company, negotiating an extension with an outside party, judging whether an objective is still feasible, resetting a target the quarter proved was sized wrong. Work an owner can do — refreshing a reading, chasing a supplier, recovering an at-risk pace — goes into the objective narrative with the owner's name and a date, because handing it upward takes it from the person who has to do it.

So the list is as long as the export warrants: often two or three, sometimes one, sometimes none. An urgent response — a regulatory finding, a window closing this month — is a different kind from a planning reset, and the page says which, because the first needs an answer this week and the second before next quarter's plan is set.

The cadence sets the register. At the close the results are final: misses are stated as misses and decisions are about what is carried forward and on what terms. At a mid-quarter check-in the at-risk items are still recoverable, so the page says what has to happen by when for each promise to be kept.

## When nothing needs deciding

A quarter can hold low percentages and need nothing from leadership, when the off-track figures wait on a sequence, the at-risk ones are within their owners' reach and the blocked one sits with an outside body. Then the leadership section holds one plain sentence saying leadership has nothing to decide, and the reason in the export's terms. An empty list with its reasons tells the reader the review looked and found nothing to remake; a padded one teaches everyone to skim the section that matters.

Where the export cannot support a verdict on a key result at all — no figure, an old one, only the owner's word — the verdict is the gap itself, with what is known, what would settle it, and who.

## The page

Five sections, in this order, and the request decides their form. Its first lines say how the answer is returned — plain text with labelled headings, markdown with ## headers and tables, one JSON object with named keys, labels in capitals with a colon after each — and that instruction is followed exactly, because a review is pasted into a board pack or a parser, and a page in the wrong shape is rejected unread. Where the request names keys, the page is that object with exactly those keys, from its opening brace to its closing one. Where it rules a form out, a table becomes one line or paragraph per key result and bullets become sentences.

**Executive summary** — the posture and the one issue or decision that matters most, in the first two sentences, with its figure and date; then the chain it sets off and the other misses, a line each. **Leadership decisions** — each item what is to be decided, why only leadership can, who brings the options and by when; or the sentence that none are required, and why. **At-risk and blocked** — only the key results not on track, each with owner, pace ratio with its inputs (or the binary or threshold result against the promise), status with any colour it overrides, blocker type — internal dependency, external dependency, resourcing conflict, scope change, measurement gap, sequencing, execution — and what would unblock it. **Objective narratives** — a verdict per objective with its reasons, then owner actions with named owner and date. **Informational updates** — wins and on-track key results, briefly, with their figures.

Carry the method's words — governing date, inherited risk, one blocker, measurement gap, scope change, judged as written — applied to this export's facts rather than defined. The page returns alone, beginning at its first label, heading or brace.

The four pages below are the calibration: four formats because four requests asked for four, different lengths because the exports held different amounts, and two, no, two and one leadership decisions as each export warranted.

## Worked — a tender cut down before the quarter ends

*Pembury School Kitchens, contract catering for primary schools — end-of-quarter review, Q1, week 13 of 13, export 2025-03-28. The request asked for plain text with labelled section headings.*

>
> Leadership has a scope change to accept or refuse before Q2 can be planned. KR 1.2 promised a full tender for the county's 60 primary schools, was cut in week 8 to a pilot offer covering 15, and the tracker marks the smaller submission 100% GREEN. Judged as written it is not met, and the cook recruitment in KR 2.2, sized to a 60-school contract, inherits the miss. Central-kitchen output stopped climbing at 7,500 meals a day after week 9, against 9,000 promised, on a constraint no owner can buy alone. The produce agreements in KR 1.3 have gone six weeks without an update, and one water company's trade-effluent consent holds KR 3.2 and KR 3.3 together. KR 1.1's food-hygiene accreditation is credited; its updates read 0% through week 11, an update-cadence concern.
>
> LEADERSHIP DECISIONS
>
> 1. Accept or refuse the tender scope change. Only leadership can accept a changed definition; the answer decides whether Q2 plans for a 15-school pilot or reopens the full county bid, and KR 2.2 recruitment follows whichever is chosen. Reopening the full bid keeps the bid team on it through April. Harriet Penrose brings both options; decision by 2025-04-04.
>
> 2. Fund a second blast-chill line or reset the output target. > AT-RISK AND BLOCKED KEY RESULTS

>
> KR 1.3, five produce-supply agreements. Owner Aubrey Ottley. 2 of 5 = 40%; pace 0.40 ÷ 1.0 = 0.40. OFF-TRACK and a measurement gap: last update 2025-02-14, six weeks before the export. ## Final check

A correct page begins at its first label, heading or brace, in the form the request named, with the issue that matters most in its opening sentences. Every leadership decision remakes a promise only leadership can remake, or the section says there is none and why. The table holds only key results not on track, each ratio with its inputs, and every contradicted colour is overridden in words. Common causes count once, gaps sit beside verdicts, every figure came from the export, and every ratio divides correctly.
